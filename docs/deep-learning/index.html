Deep Learning

Deep learning is the discovery that if you stack differentiable functions, feed them enough data and compute, and descend the gradient, the representations you would otherwise have hand-designed appear on their own. This syllabus covers the mechanism one layer below the framework API: how gradients actually flow, why the optimisers and normalisers that work were found empirically before anyone could explain them, what a transformer computes step by step, how a training run is distributed across thousands of accelerators, and what the theory can and cannot yet account for.

What this topic is

Deep learning is representation learning by gradient descent. Its founding bet is that a stack of simple differentiable transformations, composed deeply enough and fitted to enough data, will discover intermediate representations that no human would have thought to specify — edges into textures into parts into objects, characters into words into syntax into semantics. That bet was made repeatedly from the 1960s onward and lost each time, until the combination of large labelled datasets, GPU compute and a handful of engineering fixes (better initialisation, ReLU, normalisation, residual connections) made deep networks trainable in practice. Nothing in the mathematics changed in 2012; what changed was that the optimisation started working.

The field's defining intellectual awkwardness is that practice runs decades ahead of theory. Models with more parameters than training examples interpolate their data exactly and still generalise; the optimisers that work were found by trial; normalisation layers are universal and their mechanism is still contested. Classical statistical learning theory, which explains classical machine learning adequately, does not explain any of this — the bounds it produces are numerically vacuous. This syllabus therefore teaches the mechanism carefully, is explicit about which explanations are established and which are folklore, and treats the theory modules as a report on an open problem rather than as a foundation.

M01

The representation problem, and three failed attempts to solve it

≈4 h

Deep learning is usually introduced as a set of architectures, which makes the design choices look arbitrary. It is better understood as the eventual answer to one question that classical machine learning could not handle: where do the features come from? This module installs that question, and the history of failing to answer it, so that every later mechanism reads as a solution to a specific obstacle rather than as a convention.

Concepts

  • Why the classical pipeline hit a wall — a linear model on raw pixels or raw audio samples is hopeless, so for forty years the real work was hand-designed features
    • SIFT, HOG and MFCCs as the state of the art in 2010, each the product of years of expert design, each superseded by a learned equivalent within two years of enough compute existing
    • The kernel answer from the classical page: fix a similarity function rather than learn one. It works, and it does not scale past a kernel matrix you can hold in memory
    • The deep answer: parameterise the feature extractor and fit it with the same gradient that fits the classifier
  • Three winters, three specific obstacles
    • The perceptron and Minsky and Papert's XOR result — a single layer cannot represent it, which was true and was widely over-read as a claim about layered networks
    • Backpropagation's popularisation in 1986 and what still did not work: vanishing gradients through saturating nonlinearities, no principled initialisation, and datasets three orders of magnitude too small
    • The 1990s: SVMs and boosted trees beat neural networks on the benchmarks of the day, with better theory and fewer knobs. This was a correct decision on the evidence available
  • What actually changed around 2012, in order of importance
    • Data — ImageNet's 1.2 million labelled images, which is a curation achievement rather than an algorithmic one
    • Compute — GPUs giving two orders of magnitude more throughput per dollar on exactly the operation these models need
    • A handful of engineering fixes: ReLU instead of sigmoid, better initialisation, dropout, and enough augmentation to use the capacity
    • AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet result as the moment the field's default flipped, and the fact that essentially none of its components were new
  • The shape of the argument this page will keep making — that mechanism and explanation have separated, and that most of what works was found before it was understood

Work through

  • Deep Learning (Nature review) — the LeCun, Bengio and Hinton Nature review, which is the field's own account of what happened and why.
  • Deep Learning — Chapter 1, for the historical framing and the representation-learning argument.
  • ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks — AlexNet. Read section 3 and notice how much of it is engineering rather than novelty.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 1, which is the clearest modern statement of what the field is trying to do.
  • ImageNet — the dataset itself; skim its construction paper and its later audits, because the curation is the achievement.
  • Practical Deep Learning for Coders — lesson 1, if you want something working before you understand it — the reverse of this page's order, and a useful counterweight.

Practice

Classify CIFAR-10 three ways: logistic regression on raw pixels, a gradient-boosted tree on raw pixels, and the same two models on HOG features you compute yourself. Then train a small convolutional network end to end. Record all four accuracies. You are done when you can state how much of the gap between the first and the last is attributable to features rather than to model class — because that number is the case for this entire page.

Where people go wrong

Believing the field was held back by a lack of ideas. Backpropagation, convolutions, LSTMs and even attention-like mechanisms all predate the deep learning era by a decade or more; what was missing was data, compute and a small set of tricks that made optimisation converge. Reading the history as an intellectual breakthrough rather than as an engineering and resource threshold leads to the expectation that the next advance will be conceptual — when the record suggests it is at least as likely to be scale plus a fix that looks trivial afterwards.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain to someone who knows classical machine learning what deep learning is for, without naming a single architecture — and then name the specific obstacle that each of the three historical failures ran into.

M02

The multilayer perceptron, and what universal approximation does not say

≈5 h

The MLP is the object every other architecture is a constrained version of, and it is where the vocabulary is set. It is also the site of the field's most over-cited theorem: universal approximation is quoted as though it explains why deep learning works, when it says nothing about depth, learnability or generalisation. Getting that straight now prevents a category error that recurs in every later module.

Concepts

  • The architecture — affine transformation, elementwise nonlinearity, repeat; and the observation that without the nonlinearity a stack of affine maps is a single affine map
    • Width, depth, and the parameter count as a function of both
    • Activations and their properties: sigmoid and tanh (saturating, gradient-killing), ReLU (piecewise linear, dead units), leaky ReLU, GELU and SiLU, and the fact that the modern preference is empirical rather than derived
    • The output layer as a choice about the distribution being modelled: linear for Gaussian, sigmoid for Bernoulli, softmax for categorical — with the loss following from the likelihood rather than being a separate decision
  • Universal approximation, stated precisely and then bounded
    • Cybenko (1989) and Hornik: a single hidden layer with a non-polynomial activation can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary accuracy
    • What it does not say: nothing about how many units (the bound can be exponential in the input dimension), nothing about whether gradient descent will find those weights, nothing about generalisation from finite samples, and nothing that distinguishes deep from shallow
    • The depth separation results that do say something: functions representable by a deep network with polynomially many units require exponentially many in a shallow one. This is the actual theoretical case for depth
  • What networks represent geometrically
    • A ReLU network is a piecewise-linear function; the number of linear regions grows exponentially in depth and polynomially in width, which is the geometric form of the depth argument
    • Composition as feature reuse: each layer's units are built from the previous layer's, so depth buys sharing rather than raw capacity
  • The vocabulary that persists everywhere: parameters versus hyperparameters, forward pass, logits, batch, epoch, step — set carefully here because every later module uses it without comment

Work through

  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapters 3 and 4, which build the MLP and the piecewise-linear picture with the best figures in any textbook.
  • Approximation by Superpositions of a Sigmoidal Function — Cybenko's theorem in the original, so you can see exactly what is and is not claimed.
  • Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts — Chapter 6, for the modern textbook treatment of network functions.
  • Dive into Deep Learning — Chapter 5, which pairs each idea with runnable code in three frameworks.
  • Neural Networks — 3Blue1Brown's first two videos, which build the MLP geometrically before any algebra.

Practice

Fit a one-dimensional target function — something with several bends, like a damped sine — with ReLU networks of matched parameter count but different shapes: one hidden layer of 256 units, two of 32, four of 16. Plot each fit and, for the shallow one, plot the individual hinge locations. Then count linear regions numerically for each. You are done when the region counts match the depth-versus-width prediction and when you can see, in the plots, what the deep network is reusing that the shallow one is not.

Where people go wrong

Citing universal approximation as an explanation of deep learning's success. It is an existence result about a single hidden layer of unbounded width, proved for a function class, with no reference to data, optimisation or depth. Taken at face value it argues against depth. The properties that actually matter — that gradient descent finds good solutions and that those solutions generalise — are exactly what the theorem is silent about, and they remain open questions in M17.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the universal approximation theorem correctly, list three things it does not establish, and give the depth-separation argument that does provide a theoretical case for depth.

M03

Backpropagation as reverse-mode automatic differentiation

≈6 h

This is the load-bearing module of the page. Backpropagation is usually taught as a formula to memorise for a specific network, which leaves people unable to reason about any architecture the formula was not derived for. Taught as reverse-mode autodiff it is a general algorithm over computation graphs, and the cost asymmetry it exploits is the single fact that makes deep learning computationally possible at all.

Concepts

  • Computation graphs and the two modes of differentiation
    • Forward mode propagates derivatives with respect to one input through the graph: one pass gives you a full column of the Jacobian, so covering n inputs costs n passes
    • Reverse mode propagates derivatives of one output backward: one pass gives a full row of the Jacobian, so a scalar loss with a hundred billion parameters costs one backward pass
    • That asymmetry is the whole argument. Deep learning has many inputs and one output, which is precisely the regime where reverse mode wins by a factor of the parameter count
    • The cost: reverse mode must store the forward activations to use on the way back, so it trades memory for time — which is why activation memory dominates the budget in M11
  • Deriving it, by hand, for a two-layer MLP
    • The chain rule as local Jacobian-vector products; each node needs only its own local derivative and the gradient flowing in from above
    • Writing the gradients for softmax with cross-entropy and noticing that the combination simplifies to (predicted − target), which is why frameworks fuse them
    • Numerical gradient checking with central differences, and the tolerance you should expect — this is the diagnostic that catches every hand-derived gradient bug
  • Where gradients go wrong, mechanistically
    • Vanishing gradients: the backward pass multiplies Jacobians, so a chain of sigmoids with derivative at most 0.25 attenuates by 4⁻ᵈ. This is arithmetic, not a mystery
    • Exploding gradients and gradient clipping as the blunt fix
    • Why ReLU changes the picture — derivative exactly 1 on the active side — and why it does not fix it: dead units, and the fact that the product of many weight matrices still shrinks or grows depending on their spectra. M05's residuals are the real fix
  • How frameworks actually implement it — tape-based versus graph-based autodiff, the difference between PyTorch's eager tape and JAX's traced transformations, and what detach, no_grad and stop-gradient do to the graph
    • Checkpointing (recomputing activations instead of storing them) as the standard memory-for-compute trade, previewed here and used in M11

Work through

  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — the micrograd video, in which Karpathy builds a scalar autodiff engine in about a hundred lines. Do it alongside him rather than watching.
  • Deep Learning — Chapter 6 sections 6.5, which is the careful general treatment of backpropagation over computation graphs.
  • Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors — the 1986 Nature paper. Two pages, and worth reading for what they thought the contribution was.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 7, which is unusually clear on the memory cost of the backward pass.
  • PyTorch documentation — the autograd mechanics page, for what the framework is actually doing to your graph.
  • micrograd — read it only after writing your own; the differences are the ones worth noticing.
  • CS231n assignments — assignment 1's layer-and-gradient implementations, with numerical gradient checks throughout.
  • JAX documentation — the autodiff cookbook, which explains forward and reverse mode as composable transformations.

Practice

Build a scalar-valued autodiff engine from scratch — a Value class with add, multiply, power, tanh and a topological-sort backward pass — then use it to train a small MLP on a two-dimensional classification problem. Verify every operation's gradient against a central-difference approximation. Then implement forward mode as well and time both on a network with 10, 100 and 1,000 parameters. You are done when the timing curves diverge exactly as the cost analysis predicts.

Where people go wrong

Treating backpropagation as a formula for MLPs rather than an algorithm over graphs. The symptom appears the first time someone needs a gradient through something unusual — a custom loss, a straight-through estimator, a layer with a non-obvious backward — and cannot reason about it. The general form is small: every node needs its local Jacobian-vector product, and the algorithm is a topological sort. Learn it that way and no architecture is ever mysterious.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why reverse mode costs one backward pass for a scalar loss regardless of parameter count while forward mode costs one pass per parameter, and say what reverse mode pays for that advantage in memory.

M04

Optimisers, schedules and the loss landscape

≈6 h

M03 produced a gradient; this module is about what to do with it, and it is where the gap between theory and practice is widest. The classical page's optimisation module assumed convexity and got guarantees; nothing here has guarantees, the methods that work were found empirically, and the explanations for why they work are contested. Knowing which parts are established and which are folklore is what lets you tune a run rather than superstitiously copy a config.

Concepts

  • Stochastic gradient descent and its knobs
    • Mini-batching: gradient noise scaling as 1/√B, and the linear scaling rule relating batch size to learning rate, with its empirically observed breakdown at very large batches
    • Momentum as an exponential moving average of gradients; Nesterov's variant; the physical analogy and its limits
    • Why the noise is not purely a nuisance — SGD's noise is widely believed to bias the solution toward flatter regions, which is one of the candidate explanations in M17
  • Adaptive methods
    • AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam: per-parameter step sizes from running estimates of the gradient's first and second moments, with bias correction
    • AdamW and the decoupling of weight decay from the adaptive scaling — L2 regularisation and weight decay are not equivalent under Adam, which is a real bug in the original formulation and the reason AdamW is now the default
    • Where Adam wins (transformers, sparse gradients, poorly conditioned problems) and where well-tuned SGD with momentum still matches or beats it (convolutional vision models), and the generalisation-gap literature around that comparison
    • Newer optimisers as a live area: second-order-flavoured methods such as Shampoo and its practical descendants, and the muon-style updates that gained traction on large runs — worth tracking, not yet defaults
  • Learning-rate schedules, which matter as much as the optimiser
    • Warmup and why transformers need it — early in training the second-moment estimates are unreliable and the updates are enormous
    • Cosine decay, linear decay to zero, step schedules, and the fact that the final learning rate matters for the final loss more than most tuning acknowledges
    • One-cycle and cyclical schedules; learning-rate range tests as a cheap way to find the maximum stable rate
  • What the landscape actually looks like, as far as anyone knows
    • Saddle points rather than local minima dominate in high dimensions, which reverses the intuition trained on two-dimensional pictures
    • Mode connectivity: independently trained solutions are connected by low-loss paths, so the minima are not isolated basins
    • Sharp versus flat minima and the batch-size debate, including the objection that sharpness is not reparameterisation-invariant
    • Edge of stability: training frequently runs at a curvature where classical analysis predicts divergence, and does not diverge — an empirical finding that no standard theory covers

Work through

  • Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization — read it for the moment estimates and the bias correction, both of which are simpler than their reputation.
  • Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization — AdamW, and the clearest demonstration that a widely used implementation detail was wrong for years.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 6, on optimisation, and Chapter 20's discussion of why the landscape is navigable.
  • Deep Learning Tuning Playbook — the Google Research guide, which is the most useful practical document on schedules and search strategy in existence.
  • Deep Learning — Chapter 8, for the optimisation-difficulty analysis including ill-conditioning and saddle points.
  • Deep Learning Course — Fleuret's optimisation lectures, which cover the mechanics most courses assume.

Practice

Train the same small network four ways on the same data: SGD, SGD with momentum, Adam, and AdamW — each with a learning-rate range test to pick the maximum stable rate, then with cosine decay. Plot training and validation loss against both steps and wall-clock time. Then repeat the AdamW run with weight decay implemented as an L2 penalty in the loss instead, and compare. You are done when the L2-versus-decoupled difference is visible in your own curves, because that is the AdamW paper's entire claim.

Where people go wrong

Copying a learning rate from a paper that used a different batch size, model size, schedule or optimiser. The learning rate interacts with all four, and it is the hyperparameter with by far the largest effect on the outcome — a run that diverges and a run that plateaus can be a factor of three apart. Run a range test on your own setup; it costs a few minutes and replaces the most common source of wasted GPU time in the field.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why L2 regularisation and weight decay differ under Adam but coincide under plain SGD, and separately explain why transformer training needs warmup in terms of what Adam's second-moment estimate is doing in the first few hundred steps.

M05

Initialisation, normalisation and residual connections

≈6 h

These three are why deep networks train at all, and each is a case study in the field's characteristic pattern: an empirical fix that worked immediately, followed by a decade of argument about the mechanism. They are grouped in one module because they solve the same problem — keeping signal and gradient magnitudes in a usable range through a deep stack — by three different means, and understanding that shared purpose is what lets you reason about a new architecture's stability.

Concepts

  • Initialisation as variance bookkeeping
    • The requirement: activation variance should neither shrink nor grow across layers, or a deep stack saturates or explodes before the first update
    • Xavier/Glorot for symmetric activations, He/Kaiming for ReLU with its factor-of-two correction for the halved output variance — derive both, they are three lines each
    • Why zero initialisation fails by symmetry, and why very small initialisation is not a safe default
    • Residual-aware schemes that scale the last layer of each block toward zero, so the network starts near the identity
  • Normalisation layers, and the contested explanation
    • BatchNorm: normalise across the batch dimension, then rescale with learned parameters. The original "internal covariate shift" explanation has been substantially undermined — the better-supported account is that it smooths the loss landscape and permits larger learning rates
    • BatchNorm's real costs: a train/eval discrepancy through running statistics, dependence on batch size, and awkwardness with sequences and with distributed training
    • LayerNorm: normalise across features within each example, which removes the batch dependence and is why transformers use it
    • RMSNorm as LayerNorm without the mean subtraction — cheaper, and empirically no worse, which is why modern large models use it
    • GroupNorm and InstanceNorm for the vision cases where batch statistics are unusable
  • Residual connections, which are the single most important architectural idea after the transformer
    • y = x + F(x): the gradient of the identity path is exactly 1, so there is always an unattenuated route from the loss to every earlier layer. This is the mechanism, and it is the direct answer to M03's vanishing-gradient arithmetic
    • The degradation problem ResNet was written to solve — deeper plain networks had higher training error, which is an optimisation failure and not overfitting
    • The ensemble reading: a residual network behaves like an ensemble of exponentially many shallow paths
    • Pre-norm versus post-norm placement, and why every large transformer moved to pre-norm — post-norm needs careful warmup to be stable at depth, pre-norm mostly does not
  • The three interacting, which is how they are actually used — and the general skill of reasoning about whether a proposed block preserves signal scale

Work through

  • Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition — ResNet. Read the degradation-problem framing in section 3; it is what motivates the whole design.
  • Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift — the original, read knowing its explanation is disputed.
  • Layer Normalization — short, and the variant every transformer uses.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapters 7 and 11, which cover initialisation and residual networks with the variance arithmetic worked through.
  • Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts — Chapter 7, for normalisation treated as one family rather than as separate tricks.

Practice

Build a 50-layer plain MLP and instrument it: log the mean and variance of activations and of gradients at every layer at initialisation. Try three initialisations (small constant, Xavier, He) and observe which keeps both flat. Then train it and watch it fail. Add residual connections and train again; add normalisation and train again. You are done when you have four training curves and per-layer gradient-norm plots that explain each outcome.

Where people go wrong

Repeating that BatchNorm works by reducing internal covariate shift. That was the original hypothesis and later work injected deliberate covariate shift after the normalisation layer without hurting performance, which is strong evidence against it. The better-supported account is landscape smoothing. This matters practically: if you believe the covariate-shift story you will reach for BatchNorm in settings where its actual mechanism does not apply and its batch-dependence bites — small batches, recurrent models, distributed training with tiny per-device batches.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what a residual connection does to the backward pass in terms of the Jacobian of the identity path, and say why pre-norm transformers are more stable at depth than post-norm ones.

M06

Regularisation, and the generalisation puzzle

≈6 h

This module is placed before the architectures deliberately. The central empirical fact about deep learning — that hugely overparameterised models trained to zero training error generalise well, and that the same models can also fit pure noise perfectly — contradicts the classical picture the reader arrives with. Meeting every subsequent architecture already knowing that its performance is not explained by capacity control changes how you read the rest of the page.

Concepts

  • The regularisers that are actually used, and what each does
    • Weight decay: a Gaussian prior on parameters in the classical reading, and in practice as much an optimisation effect as a capacity constraint — its interaction with normalisation layers means the effective mechanism is often a change to the effective learning rate
    • Dropout: randomly zero units at training time, rescale at test; readable as an implicit ensemble over exponentially many subnetworks. Now largely displaced in large models by data scale and by other stabilisers
    • Data augmentation as the most effective regulariser in vision, and the only one that injects real prior knowledge about invariances; mixup and cutmix as the learned-interpolation variants; label smoothing and its effect on calibration
    • Early stopping as implicit regularisation, and its equivalence to an L2 penalty in the linear case
    • Stochastic depth, and ensembling as the reliable but expensive option
  • The result that broke the classical account
    • Zhang et al.: standard architectures trained with standard optimisers fit ImageNet and CIFAR with randomly permuted labels to zero training error, and do it in about the same number of epochs
    • What that rules out: any explanation of generalisation resting on the hypothesis class being too small to fit noise, which is what VC and Rademacher arguments provide
    • The corollary that explicit regularisation is neither necessary nor sufficient — removing all of it degrades test error modestly, not catastrophically
  • Double descent and benign overfitting, in the deep setting
    • Test error peaking at the interpolation threshold and falling again past it; model-wise, epoch-wise and sample-wise variants
    • The current best account: implicit bias of the optimiser toward low-complexity interpolating solutions, where the right complexity measure is not parameter count
  • Grokking, as the sharpest available anomaly
    • On small algorithmic tasks, networks reach perfect training accuracy and near-chance validation accuracy, and then — thousands of steps later, with no change in the training loss — generalise abruptly
    • Why it matters: it decouples fitting from generalising in time, which no account based on capacity alone can produce, and it has become a standard testbed for interpretability work in M18

Work through

  • Understanding Deep Learning Requires Rethinking Generalization — the random-label experiments. Short, decisive, and the paper this module is built around.
  • Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting — the JMLR paper, for the ensemble reading in the authors' terms.
  • Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets — read it for the phenomenon; the explanations came later and are in M18.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapters 9 and 20, which treat regularisation and then the generalisation question honestly as unresolved.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics — the generalisation chapter, for the theoretical state of play in textbook form.

Practice

Reproduce the random-label result at small scale. Train a modest convolutional network on CIFAR-10 with correct labels, then with 50% of labels randomly permuted, then with 100% permuted, recording training and test accuracy and the epochs to convergence in each case. Then repeat the 100%-random run with all explicit regularisation removed, and again with heavy weight decay and dropout. You are done when you can show that the network memorises noise either way and state what that rules out.

Where people go wrong

Reasoning about deep models with the classical intuition that more parameters means more overfitting. It is not merely a simplification here, it is the wrong sign for the regime these models operate in: past the interpolation threshold, adding parameters typically improves test error. The practical consequence is people shrinking a model that is underperforming, when the correct move is usually more data, better augmentation, or a longer schedule.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the random-label finding precisely, name two popular explanations of generalisation it rules out, and describe grokking in a way that makes clear why it is evidence about the optimiser rather than about capacity.

M07

Convolutional networks

≈6 h

Convolutions are the first and clearest case of building a prior into an architecture rather than learning it: translation equivariance and locality are asserted, not discovered, and the parameter saving is enormous. The lineage from LeNet to ConvNeXt is also the best available worked example of architecture search by human iteration, and the modern twist — that a convolutional network updated with transformer-era training recipes matches a vision transformer — is a sharp lesson about attributing gains to architecture.

Concepts

  • The convolution as a constrained dense layer
    • Weight sharing and sparse connectivity: a 3×3 kernel over a 224×224 image is nine parameters doing the work of fifty thousand, which is where the sample efficiency comes from
    • Translation equivariance (the feature map shifts when the input shifts) versus invariance (the output does not change), and the fact that pooling and stride buy the second at the cost of the first
    • Receptive field growth with depth, stride and dilation; computing it for a stack is a skill worth having
    • Padding, stride, channels, and the arithmetic that determines output size and FLOPs
  • The architecture lineage, read as a sequence of specific fixes
    • LeNet-5 (1998): the template, on digits
    • AlexNet (2012): depth, ReLU, dropout, GPU training, augmentation
    • VGG (2014): the observation that stacked 3×3 kernels match a larger receptive field with fewer parameters and more nonlinearity
    • Inception: multi-scale branches and 1×1 convolutions as channel-mixing and dimensionality reduction
    • ResNet (2015): residual connections making 100+ layers trainable, which is M05's material in its original setting
    • DenseNet, and the efficiency line — depthwise separable convolutions (MobileNet), compound scaling (EfficientNet)
    • ConvNeXt (2022): take a ResNet and apply transformer-era training recipes and design choices one at a time, and recover vision-transformer performance. The lesson is about training recipes as a confounder in architecture comparisons
  • Beyond classification
    • Semantic segmentation: fully convolutional networks, encoder–decoder with skip connections, U-Net's shape (which reappears on the generative page)
    • Object detection: the two-stage lineage (R-CNN → Faster R-CNN) versus single-stage (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet), and anchor-free approaches
    • Transposed convolution and its checkerboard artefacts; upsample-then-convolve as the standard fix
  • What convolutional networks actually learn, as far as interpretability has established — Gabor-like filters in the first layer reliably, texture and part detectors deeper, and the finding that ImageNet-trained networks are biased toward texture rather than shape

Work through

  • Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition — LeCun et al.'s LeNet paper, for convolutions motivated from first principles.
  • Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition — revisit it here in its native setting.
  • CS231n: Deep Learning for Computer Vision — the convolutional-network notes, still the best single explanation of the arithmetic and the architectures.
  • Dive into Deep Learning — Chapters 7 and 8, which implement each architecture in the lineage so the differences are concrete.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 10, for the equivariance framing done properly.
  • StatQuest — the convolution videos, if the arithmetic is easier to absorb visually first.

Practice

Implement 2D convolution yourself with explicit loops, then as an im2col matrix multiply, and check both against the framework's version. Then train three networks on CIFAR-10 with matched parameter budgets: a plain MLP, a plain deep convolutional network, and the same convolutional network with residual connections. Finally, compute the theoretical receptive field of your deepest network and verify it empirically by occluding input regions. You are done when the receptive-field calculation and the occlusion experiment agree.

Where people go wrong

Attributing a benchmark improvement to an architectural change when the training recipe also changed. Modern augmentation, longer schedules, AdamW, label smoothing and stochastic depth are collectively worth several points of ImageNet accuracy, and papers routinely bundle them with a new block. ConvNeXt is the demonstration: applying those recipes to a plain ResNet closes most of the gap that was attributed to attention. Before believing an architecture claim, check whether the baseline received the same recipe.

M08

Recurrence, and the bottleneck that produced attention

≈5 h

Recurrent networks are no longer the default for sequences, and this module exists because attention is unintelligible without the problem it solved. Encoder–decoder RNNs compress an entire input sequence into one fixed vector, and attention was introduced as a patch for exactly that. Meeting the transformer without this history makes it look like an arbitrary design; meeting it after makes every component read as a response to a specific failure.

Concepts

  • The recurrent formulation — a hidden state updated at each step by shared weights — and backpropagation through time as M03's algorithm applied to the unrolled graph
    • Why gradients vanish or explode over long sequences: the same weight matrix is applied repeatedly, so the gradient is governed by powers of its spectral radius. Sharper than the general depth argument because the matrix is literally the same one
    • Truncated BPTT as the practical compromise, and what it costs in dependency length
  • Gated architectures as the engineered fix
    • The LSTM's cell state as an additive path with gated writes — structurally the same trick as a residual connection, arriving nine years earlier
    • Forget, input and output gates, and what each controls; the GRU as the simplified variant with comparable performance
    • What gating bought and what it did not: dependencies of hundreds of steps instead of tens, and no help at all with the sequential-computation bottleneck
  • Sequence-to-sequence and the fixed-vector problem
    • Encoder–decoder for translation: read the source into a final hidden state, generate from it. The performance degradation with source length is severe and diagnostic
    • Bahdanau attention: let the decoder compute a weighted combination over all encoder states at every output step, with weights from a learned alignment score. This is where attention comes from and it was a patch, not a paradigm
    • Luong's variants and the multiplicative score that becomes the transformer's dot product
    • Attention weights as a soft alignment, and the caution — well established by later work — that attention weights are not reliable explanations
  • The remaining bottleneck that motivated the transformer — recurrence is inherently sequential, so training cannot be parallelised across the time dimension, and this is a hardware argument rather than a modelling one
    • Where recurrence survives: streaming and online settings with unbounded context and a hard memory budget, and as the conceptual ancestor of the state-space models in M10

Work through

  • Long Short-Term Memory — Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's 1997 paper, for the vanishing-gradient analysis that motivated the design.
  • Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate — Bahdanau et al., where attention is introduced. Read section 3 and note how modest the change is.
  • Understanding LSTM Networks — Olah's diagrams, which are the standard mental model for a reason.
  • Dive into Deep Learning — Chapters 9 and 10, which implement RNNs, LSTMs and Bahdanau attention in sequence.

Practice

Train a character-level LSTM on a small text corpus and sample from it. Then build an encoder–decoder for a toy translation task (reversing or transliterating sequences), and plot BLEU against source length with and without attention. You are done when the no-attention curve degrades visibly with length while the attention curve stays flat — and when you can show the alignment matrix for one example and see the diagonal.

Where people go wrong

Reading attention weights as an explanation of the model's decision. It is intuitive — the weights look like an alignment and often are one — but multiple studies have shown that quite different attention distributions produce identical outputs, and that attention weights can be adversarially altered without changing predictions. They are a useful diagnostic and a poor explanation, and M18's mechanistic methods exist because of exactly this gap.

M09

The transformer, mechanically

≈8 h

The transformer is the architecture the rest of the field now runs on, and it is short enough to implement completely in an afternoon — which is the only way to know it. This module goes component by component, and for each one names the alternative that lost and why. It is the longest module on the page because everything from M10 onward, and the whole of the language-model page, assumes it in detail.

Concepts

  • Scaled dot-product attention, derived rather than presented
    • Queries, keys and values as three learned projections of the same input, and the retrieval metaphor: each position asks a question, every position advertises a key, and the answer is a weighted sum of values
    • softmax(QKᵀ/√d)V — and why the √d: without it, dot products of d-dimensional vectors have variance growing with d, pushing softmax into its saturated regime where gradients vanish
    • Complexity: O(n²d) time and O(n²) memory in sequence length, which is the constraint that M10 exists to relax
    • Causal masking for autoregressive models, implemented as −∞ before the softmax
  • Multi-head attention
    • Split the model dimension across h heads, attend independently, concatenate, project. The parameter count is unchanged; what is bought is multiple attention patterns per layer
    • What heads empirically do — positional, syntactic, and the induction heads that M18 analyses — and the finding that many heads can be pruned with little loss
  • Position, which attention has no notion of
    • Attention is permutation-equivariant, so position must be injected. Sinusoidal encodings in the original paper; learned absolute embeddings in BERT and GPT-2
    • Relative position (T5 biases, ALiBi) and their extrapolation behaviour
    • RoPE: rotate query and key vectors by a position-dependent angle so that the dot product depends on relative position by construction. Now the default, and the reason context-length extension is possible at all
  • The rest of the block, each item a decision
    • The position-wise feed-forward network, typically 4× the model dimension, which holds roughly two-thirds of the parameters and — per M18 — appears to act as key–value memory
    • Activation choice: ReLU → GELU → SwiGLU, the last now standard in large models
    • Pre-norm versus post-norm, from M05, with pre-norm winning for stability at depth
    • Residual stream as the unifying picture: every block reads from and writes to a shared residual stream, which is the frame M18's interpretability work depends on
  • The three configurations and what each is for — encoder-only (BERT, bidirectional, for representation), decoder-only (GPT, causal, for generation), encoder–decoder (T5, for conditional sequence transduction) — and why decoder-only won for general-purpose models

Work through

  • Attention Is All You Need — the original. Read it after implementing, not before; it is terse and assumes the seq2seq context from M08.
  • The Illustrated Transformer — Alammar's walkthrough, which is the standard first explanation and worth reading before the paper.
  • The Annotated Transformer — the Harvard NLP line-by-line implementation alongside the paper text.
  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — the "Let's build GPT" video, which constructs a working decoder-only transformer from nothing in two hours.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 12, for the textbook treatment with the attention arithmetic laid out.
  • A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits — read the residual-stream framing in the first sections; it is the most useful way to think about the architecture even outside interpretability.
  • nanoGPT — read it against your own implementation once yours trains; the gap is the production detail.
  • Hugging Face Transformers documentation — the modelling source for a model you have just implemented — the reference implementation, annotated.
  • CS25: Transformers United — the seminar talks by the architecture's own authors on why each decision was made.

Practice

Implement a decoder-only transformer from scratch in PyTorch — token and position embeddings, multi-head causal self-attention with RoPE, pre-norm blocks with a SwiGLU feed-forward, and weight tying — and train it on a few megabytes of text. Verify your attention implementation against the framework's fused version to numerical agreement. Then ablate one component at a time: remove the √d scaling, remove position information entirely, switch pre-norm to post-norm. You are done when each ablation's failure mode matches what the mechanism predicts.

Where people go wrong

Treating the √d scaling as a cosmetic normalisation. Without it, the pre-softmax logits have variance proportional to the head dimension, so at d = 128 the softmax is effectively an argmax from initialisation, gradients through it are near zero, and the model trains extremely poorly or not at all. It is one line and it is load-bearing — which makes it the best available demonstration that in this architecture the small numerical details are not incidental.

Checkpoint

M10

Efficient attention, state-space models and mixtures of experts

≈6 h

M09's attention is quadratic in sequence length and its feed-forward layers are dense in parameters. Both are binding constraints at scale, and this module covers the three families of response — make attention cheaper, replace it, or make the parameters sparse. It also contains the field's cleanest example of a systems insight beating an algorithmic one: FlashAttention changed nothing about the mathematics and delivered more speedup than years of approximate-attention research.

Concepts

  • Exact attention, made fast — the approach that won
    • The observation that attention is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound: the n × n matrix is written to and read from high-bandwidth memory, and that traffic dominates
    • FlashAttention: tile the computation, use online softmax so the full matrix is never materialised, recompute in the backward pass instead of storing. Exact, not approximate — same outputs, several times faster and linear in memory
    • The lesson: an IO-aware analysis on real hardware beat a large literature of asymptotically better approximations
    • KV caching for autoregressive inference, and grouped-query and multi-query attention as the standard ways to shrink that cache
  • Approximate and structured attention, and why most of it did not stick
    • Sparse patterns (Longformer, BigBird): local windows plus a few global tokens, with theoretical universality results
    • Low-rank and kernel methods (Linformer, Performer): approximate the softmax kernel to get linear complexity
    • The honest assessment: on realistic benchmarks these usually trade quality for speed, and once FlashAttention made exact attention cheap the case for most of them weakened considerably
  • State-space models, the serious alternative
    • The lineage from continuous linear state-space systems: S4's structured parameterisation, and the connection back to M08's recurrence with a much better-behaved recurrence matrix
    • Mamba's selectivity — making the state-space parameters input-dependent, which breaks the convolutional view and requires a hardware-aware scan implementation
    • The real trade: linear scaling and constant-memory inference, against a fixed-size state that provably cannot do exact recall over long contexts the way attention can
    • Hybrid architectures interleaving SSM and attention layers as the current pragmatic answer, on the reasoning that you need attention only for the recall-heavy operations
  • Mixture of experts — sparsity in parameters rather than in attention
    • Replace the feed-forward layer with N experts and a router that activates k of them per token, decoupling total parameters from per-token compute
    • The engineering problems that dominate: load balancing (auxiliary losses to stop the router collapsing onto a few experts), expert capacity and token dropping, and the all-to-all communication that makes MoE a distributed-systems problem
    • Why MoE is now standard in frontier models, and the costs — memory for all experts regardless of activation, and harder fine-tuning

Work through

  • FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness — read the IO-complexity analysis in section 3, which is the transferable idea.
  • Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces — for the selectivity mechanism and the hardware-aware scan.
  • Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer — the paper that introduced the modern MoE layer and its load-balancing loss.
  • Efficient Transformers: A Survey — for the taxonomy of the approximate-attention literature, read as a map rather than a menu.
  • The Annotated S4 — the line-by-line implementation, which is the only way the state-space parameterisation becomes concrete.
  • AI Coffee Break with Letitia — the state-space and mixture-of-experts explainers, which are the clearest short treatments of both.

Practice

Benchmark attention implementations on your own hardware: a naive PyTorch version, the fused scaled-dot-product-attention kernel, and a windowed sparse version, at sequence lengths from 512 to 16,384. Plot wall-clock time and peak memory against length. Then implement a simple mixture-of-experts feed-forward layer with top-2 routing and log the expert-utilisation histogram over training with and without a load-balancing loss. You are done when you have observed router collapse yourself.

Where people go wrong

Choosing a linear-attention variant on the basis of its asymptotic complexity. At the sequence lengths most work actually uses, the constants dominate and a fused exact implementation is usually both faster and better. The quadratic term only becomes decisive at very long contexts, and by then the interesting question is whether you need exact recall — which is the state-space trade — rather than whether you can approximate the softmax. Benchmark on your own shapes before believing any complexity claim.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why FlashAttention is faster despite computing exactly the same function, and state the fundamental capability difference between a fixed-size recurrent state and attention over the full context.

M11

Training at scale, and the memory arithmetic

≈7 h

Beyond a certain model size the binding constraint stops being ideas and starts being memory, bandwidth and failure rates. This module is the arithmetic — how much memory a run needs, where it goes, and which parallelism strategy fits — because that calculation is the first thing anyone doing large-scale work has to be able to do, and it is almost never taught. It is also the module that explains why frontier training is a systems discipline rather than a modelling one.

Concepts

  • The memory budget, itemised
    • Parameters, gradients, and optimiser state — Adam keeps two moments, so mixed-precision training with an fp32 master copy costs roughly 16 bytes per parameter before any activations. A 7-billion-parameter model is therefore about 112 GB of state alone
    • Activations, which scale with batch size, sequence length and depth, and typically dominate at small model sizes
    • Fragmentation and workspace as the reason measured usage exceeds the arithmetic
  • Precision
    • fp32, fp16 with loss scaling to avoid gradient underflow, bf16 with its wider exponent and no need for loss scaling, and fp8 on recent hardware
    • Master weights in higher precision, and which operations (softmax, layer norm, loss) must stay in fp32 for numerical stability
  • The parallelism strategies, and when each applies
    • Data parallelism: replicate the model, split the batch, all-reduce the gradients. Simple, and it does nothing about a model that does not fit
    • ZeRO / FSDP: shard optimiser state, gradients and parameters across data-parallel ranks, gathering them just in time. The three stages and the communication each adds
    • Tensor parallelism: split individual matrices across devices, with an all-reduce inside every layer — so it needs very fast interconnect and is normally kept within a node
    • Pipeline parallelism: split layers across devices, with micro-batching to fill the bubble; the bubble fraction as the quantity to compute
    • Sequence and context parallelism for long sequences; expert parallelism for M10's MoE
    • 3D parallelism as the composition, and the practical rule of thumb: tensor parallel within a node, pipeline across nodes, data parallel outermost
  • Making it fit and making it fast
    • Gradient checkpointing: store a subset of activations and recompute the rest, trading roughly 30% more compute for a large memory saving
    • Gradient accumulation to simulate large batches; the difference between micro-batch and global batch
    • Model FLOPs utilisation as the metric that matters, and why a well-tuned large run reaches only a fraction of peak
    • Overlapping communication with computation, which is where most of the engineering effort goes
  • Failure as a design constraint — at thousands of accelerators for weeks, hardware failures are routine, so checkpointing frequency and restart cost are first-order design decisions, and loss spikes require a documented recovery procedure rather than improvisation

Work through

  • ZeRO: Memory Optimizations Toward Training Trillion Parameter Models — the memory analysis in section 3 is the clearest itemisation of where training memory goes.
  • Mixed Precision Training — for loss scaling and the operations that must stay in fp32.
  • GPipe: Efficient Training of Giant Neural Networks Using Pipeline Parallelism — pipeline parallelism and the bubble analysis.
  • How to Scale Your Model — a systems-level treatment of exactly this arithmetic, worked through rather than asserted.
  • The Ultra-Scale Playbook — Hugging Face's guide to distributed training, with runnable configurations for each strategy.
  • PyTorch documentation — the FSDP and distributed guides, which are where the abstractions meet the API.

Practice

Write a memory calculator: given parameter count, optimiser, precision, batch size, sequence length and layer count, predict total memory and the split between parameters, optimiser state, gradients and activations. Then train a model on multiple GPUs and compare your prediction against the measured peak. Enable gradient checkpointing and re-measure. You are done when your calculator is within 15% of reality and you can explain the residual.

Where people go wrong

Estimating memory as parameter count times bytes per parameter. For Adam in mixed precision the real figure is roughly eight times that before a single activation is stored — fp16 parameters and gradients, plus fp32 master weights and two fp32 moments. People size a run by the weights alone, hit an out-of-memory error at step one, and reach for a smaller batch when the actual fix is sharding the optimiser state. Do the arithmetic before requesting the hardware.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the full memory requirement for a 7-billion-parameter model trained with AdamW in bf16, and choose a parallelism strategy for a given cluster topology with a reason that references interconnect bandwidth rather than convention.

M12

Scaling laws

≈5 h

Scaling laws are the closest thing deep learning has to a quantitative predictive theory, and they are the reason frontier training is planned rather than attempted. The finding — that loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute across many orders of magnitude — is genuinely surprising and has held up. Equally important is what the laws do not predict, which is where most over-claiming happens.

Concepts

  • The empirical result
    • Test loss as a power law in each of parameters N, dataset size D and compute C, with the other two held non-binding; smooth over more than seven orders of magnitude in compute
    • The irreducible-loss term, and why the curve must eventually flatten
    • Kaplan et al.'s original conclusion — scale parameters faster than data — and Hoffmann et al.'s correction: at compute-optimal allocation, parameters and tokens should scale roughly in proportion. Chinchilla at 70B parameters beat Gopher at 280B on equal compute, which is the concrete demonstration
    • The methodological reason the first answer was wrong: the original fits held the learning-rate schedule fixed in a way that disadvantaged smaller models trained longer
  • Using the laws
    • Deriving the compute-optimal N and D for a budget, and the arithmetic C ≈ 6ND for a dense transformer
    • Why deployment economics push past compute-optimal: inference cost scales with parameters, not with training tokens, so a smaller model trained far longer is usually the right product decision even though it is not compute-optimal
    • Fitting your own scaling law on a small ladder of models to predict a large run — the standard practice, and the sanity check that catches a broken configuration before it costs a month
  • What the laws do not tell you
    • They predict loss, not capability. The mapping from loss to downstream task performance is not a power law and is not smooth
    • Emergent abilities — sharp jumps in benchmark performance at scale — and the substantial argument that many are artefacts of discontinuous metrics such as exact-match accuracy, which disappear under continuous scoring
    • Nothing about data quality, which changes the constant and sometimes the exponent; nothing about architecture beyond broad families; nothing about what happens when high-quality data runs out
  • The current state — data-constrained scaling, repeated-epoch results, and the shift of marginal compute toward post-training and inference-time methods rather than pretraining, which is the live strategic question and is developed on the language-model page

Work through

  • Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models — the original Kaplan et al. paper, read knowing its allocation conclusion was later corrected.
  • Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Chinchilla, including the three independent estimation approaches and why they agree.
  • Deep Learning Scaling Is Predictable, Empirically — the earlier Baidu paper that found power-law scaling across four domains before it was famous.
  • Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage? — the metric-discontinuity argument, which is the necessary counterweight to the emergence literature.
  • arXiv cs.LG — follow the scaling-law authors rather than the topic; this literature moves by group, not by keyword.

Practice

Fit your own scaling law. Train a ladder of six small transformers — spanning at least two orders of magnitude in parameters — on the same corpus with matched, properly tuned schedules, and record final loss and compute for each. Fit a power law and extrapolate to a model four times larger than your biggest. Then train that model and check the prediction. You are done when your extrapolation is within a few percent, or when you can explain which of your assumptions broke.

Where people go wrong

Reading a scaling law as a prediction about capability. The laws predict cross-entropy loss, and the relationship between loss and any particular downstream task is neither smooth nor known in advance — a large loss improvement can produce no benchmark movement, and a small one can cross a threshold. The emergence debate is exactly this confusion made visible: several dramatic "emergent" jumps turned out to be artefacts of scoring a task by exact match rather than by a continuous metric.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive the compute-optimal parameter and token counts for a stated compute budget using C ≈ 6ND, and then explain why a production team would deliberately train past that point.

M13

Self-supervised learning and transfer

≈6 h

Labels are the scarce resource; raw data is not. Self-supervised learning is the set of tricks for manufacturing a supervision signal from unlabelled data, and it is what made pretraining-then-adapting the default workflow across every modality. This module is also where the two dominant recipes — contrastive and masked-prediction — are set against each other, because their engineering requirements are completely different and choosing between them is a real decision.

Concepts

  • The transfer workflow, and its options
    • Pretrain on a large generic corpus, then adapt: linear probing (freeze the backbone, fit a classifier), full fine-tuning, or parameter-efficient methods
    • Linear probe versus fine-tune as a measurement: a high linear-probe score says the representation is linearly separable, which is a stronger claim about the features than fine-tuned accuracy
    • Catastrophic forgetting, layer-wise learning-rate decay, and discriminative fine-tuning as the standard mitigations
    • LoRA and adapters, mentioned here and developed on the language-model page
  • Contrastive learning
    • The objective: pull augmented views of the same example together, push different examples apart, with InfoNCE as the loss
    • SimCLR's finding that the augmentation policy is the single most important design choice — crop plus colour jitter carries most of the benefit — and its dependence on very large batches for enough negatives
    • MoCo's momentum encoder and queue as the alternative to large batches
    • BYOL and SimSiam: no negatives at all, avoiding collapse through a predictor head and a stop-gradient, which was surprising and is still not fully explained
    • Dimensional collapse as the characteristic failure, and the uniformity/alignment framing for diagnosing it
  • Masked prediction
    • BERT's masked language modelling; the 15% masking ratio and the mask-token train/test discrepancy
    • Masked autoencoders for images: a much higher masking ratio (around 75%) because pixels are far more redundant than tokens, with an asymmetric encoder–decoder so the encoder only sees visible patches
    • Why masked methods need no negatives, no large batches and no careful augmentation policy — a substantially simpler engineering problem than contrastive learning
  • Other self-supervised signals worth knowing — next-token prediction as the dominant one for text and increasingly for everything, plus the self-distillation family (DINO) and joint-embedding predictive architectures
    • The pretext-task era (rotation prediction, jigsaw, colourisation) and why it was superseded: those tasks were solvable by shortcuts that did not require good representations
    • Shortcut learning as the general failure mode of any hand-designed pretext task

Work through

  • A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations — SimCLR. The augmentation ablation in section 3 is the part that matters.
  • Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners — for the asymmetric design and the argument about why the masking ratio must be so much higher for images than for text.
  • BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding — the masked-language-modelling objective in its original setting.
  • Bootstrap Your Own Latent — BYOL, for the result that contrastive learning does not actually need negatives.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — the self-supervision material, for a textbook framing of the family.

Practice

Pretrain a small encoder on unlabelled CIFAR-10 two ways — SimCLR-style contrastive and a masked-autoencoder — then evaluate both by linear probe and by full fine-tuning with 1%, 10% and 100% of the labels. Plot accuracy against label fraction for both methods and a from-scratch baseline. Then rerun the contrastive version with only crop augmentation, and with batch size 64 instead of 512. You are done when both ablations degrade in the way the papers predict.

Where people go wrong

Running contrastive learning with a small batch and concluding the method is weak. The InfoNCE objective's quality depends directly on the number of negatives, and at batch size 64 there are not enough for the loss to be informative — SimCLR's results assume batches in the thousands. If large batches are not available the correct response is a momentum queue or a masked-prediction method, not more epochs of a starved contrastive objective.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why masked autoencoding needs a 75% masking ratio on images and 15% on text, and say what a high linear-probe score establishes that a high fine-tuned score does not.

M14

Vision transformers and multimodal models

≈5 h

Two results converge here. The vision transformer showed that the convolutional prior is not necessary given enough data — a general architecture plus scale beats a domain-specific one, which is the bitter lesson in its most direct experimental form. CLIP then showed that aligning two modalities in a shared embedding space produces zero-shot transfer, and that alignment is the mechanism behind every text-conditioned generative model on the sibling page.

Concepts

  • The vision transformer
    • Images as sequences of patches: split into 16×16 patches, linearly embed, add position embeddings, feed to a standard transformer encoder
    • The data-regime finding, which is the actual contribution: ViT underperforms a ResNet on ImageNet-scale data and overtakes it given hundreds of millions of images. The convolutional inductive bias is a substitute for data
    • Hybrid and hierarchical variants (Swin's shifted windows) that reintroduce locality and multi-scale structure for dense prediction tasks
    • ConvNeXt from M07 as the counter-result, and the honest reading of the pair: architecture mattered less than the training recipe and the data scale
  • CLIP and contrastive language–image pretraining
    • Two encoders, one for images and one for text, trained so matched pairs have high cosine similarity and mismatched pairs low — a symmetric InfoNCE over the batch
    • Zero-shot classification by embedding class names as text and taking the nearest: a genuinely new capability, since the class set is specified at inference time
    • Why prompt phrasing matters ("a photo of a {}") and what that reveals about the text encoder's distribution
    • The known weaknesses: poor compositional and relational understanding, weak counting, and benchmark performance that is substantially explained by pretraining-data overlap
  • Multimodal architectures beyond dual encoders
    • Cross-attention fusion (Flamingo's perceiver resampler) and projection-into-the-language-model approaches (LLaVA-style), which is now the dominant recipe for vision-language models
    • The general pattern: freeze a strong unimodal model, train a small connector, fine-tune lightly. Cheap and effective, and it is why capable vision-language models appeared so quickly
    • Audio and video encoders following the same template
  • Why this module is load-bearing for the sibling page — CLIP's text encoder and its embedding space are the conditioning mechanism for text-to-image diffusion, and its score is used (with well-documented caveats) as an automatic evaluation metric there

Work through

  • An Image Is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale — read Figure 4 and the data-regime discussion, which is the paper's real finding.
  • Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision — CLIP. Long, and worth reading sections 2–3 and the limitations section in full.
  • A ConvNet for the 2020s — ConvNeXt, as the controlled dismantling of the architecture-versus-recipe question.
  • Flamingo: a Visual Language Model for Few-Shot Learning — for the cross-attention fusion design and the frozen-backbone pattern.
  • CS231n: Deep Learning for Computer Vision — its transformer and multimodal lectures for the course treatment.
  • Yannic Kilcher's paper reviews — his CLIP and ViT walkthroughs, useful for the figure-by-figure reading of two long papers.

Practice

Take a pretrained CLIP model and build a zero-shot classifier for a dataset it was not evaluated on. Measure how much accuracy moves as you vary the prompt template across five phrasings, then try prompt ensembling. Next, probe its compositional understanding: construct pairs like "a red cube on a blue sphere" versus "a blue cube on a red sphere" and measure how often it distinguishes them. You are done when you have quantified both the prompt sensitivity and the compositional failure.

Where people go wrong

Reading CLIP's zero-shot numbers as evidence of general visual understanding. Its pretraining corpus is enormous and largely undisclosed, and there is good evidence that performance on a given benchmark tracks how well that benchmark's concepts are represented in the pretraining data — which makes "zero-shot" a claim about the evaluation protocol rather than about novelty. Test on a genuinely unusual domain before relying on it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why ViT needs more data than a ResNet to reach the same accuracy, in terms of what the convolutional prior supplies — and describe how CLIP's embedding space is used to condition a text-to-image model.

M15

Graph neural networks

≈5 h

Grids and sequences are special cases of graphs, and a great deal of real data — molecules, social networks, dependency structures, meshes, code — is natively graph-shaped with no canonical ordering. Message passing is the generalisation of convolution to that setting, and this module also delivers the field's cleanest expressiveness result: a precise theorem bounding what standard GNNs can and cannot distinguish. That kind of sharp limit is rare in deep learning and worth seeing.

Concepts

  • Message passing as the unifying framework
    • Each node aggregates transformed messages from its neighbours with a permutation-invariant function, then updates its own state; stack for multi-hop information
    • Permutation equivariance as the required symmetry, exactly analogous to translation equivariance in M07
    • The main variants as choices of aggregator: GCN (degree-normalised mean), GraphSAGE (sample and aggregate, enabling minibatching), GAT (attention-weighted), GIN (sum, chosen for expressiveness)
    • Readout functions for graph-level tasks, and node-, edge- and graph-level prediction as the three task types
  • Expressiveness, stated precisely
    • Message-passing GNNs are at most as powerful as the 1-Weisfeiler–Lehman graph isomorphism test at distinguishing graphs — a hard upper bound with a clean proof
    • The immediate consequence: they cannot count triangles or distinguish certain regular graphs, which matters for molecular property prediction where rings are the chemistry
    • GIN's sum aggregator as the choice that attains the 1-WL bound, and why mean and max fall short of it
    • Routes past the bound: higher-order GNNs, random node features, subgraph methods, positional and structural encodings
  • The characteristic failure modes
    • Over-smoothing: repeated neighbourhood averaging drives node representations toward a common value, so deep GNNs get worse. Residuals, jumping-knowledge connections and normalisation help, and most deployed GNNs remain shallow
    • Over-squashing: information from an exponentially growing receptive field is compressed through a fixed-width bottleneck, which is why long-range dependencies on graphs are hard; graph rewiring as one response
    • Scalability: neighbourhood explosion in dense graphs, and sampling strategies as the mitigation
  • Graph transformers, and where the field has moved — treat the graph as a fully connected set with structural encodings, trading the locality prior for expressiveness, which is the same bargain ViT made in M14
    • Applications that actually pay: molecular property prediction and force fields, recommendation, traffic forecasting, and combinatorial optimisation heuristics

Work through

  • How Powerful Are Graph Neural Networks? — the WL bound and GIN. The proof is followable and the result is the most useful thing in this module.
  • Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks — Kipf and Welling's GCN, the paper that made the area accessible.
  • A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks — the interactive Distill article, which is the fastest way to build the mental model.
  • Geometric Deep Learning — the blueprint paper, for the symmetry-based framing that unifies convolutions, attention and message passing.
  • PyTorch Geometric — the library and its tutorials, for actually building one.

Practice

Implement message passing from scratch with a configurable aggregator (sum, mean, max) and test all three on a task that requires counting neighbours — then confirm that only sum can do it. Next, construct two non-isomorphic regular graphs that 1-WL cannot distinguish and verify that your GNN gives them identical embeddings. Finally, train GCNs of depth 2, 4, 8 and 16 on a node-classification benchmark and plot accuracy against depth. You are done when over-smoothing is visible in your own curve.

Where people go wrong

Building a deep GNN by analogy with deep convolutional networks. Beyond about four message-passing layers, node representations converge toward each other and accuracy falls — over-smoothing is a property of repeated averaging over a connected graph, not a training problem to be tuned away. If you need long-range information, the answer is a graph transformer, a rewiring, or an explicit global node, not more layers.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the 1-WL upper bound on message-passing GNNs, give a concrete pair of graphs they cannot distinguish, and explain why the sum aggregator is more expressive than the mean.

M16

Deep reinforcement learning

≈7 h

The Classical AI page established MDPs, the Bellman equations and tabular Q-learning, and ended by naming the deadly triad — function approximation plus bootstrapping plus off-policy training — as the thing that breaks the convergence guarantees. This module is what happens when you take that risk deliberately, and the engineering responses that make it work anyway. It is also the direct ancestor of the RLHF and RLVR machinery on the language-model page, which is why policy-gradient methods get the weight here.

Concepts

  • Value-based deep RL
    • DQN: a network approximating Q(s, a), trained on the Bellman target. Its two innovations are both mitigations of the deadly triad — a replay buffer to decorrelate samples and restore something closer to i.i.d., and a target network to stop the regression target moving with the parameters
    • The improvements, each fixing a named defect: double DQN (maximisation bias), duelling architectures (separating state value from advantage), prioritised replay, n-step returns, distributional RL (C51), noisy networks — and Rainbow as the combination with its ablation study
    • Why value-based methods are awkward with continuous actions, which is what motivates the policy-gradient family
  • Policy gradients
    • REINFORCE and the score-function estimator; baselines and advantage estimation for variance reduction; GAE as the standard bias–variance knob
    • Actor–critic methods, and A2C/A3C as the parallel versions
    • Trust regions: TRPO's constrained update, and PPO's clipped surrogate objective as the practical approximation that made the idea usable. PPO is the algorithm to know in detail, because it is the one that reappears in RLHF
    • Continuous control: DDPG, TD3 and SAC, with SAC's maximum-entropy objective as the current strong default
  • Model-based and offline RL
    • Learning a dynamics model and planning in it; the sample-efficiency gain and the compounding-error problem; Dreamer-style latent world models and the MuZero lineage from the Classical AI page
    • Offline RL: learning from a fixed dataset with no environment access, where distribution shift and extrapolation on out-of-distribution actions are the central difficulty; conservative methods as the response
  • The reproducibility problem, which this subfield deserves to be known for
    • Results that vary by more than the claimed improvement across random seeds; implementation details worth more than algorithmic differences; the finding that PPO's advantage over its predecessors is largely attributable to code-level optimisations rather than the clipped objective
    • Reward hacking as the practical face of the classical page's specification problem — agents exploiting simulator bugs and metric loopholes, reliably and creatively
    • What to do about it: many seeds, report distributions rather than best runs, and use a reference implementation before writing your own

Work through

  • Human-Level Control Through Deep Reinforcement Learning — the DQN paper. Read the methods section for replay and the target network, which are the deadly-triad mitigations.
  • Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms — PPO. Short, and the algorithm you will meet again in RLHF.
  • Spinning Up in Deep RL — OpenAI's guide, with clean single-file implementations and the derivations of the policy-gradient family.
  • CS 285: Deep Reinforcement Learning — Levine's Berkeley course, the standard graduate treatment, with full lecture videos.
  • Deep Reinforcement Learning That Matters — the reproducibility study, which should be read before trusting any result in this area including your own.

Practice

Implement DQN and PPO from scratch and run each on the same environment with ten random seeds. Plot the mean and the full spread of returns, not just the best run. Then ablate DQN's target network and its replay buffer separately and watch each removal destabilise training in a different way. You are done when your seed spread is wide enough that you would not trust a two-seed comparison — which is the point of the exercise.

Where people go wrong

Comparing algorithms on a handful of seeds. Deep RL's variance across random seeds routinely exceeds the difference between algorithms, so a three-seed comparison is a coin flip dressed as a result — and this has been demonstrated repeatedly, including for published improvements that did not survive re-evaluation. Run at least ten seeds, report the distribution, and treat any single-run curve, including your own, as an anecdote.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name the three components of the deadly triad, point at the specific DQN component that mitigates each, and explain PPO's clipped objective well enough to say what would go wrong without the clip.

M17

Theory: neural tangent kernels, implicit bias and lottery tickets

≈6 h

M06 posed the puzzle; this module reports on the attempts to solve it. The honest summary is that no theory yet explains why deep networks generalise, but several explain fragments, and knowing which fragment each covers — and where it stops — is what separates useful theoretical literacy from either dismissal or over-reliance. This is a module about the state of an open problem.

Concepts

  • The infinite-width limit
    • A randomly initialised network with infinite width is a Gaussian process, which connects deep learning to the classical page's kernel methods exactly
    • The neural tangent kernel: in that limit, training by gradient descent is kernel regression with a fixed kernel determined at initialisation, and the parameters barely move — the "lazy" regime
    • What it explains: why training converges despite non-convexity, and why very wide networks are well-behaved
    • Where it stops, and this is the important part: real networks do learn features, which the NTK regime by construction does not. Empirically, finite networks outperform their own NTK, so the limit describes a regime that is not the interesting one. The mean-field or feature-learning parameterisation is the alternative scaling that keeps feature learning alive
  • Implicit regularisation as the leading candidate explanation
    • The observation: among the many parameter settings that fit the training data exactly, gradient descent reliably finds particular ones. The algorithm, not the hypothesis class, is doing the capacity control
    • The one clean case: for separable data with logistic loss, gradient descent on a linear model converges in direction to the maximum-margin solution — proved, and the template for what a deep result would look like
    • Candidate mechanisms in the deep case: norm minimisation, SGD noise driving toward flat regions, and the sharpness-aware minimisation results as indirect evidence
    • Why this reframes M06 entirely — the right complexity measure is a property of the optimisation trajectory, not of the parameter count
  • The lottery ticket hypothesis
    • The claim: a randomly initialised dense network contains a sparse subnetwork that, trained in isolation from the same initialisation, matches the full network's accuracy
    • Iterative magnitude pruning as the procedure that finds them, and the crucial detail that resetting to the original initialisation is required — random reinitialisation of the same mask fails
    • Rewinding to an early-training checkpoint rather than initialisation, needed for the result to hold at scale
    • The honest caveats: finding a ticket costs more than training the dense network, and the effect is weaker and more fragile on large models. It is a probe into what training does, not a compression method
  • Other threads worth knowing — PAC-Bayes bounds as the only family currently producing non-vacuous numbers for real networks, information-bottleneck accounts and their replication difficulties, and the honest position that these are complementary partial descriptions rather than competing complete theories

Work through

  • Neural Tangent Kernel: Convergence and Generalization in Neural Networks — the original NTK paper. Dense; read the setup and the main theorem and skip the proofs on a first pass.
  • The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis: Finding Sparse, Trainable Neural Networks — read it for the experimental design, especially the reinitialisation control that makes the result meaningful.
  • The Implicit Bias of Gradient Descent on Separable Data — the clean max-margin result, and the model for what a deep version would need to prove.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics — for a textbook survey of these threads in one notation.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 20, which is unusually honest about what remains unexplained.

Practice

Demonstrate implicit bias directly. Take a linearly separable two-dimensional dataset with infinitely many separating hyperplanes and train an unregularised linear model with logistic loss by gradient descent for a very long time. Plot the decision boundary at increasing step counts and show it converging to the maximum-margin solution. Then run iterative magnitude pruning on a small network on MNIST, and compare three conditions at each sparsity level: the winning ticket, the same mask with random reinitialisation, and a random mask. You are done when the reinitialisation control fails as the hypothesis predicts.

Where people go wrong

Citing the NTK as an explanation of why deep learning works. It explains a regime — infinite width, lazy training, no feature learning — that the useful models are demonstrably not in: finite networks beat their own NTK, and feature learning is the thing the whole field is built on. It is a genuine and valuable result about a limit, and treating it as the theory of deep learning gets the relationship backwards.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state what the NTK regime explains and the specific empirical fact that shows real networks are outside it, and explain why the lottery ticket result's reinitialisation control is what makes it interesting.

M18

Mechanistic interpretability

≈6 h

M17 asks why networks generalise; this module asks what they compute, which turns out to be a more tractable question and has produced sharper results. Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer specific learned algorithms rather than to produce attributions, and the distinction from the classical page's SHAP and LIME is exactly the point: those explain a model's output, these attempt to explain its mechanism. The field is young, partially successful, and its central obstacle — superposition — is a real finding about how networks store information.

Concepts

  • The programme and its vocabulary
    • Features as the unit of representation, circuits as subgraphs implementing an algorithm, and the residual stream as the shared communication channel every block reads and writes
    • The distinction from attribution methods: the goal is a causal, mechanistic account you can intervene on, not a decomposition of the output
    • Attention heads as reading from and writing to subspaces; QK and OV circuits as the two computations a head performs
  • Superposition, the central obstacle
    • Networks represent more features than they have dimensions, by storing them in overlapping non-orthogonal directions — possible because features are sparse, so interference is rare
    • The immediate consequence: individual neurons are polysemantic, responding to several unrelated features, so neuron-level analysis is looking at the wrong basis
    • Toy models where superposition can be induced and studied exactly, including the geometric structures (digons, pentagons) that features arrange themselves into
  • The methods that work
    • Sparse autoencoders: train an overcomplete autoencoder on activations with an L1 penalty to recover a sparse, more monosemantic feature basis — currently the main tool for finding interpretable directions at scale
    • Activation patching and causal tracing: replace an activation with one from a different input and measure the effect on the output, which establishes a causal role rather than a correlation
    • Probing classifiers, and the standard objection that a probe's success may reflect the probe's capacity rather than the representation's content — hence control tasks
    • Logit lens and the practice of decoding intermediate residual-stream states through the output embedding
    • Activation steering: add a direction to the residual stream at inference and observe a behavioural change, which is both a test and a control method
  • What has actually been established
    • Induction heads: a two-head circuit implementing "find the previous occurrence of the current token and predict what followed it", which forms during a visible phase change in training and is strongly associated with in-context learning
    • Indirect object identification as a fully reverse-engineered circuit in a small model
    • Grokking explained mechanistically on modular arithmetic — the network transitions from memorisation to a Fourier-basis algorithm, which is M06's anomaly given a mechanism
    • The honest limits: these are small models and narrow behaviours, sparse autoencoder features are not obviously the right decomposition, and evaluating an interpretability claim is itself unsolved

Work through

  • A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits — the foundational document. Read the residual-stream and QK/OV decomposition sections carefully.
  • Toy Models of Superposition — where superposition is demonstrated and studied in a setting small enough to understand exactly.
  • In-Context Learning and Induction Heads — the strongest available link between a specific circuit and a macroscopic capability.
  • Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models — the method that made feature extraction practical at scale.
  • Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits — the Distill article on circuits in vision models, which is where the programme started and is still the clearest introduction.
  • TransformerLens — the library for running these experiments; it exists to make activation patching a few lines.
  • Mechanistic interpretability exercises — Nanda's guided exercises, which are the concrete answer to how to start doing this.
  • Transformer Circuits Thread — the running publication venue; read the most recent entries after the foundational ones.

Practice

Take a small pretrained transformer and find its induction heads: construct sequences with repeated random token patterns, measure each head's attention to the position after the earlier occurrence, and rank them. Then verify causality by ablating the top candidates and measuring the loss increase on repeated versus non-repeated sequences. Finally, train a tiny model on modular addition until it groks, and inspect its weights in the Fourier basis. You are done when your induction-head ranking survives the ablation test.

Where people go wrong

Interpreting individual neurons. Superposition means a neuron is generally a mixture of unrelated features, so a neuron that fires on "Canada" and on Python list comprehensions is not confused — it is the expected consequence of storing more features than dimensions. Finding a maximally activating input for a neuron and narrating what it "detects" produces confident and unreliable stories. Work in a learned sparse basis, and verify any claimed mechanism by intervention rather than by correlation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain superposition and why it implies neurons are the wrong unit of analysis, describe what an induction head computes in two sentences, and say how activation patching establishes causation where a probe establishes only correlation.

M19

Robustness, adversarial examples and calibration

≈5 h

A model with excellent test accuracy can be confidently wrong on inputs a human would find identical, systematically over-confident on everything, and dependent on a background correlation that will not survive deployment. These three failures share a cause — the model learned the statistics of the training distribution, which is exactly what it was asked to do — and this module covers what is known about each. It is the deep-learning counterpart to the classical page's shift and calibration material, and the failures are sharper here.

Concepts

  • Adversarial examples
    • Imperceptible perturbations, found by ascending the loss gradient, that flip the prediction with high confidence; FGSM and PGD as the standard attacks
    • Transferability across architectures and training sets, which rules out the explanation that they are idiosyncratic artefacts of one model
    • The "features not bugs" argument: adversarial directions correspond to genuine, predictive, non-robust features in the data — an uncomfortable and well-supported reading
    • Adversarial training as the only defence that has consistently survived scrutiny, at a real cost in clean accuracy; certified defences and their scale limits
    • Gradient masking as the reason a long list of published defences were later broken — a defence that only obscures the gradient does not remove the vulnerability, and the evaluation methodology matters more than the method
  • Distribution shift and spurious correlations
    • Natural distribution shift: the accuracy drops on ImageNet-v2 and ImageNet-R that are not explained by overfitting to the test set
    • Shortcut learning — texture over shape, background over object, the ruler in the melanoma photographs, the hospital marker in the pneumonia radiographs. The model is not failing; it is exploiting a correlation you did not intend to offer
    • Group robustness and worst-group accuracy as the evaluation that reveals this, since average accuracy hides it entirely
    • Domain generalisation methods and the sobering benchmark finding that careful empirical risk minimisation is competitive with most of them
  • Calibration
    • Modern networks are systematically over-confident, and — unlike the classical picture — over-confidence increases with model capacity and with training past convergence
    • Expected calibration error and reliability diagrams; temperature scaling as a one-parameter post-hoc fix that works remarkably well in-distribution
    • Why it fails under shift: temperature scaling fitted on a validation set from the training distribution does not transfer, which is exactly when you needed the confidence
    • Deep ensembles as the strong baseline for uncertainty, and MC dropout as the cheap approximation that underperforms it
    • Out-of-distribution detection, and the finding that networks assign high confidence to inputs far outside the training distribution

Work through

  • Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples — Goodfellow et al., for FGSM and the linear-explanation argument.
  • Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Features — the non-robust-features experiments, which are the most conceptually interesting result in the area.
  • On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks — Guo et al., which established that capacity increases miscalibration and that temperature scaling largely fixes it in-distribution.
  • Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks — the survey that gives the failure mode its name and its taxonomy.
  • Obfuscated Gradients Give a False Sense of Security — the paper that broke seven of eight defences at one conference, and the reason evaluation methodology dominates this subfield.

Practice

Train a classifier, then implement FGSM and PGD and plot accuracy against perturbation budget for both. Transfer your adversarial examples to a differently-architected model and measure the transfer rate. Separately, plot a reliability diagram before and after temperature scaling, then re-evaluate the temperature-scaled model on a shifted version of the test set (corruptions, or a different source). You are done when you can show calibration holding in-distribution and failing under shift.

Where people go wrong

Evaluating a defence against a fixed attack. A defence that reduces attack success by masking or obscuring the gradient looks strong under FGSM and collapses against an adaptive attack designed with knowledge of it — this happened to the great majority of published defences, and the pattern was systematic enough to require a paper of its own. Any robustness claim needs an adaptive evaluation, and the honest default assumption is that a new defence is broken until someone has tried properly to break it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why adversarial examples transfer between models, state what temperature scaling does and the precise condition under which it stops working, and describe an experiment that distinguishes a genuine defence from gradient masking.

M20

Debugging a training run, and when not to use deep learning

≈5 h

Every module so far described how something works when it works. This one is about the far more common situation, and it is the module with the highest immediate return: most time lost in deep learning projects goes to runs that fail silently, and the diagnostic procedure is systematic rather than intuitive. It closes with the question nobody asks often enough, which is whether a neural network was the right choice at all.

Concepts

  • The diagnostic ladder, in order
    • Overfit a single batch first. If the model cannot drive the loss to near zero on two examples, the bug is in the model, the loss or the data pipeline, and no amount of tuning will help
    • Check the initial loss against the theoretical value for a random model — log(num_classes) for cross-entropy. A wrong initial loss localises the bug immediately
    • Verify the data pipeline by eye: look at the actual tensors going in, after augmentation, with their labels
    • Turn off all regularisation, get the model to overfit, then add regularisation back
  • Reading the instruments
    • Loss curves: divergence (learning rate too high, or a numerical bug), plateau at initial loss (dead network, wrong loss reduction, zero gradients), noisy but flat (learning rate too low, or batch too small), train falling while validation rises (genuine overfitting)
    • Gradient norms per layer, which localise vanishing or exploding to a specific block
    • Activation statistics, dead-ReLU fraction, and weight-update ratios — the update-to-weight norm ratio should sit around 10⁻³ and is a fast learning-rate sanity check
    • Loss spikes in large runs, their correlation with specific data batches, and the standard responses: skip the batch, lower the learning rate, or restart from an earlier checkpoint
  • The bugs that do not announce themselves
    • Data leakage between splits, which produces excellent validation metrics and a useless model — the same failure as on the classical page, and just as common here
    • Train/eval mode: forgetting to switch, so dropout and BatchNorm behave wrongly at evaluation
    • Silent broadcasting, which turns a shape error into a wrong-but-runnable computation
    • Not zeroing gradients; label misalignment after a shuffle; augmentation applied to the validation set; a non-deterministic pipeline making results unreproducible
  • Reproducibility and hygiene — seeds, deterministic kernels and the throughput they cost, environment and data versioning, and logging enough to reconstruct a run six months later
  • When not to use deep learning
    • Tabular data of modest size, where the classical page's boosted trees remain the better default
    • Small datasets with no relevant pretrained model, where a simpler model plus good features wins
    • Hard latency, memory or energy budgets; requirements for auditability or a provable guarantee; problems a domain expert can express as a rule
    • The honest heuristic: use deep learning when the input is perceptual or sequential, when a pretrained model exists, or when you have enough data that feature engineering has become the bottleneck

Work through

  • A Recipe for Training Neural Networks — Karpathy's essay, which is the single most useful document on this topic and takes twenty minutes.
  • Deep Learning Tuning Playbook — for the systematic search strategy and for what to do when a run is merely mediocre rather than broken.
  • PyTorch documentation — the reproducibility and profiler guides, plus the common-pitfalls notes.
  • Weights & Biases — experiment tracking; the point is not the product but that a run you cannot reconstruct is a run you cannot debug.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 8, for the practical training-and-diagnosis material in textbook form.

Practice

Take a working training script and break it in six ways, one at a time: forget to zero gradients, leave the model in train mode during evaluation, shuffle labels independently of inputs, apply training augmentation to the validation loader, set the learning rate a hundred times too high, and introduce a silent broadcasting error in the loss. For each, record what the loss curves and gradient norms look like. You are done when you have a personal reference table mapping symptom to cause — which is worth more than any checklist you could read.

Where people go wrong

Tuning hyperparameters before establishing that the model can learn at all. If the pipeline has a bug — misaligned labels, a broken loss, gradients not flowing — then every configuration will perform similarly badly, and a large sweep will find the least-bad one and waste days doing it. Overfitting a single batch takes two minutes and either finds the bug or proves the machinery works. Do it first, every time.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: look at a loss curve and a per-layer gradient-norm plot and produce a ranked list of likely causes — and separately, name three concrete situations in which you would advise against a neural network.

M21

The frontier

≈4 h

A syllabus that ends at settled material teaches a field as of two years ago. This module is the live disagreements — the ones where competent people hold opposite views and the evidence is genuinely incomplete — and the skill it develops is holding a position provisionally with its evidence attached.

Concepts

  • Architecture: is the transformer the end state?
    • State-space and hybrid models from M10 as the strongest challenger; the recall-versus-efficiency trade as the crux
    • The recurring pattern that a general architecture plus scale beats a specialised one, and whether it has a limit
  • Data, which is now the binding constraint rather than compute
    • High-quality text approaching exhaustion; repeated epochs and their diminishing returns; the curation-quality-versus-quantity results
    • Synthetic data and model collapse: training on generated data degrades distribution coverage, with the tail lost first — and the open question of how much real data is needed to prevent it
  • Where the marginal compute should go — pretraining scale versus post-training versus inference-time computation, which is the strategic question the field reoriented around, and which is developed on the language-model page
  • Efficiency as a research direction rather than an afterthought
    • Quantisation-aware training and post-training quantisation; distillation; structured sparsity; the growing gap between training and inference precision
    • Hardware co-design, and the extent to which the architecture landscape is shaped by what matrix multiplication units are fast at
  • The theory questions that would matter if answered — a non-vacuous generalisation bound for a deployed model, a predictive account of feature learning, and a principled way to choose an architecture rather than searching
  • Evaluation, which is quietly the field's weakest link — benchmark saturation and contamination, the gap between benchmark scores and usefulness, and the reproducibility problems M16 documents for RL that apply more widely than anyone likes

Work through

  • The Bitter Lesson — the argument this whole page is downstream of, and the one to be able to state and to criticise.
  • AI Models Collapse When Trained on Recursively Generated Data — the model-collapse result, which sets a real constraint on the synthetic-data answer.
  • Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models — the empirical study of repeated epochs, which is the direct response to data exhaustion.
  • Papers with Code / trending research — a way to sample what the field is currently doing rather than what it did.
  • NeurIPS — the current proceedings, and the Datasets and Benchmarks track in particular for the evaluation question.
  • ICML: International Conference on Machine Learning — the position-paper and theory tracks, where the arguments about evidence get made.
  • ICLR: International Conference on Learning Representations — read a contested paper alongside its public OpenReview thread; the objections are the lesson.

Practice

Pick one disagreement from this module and write a two-page position with the evidence on both sides — not a summary, an argument, with the experiment that would settle it named explicitly. Then find the three most recent papers on it and check whether your position survives. You are done when you can state what would change your mind.

Where people go wrong

Treating benchmark improvements as the field's progress metric. Benchmarks saturate, get contaminated by pretraining corpora, and measure what is easy to measure — and the gap between a leaderboard position and usefulness on a real task is now large enough that several widely reported advances did not transfer at all. When you read a result, ask what was held constant, whether the baseline received the same tuning budget, and whether the evaluation could have been contaminated.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state two live disagreements in this field, give the strongest evidence on each side, and name the experiment that would resolve each — without asserting a conclusion the evidence does not support.

Capstone

Two projects. The first is the integrative one; the second is short and is the honest test of whether M06, M17 and M18 landed.

Project 1 — a language model from scratch, trained deliberately. Not a fine-tune and not a notebook that calls a framework's transformer class.

  • Scope: implement a decoder-only transformer with RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU and pre-norm blocks; write the training loop with AdamW, warmup and cosine decay, gradient clipping and mixed precision; compute the memory budget in advance and verify it against measurement; add gradient checkpointing and confirm the predicted memory/compute trade; train a ladder of at least four model sizes on a fixed corpus and fit a scaling law; use the fit to predict the loss of a fifth, larger model and then train it; and instrument everything — gradient norms per layer, activation statistics, update ratios.
  • Draws on: M03, M04, M05, M09, M11, M12, M20.
  • Done when: your scaling-law extrapolation predicts the held-out model's loss to within a few percent, your memory calculator is within 15% of measurement, and you can show a per-layer gradient-norm plot from a run you deliberately destabilised and then fixed.
  • Stretch: add a mixture-of-experts feed-forward layer with load balancing and report the utilisation histogram; or train the same budget as a state-space hybrid and compare on a recall-heavy task.

Project 2 — three experiments on why it generalises. Small models, sharp questions.

  • Scope: (a) reproduce the random-label result and report epochs to memorisation with and without regularisation; (b) reproduce double descent by sweeping width through the interpolation threshold, and show it flattening with explicit regularisation; (c) train a small transformer to grok modular arithmetic, then find the mechanism in the weights and verify it by ablation.
  • Draws on: M06, M17, M18.
  • Done when: you have three figures and, for each, a paragraph naming which explanation of generalisation it supports and which it rules out.
  • Stretch: train a sparse autoencoder on your grokked model's activations and check whether the recovered features correspond to the mechanism you found by hand.

Mastery check

  • You can implement reverse-mode autodiff from scratch and explain its cost asymmetry against forward mode.
  • You can derive He initialisation and say why ReLU needs the factor of two.
  • You can explain what a residual connection does to the backward pass, in terms of the identity path's Jacobian.
  • You can state why L2 regularisation and weight decay differ under Adam.
  • You can state the random-label result and name two explanations of generalisation it rules out.
  • You can write multi-head causal attention from memory, including the √d scaling and why it is there.
  • You can explain why RoPE extrapolates better than learned absolute position embeddings.
  • You can explain why FlashAttention is faster while computing exactly the same function.
  • You can compute a training run's memory budget and pick a parallelism strategy from cluster topology.
  • You can derive compute-optimal N and D from a compute budget and say why production runs go past it.
  • You can say why contrastive learning needs large batches and masked autoencoding does not.
  • You can state the 1-WL bound on message-passing GNNs and give a pair of graphs they cannot distinguish.
  • You can name the deadly triad and point at DQN's mitigation for each component.
  • You can say what the NTK explains and give the empirical fact showing real networks are outside that regime.
  • You can explain superposition and why it makes single neurons the wrong unit of analysis.
  • You can distinguish a real adversarial defence from gradient masking by the evaluation used.
  • You can diagnose a broken training run from loss curves and gradient norms alone.
  • You can name three situations where you would advise against deep learning.

  • Generative AI Beyond Language — the other continuation. VAEs, GANs, score matching, diffusion and flow matching, applied to images, audio, video, 3D and molecules. It assumes M01–M11 of this page and picks up at the objective.
  • Classical Machine Learning — the prerequisite, and the place to go when the answer is "do not use a neural network". Its validation, calibration and causal modules apply here unchanged.

  • Information Theory — cross-entropy loss is a codelength, the ELBO is a compression bound, and the scaling laws in M12 have an information-theoretic reading. That page develops the framework these facts come from.
  • Numerical computing and accelerator programming — M11 stopped at the abstractions. CUDA, Triton, kernel fusion, memory hierarchies and the arithmetic-intensity analysis that made FlashAttention possible are the layer beneath.
  • Optimisation theory — M04 used the methods without proving anything. Non-convex optimisation, stochastic approximation, and the analysis of adaptive methods are the underlying discipline, and it is where the edge-of-stability results are being worked out.

Resources

Numbered so the syllabus can point at them. Core marks the ones worth buying or blocking out real time for.

Books

Prince (R-01) is the spine — free, current, and the best figures in the field. Goodfellow (R-02) is now dated on architectures and still the best treatment of the mathematical foundations. Everything else is a specialist reference.

  1. R-01 Understanding Deep Learning — Simon J. D. Prince, 2023

    Free from the author, current, and the best-illustrated textbook in the field — the figures on piecewise-linear regions, on residual paths and on attention are worth the download by themselves. Chapters 3–7 for the foundations, 10–12 for architectures, 20 for the honest account of what remains unexplained. This is the one to read cover to cover.

    Core Free online Intermediate ~540 pp
    Used in M01 M02 M03 M04 M05 M06 M07 M09 M13 M17 M20
  2. R-02 Deep Learning — Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio & Aaron Courville, 2016

    Free online, and comprehensively out of date on architectures — it predates the transformer entirely. Read Part I (linear algebra, probability, information theory) and Chapters 6 and 8, which remain the best treatment of backpropagation over computation graphs and of why deep optimisation is hard. Ignore Part III unless you want the historical view of generative models.

    Core Free online Advanced ~780 pp
    Used in M01 M03 M04
  3. R-03 Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts — Christopher M. Bishop & Hugh Bishop, 2024

    The successor to Bishop's classic pattern-recognition text, rebuilt around deep learning and unusually strong on the probabilistic framing — normalisation, regularisation and the generative chapters are all treated as one family rather than as tricks. Use it as the second explanation when R-01 compresses something. (The publisher site answers 202 to automated checks and opens normally in a browser.)

    Free, interactive, and every concept arrives with runnable code in PyTorch, JAX and TensorFlow. Papers & primary sources

Deep learning's literature is arXiv-first and moves fast, which makes provenance discipline important. Every arXiv id below was confirmed by title search against the API and every DOI through Crossref — a first pass from memory produced two wrong papers, which is the usual hit rate.

  1. R-11 Deep Learning (Nature review) — Yann LeCun, Yoshua Bengio & Geoffrey Hinton, Nature, 2015

    The field's own account of what happened, written by three of the people it happened to, at the moment it became mainstream. Useful for M01 as a statement of what the participants thought the contribution was — and readable in an hour. (Nature answers 403 to automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Beginner ~9 pp
    Used in M01
  2. R-12 ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks — Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever & Geoffrey E. Hinton, NeurIPS 2012 / CACM 2017

    AlexNet. Read section 3 and count how many of its components were new — the answer is close to none, which is M01's argument in the primary source. The GPU-implementation details are also a useful reminder that this was a systems result. (ACM 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Free Intermediate ~9 pp
    Used in M01
  3. R-13 Approximation by Superpositions of a Sigmoidal Function — George Cybenko, Mathematics of Control, Signals and Systems, 1989

    The universal approximation theorem in the original. Worth reading precisely because it is so much narrower than its reputation: an existence result about a single hidden layer of unbounded width, silent on depth, learnability and generalisation. (Springer 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Advanced ~12 pp
    Used in M02
  4. R-14 Learning Representations by Back-Propagating Errors — David E. Rumelhart, Geoffrey E. Hinton & Ronald J. Williams, Nature, 1986

    Two pages that popularised backpropagation. Read it for what the authors thought was interesting — that the hidden units learn useful internal representations, which is the representation-learning claim stated twenty-six years before it worked at scale. (Nature 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Intermediate ~2 pp
    Used in M03
  5. R-15 Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization — Diederik P. Kingma & Jimmy Ba, 2014

    The default optimiser, and simpler than its ubiquity suggests — two exponential moving averages and a bias correction. Read section 2 and section 3's convergence discussion, noting that the original proof had a flaw later corrected in the literature, which is itself a useful lesson about this field's standards of evidence.

    Core Free Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M04
  6. R-16 Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization — Ilya Loshchilov & Frank Hutter, 2017

    AdamW, and the demonstration that L2 regularisation and weight decay are not equivalent under adaptive optimisers — a discrepancy that sat in every major framework for years. Short, and the clearest available example of an implementation detail being a research contribution.

    Core Free Intermediate ~19 pp
    Used in M04
  7. R-17 Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition — Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren & Jian Sun, 2015

    The most consequential architectural idea before the transformer, and one of the most-cited papers in computing. Read section 3.1 for the degradation problem — deeper plain networks had higher training error, which correctly identifies this as an optimisation failure rather than overfitting, and is what makes the fix intelligible.

    Core Free Intermediate ~12 pp
    Used in M05 M07
  8. R-18 Batch Normalization: Accelerating Deep Network Training by Reducing Internal Covariate Shift — Sergey Ioffe & Christian Szegedy, 2015

    Read it knowing the title's explanation is disputed — later work injected deliberate covariate shift after the normalisation layer with no performance cost. The mechanism is still argued about; the empirical effect is not. A good case study in the field's tolerance for working methods with wrong stories attached.

    Core Free Intermediate ~11 pp
    Used in M05
  9. R-19 Layer Normalization — Jimmy Lei Ba, Jamie Ryan Kiros & Geoffrey E. Hinton, 2016

    Short, and the variant every transformer uses. The argument that matters is the removal of the batch dependence — no running statistics, no train/eval discrepancy, and no problem with variable-length sequences or tiny per-device batches.

    Free Intermediate ~14 pp
    Used in M05
  10. R-20 Understanding Deep Learning Requires Rethinking Generalization — Chiyuan Zhang, Samy Bengio, Moritz Hardt, Benjamin Recht & Oriol Vinyals, 2016

    Standard networks fit randomly permuted labels to zero training error in about the same number of epochs as real labels. Short, decisive, and it rules out any explanation of generalisation that rests on the hypothesis class being too small to fit noise — which is what the classical bounds provide. The single most important paper on this page.

    Core Free Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M06
  11. R-21 Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting — Nitish Srivastava, Geoffrey Hinton, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever & Ruslan Salakhutdinov, JMLR, 2014

    Open access from JMLR. The ensemble reading — training a network with dropout approximates averaging exponentially many thinned subnetworks — is the part worth taking away, and it explains why the test-time rescaling is what it is. Largely displaced in very large models, still standard at smaller scales.

    Free Intermediate ~30 pp
    Used in M06
  12. R-22 Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets — Alethea Power, Yuri Burda, Harri Edwards, Igor Babuschkin & Vedant Misra, 2022

    Networks that reach perfect training accuracy and chance validation accuracy, then generalise abruptly thousands of steps later with no change in training loss. It decouples fitting from generalising in time, which no capacity-based account can produce, and it has become the standard testbed for mechanistic interpretability.

    Free Intermediate ~10 pp
    Used in M06
  13. R-23 Gradient-Based Learning Applied to Document Recognition — Yann LeCun, Léon Bottou, Yoshua Bengio & Patrick Haffner, Proceedings of the IEEE, 1998

    LeNet-5, and the case for convolutions argued from first principles — local receptive fields, weight sharing and subsampling as responses to the structure of images. Long; read sections I–III. The graph-transformer-network material at the end is historical. (IEEE Xplore answers 202; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Intermediate ~46 pp
    Used in M07
  14. R-24 Long Short-Term Memory — Sepp Hochreiter & Jürgen Schmidhuber, Neural Computation, 1997

    Read section 3 for the constant error carousel — an additive path through time with gated writes, which is structurally the same idea as a residual connection and arrives eighteen years earlier. The vanishing-gradient analysis that motivates it is the part most retellings omit. (MIT Press 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Advanced ~32 pp
    Used in M08
  15. R-25 Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate — Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho & Yoshua Bengio, 2014

    Where attention comes from. Read section 3 and note how modest the modification is — a weighted sum over encoder states instead of a single final vector — and then look at Figure 2, which shows performance no longer degrading with source length. That figure is the entire justification.

    Core Free Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M08
  16. R-26 Attention Is All You Need — Ashish Vaswani et al., 2017

    The paper the field runs on. It is terse and assumes the sequence-to-sequence context, so read it after implementing rather than before. Section 3.2.1 has the √d justification and section 4 the argument about path length between positions, which is the real case for attention over recurrence.

    Core Free Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M09
  17. R-27 FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness — Tri Dao, Daniel Y. Fu, Stefano Ermon, Atri Rudra & Christopher Ré, 2022

    The IO-complexity analysis in section 3 is the transferable content: attention is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, so tiling to avoid materialising the n × n matrix wins without changing the mathematics. It delivered more speedup than years of approximate-attention research, which is a lesson about where to look.

    Core Free Advanced ~34 pp
    Used in M10
  18. R-28 Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces — Albert Gu & Tri Dao, 2023

    The selectivity mechanism — making state-space parameters input-dependent — and the hardware-aware scan that makes it fast. Read it for the honest statement of the trade: linear scaling and constant-memory inference, against a fixed-size state that cannot do exact long-range recall the way attention can.

    Free Advanced ~36 pp
    Used in M10
  19. R-29 Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer — Noam Shazeer et al., 2017

    The modern MoE layer, and — more usefully — the load-balancing losses that stop the router collapsing onto a few experts. That failure mode is the whole engineering difficulty of MoE, and this is where it was first characterised and addressed.

    Free Advanced ~19 pp
    Used in M10
  20. R-30 Efficient Transformers: A Survey — Yi Tay, Mostafa Dehghani, Dara Bahri & Donald Metzler, 2020

    A taxonomy of the approximate-attention literature — fixed patterns, learnable patterns, low-rank, kernel, recurrence. Read it as a map so you can place any new proposal, not as a menu: most of these lost to fused exact attention, and the survey predates that.

    Free Advanced ~28 pp
    Used in M10
  21. R-31 ZeRO: Memory Optimizations Toward Training Trillion Parameter Models — Samyam Rajbhandari, Jeff Rasley, Olatunji Ruwase & Yuxiong He, 2019

    Section 3's memory analysis is the clearest itemisation anywhere of where training memory actually goes — parameters, gradients, optimiser state, and the fp32 master copies — and the three sharding stages follow directly from it. Read it before sizing any run.

    Core Free Advanced ~24 pp
    Used in M11
  22. R-32 Mixed Precision Training — Paulius Micikevicius et al., 2017

    Loss scaling, fp32 master weights, and the list of operations that must stay in higher precision. bf16 has since removed the need for loss scaling on most hardware, but the analysis of why certain reductions and normalisations overflow or underflow is still exactly right.

    Free Advanced ~12 pp
    Used in M11
  23. R-33 GPipe: Efficient Training of Giant Neural Networks Using Pipeline Parallelism — Yanping Huang et al., 2018

    Pipeline parallelism and the bubble analysis — how much of each device's time is idle waiting for the stage before it, and how micro-batching reduces it. The bubble fraction is a quantity you should be able to compute for a proposed configuration, and this is where the formula comes from.

    Free Advanced ~11 pp
    Used in M11
  24. R-34 Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models — Jared Kaplan et al., 2020

    The original scaling-law paper, and the reason large runs are planned rather than attempted. Read it knowing its allocation conclusion — scale parameters faster than data — was later corrected by Chinchilla, and that the reason was a methodological detail about learning-rate schedules rather than a conceptual error.

    Core Free Intermediate ~30 pp
    Used in M12
  25. R-35 Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Jordan Hoffmann et al., 2022

    Chinchilla. Three independent estimation approaches converging on the same allocation — parameters and tokens should scale roughly in proportion — plus the concrete demonstration that a 70B model beat a 280B one on equal compute. This is the paper that changed how everyone budgets a run.

    Core Free Intermediate ~36 pp
    Used in M12
  26. R-36 Deep Learning Scaling Is Predictable, Empirically — Joel Hestness et al., 2017

    The Baidu paper that found power-law scaling across machine translation, language modelling, image classification and speech — three years before the finding became famous. Worth reading for the breadth of domains, which is the evidence that scaling laws are a property of the setup rather than of language.

    Free Intermediate ~19 pp
    Used in M12
  27. R-37 Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage? — Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda & Sanmi Koyejo, 2023

    The argument that many reported emergent abilities are artefacts of discontinuous metrics — exact-match accuracy on a multi-step task produces a sharp jump where a continuous metric on the same model outputs shows smooth improvement. The necessary counterweight to the emergence literature, and a general lesson about metric choice.

    Core Free Intermediate ~28 pp
    Used in M12
  28. R-38 A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations — Ting Chen, Simon Kornblith, Mohammad Norouzi & Geoffrey Hinton, 2020

    SimCLR. The augmentation ablation in section 3 is the substance — random crop plus colour jitter carries almost all the benefit, and the composition matters more than any individual transform. Also the clearest statement of the large-batch requirement that constrains contrastive methods in practice.

    Core Free Intermediate ~20 pp
    Used in M13
  29. R-39 Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners — Kaiming He, Xinlei Chen, Saining Xie, Yanghao Li, Piotr Dollár & Ross Girshick, 2021

    The asymmetric encoder–decoder and the 75% masking ratio, with the argument for why images need a far higher ratio than text: pixels are locally redundant in a way tokens are not. Simpler engineering than contrastive learning — no negatives, no large batches, no augmentation policy to tune.

    Core Free Intermediate ~13 pp
    Used in M13
  30. R-40 BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding — Jacob Devlin, Ming-Wei Chang, Kenton Lee & Kristina Toutanova, 2018

    Masked language modelling, and the paper that established pretrain-then-fine-tune as the default in NLP. Read section 3 for the objective and the 15% masking scheme, including the 80/10/10 corruption strategy that exists to reduce the train/test mismatch introduced by the mask token.

    Core Free Intermediate ~16 pp
    Used in M13
  31. R-41 Bootstrap Your Own Latent — Jean-Bastien Grill et al., 2020

    Self-supervised learning with no negative pairs at all, avoiding representational collapse through an asymmetric predictor and a stop-gradient. The result was surprising when published and the mechanism is still not fully explained, which makes it a good demonstration of how much of this field is empirical.

    Free Advanced ~35 pp
    Used in M13
  32. R-42 An Image Is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale — Alexey Dosovitskiy et al., 2020

    The vision transformer. The contribution is not the architecture — patches into a standard encoder — but Figure 4: ViT underperforms a ResNet at ImageNet scale and overtakes it given hundreds of millions of images. The convolutional prior is a substitute for data, quantified.

    Core Free Intermediate ~22 pp
    Used in M14
  33. R-43 Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision — Alec Radford et al., 2021

    CLIP. Long; read sections 2–3 for the contrastive objective and the zero-shot construction, and then the limitations section in full — it is unusually candid about compositional failure, counting, and the dependence on pretraining-data coverage. Its embedding space is the conditioning mechanism for text-to-image models on the sibling page.

    Core Free Intermediate ~48 pp
    Used in M14
  34. R-44 A ConvNet for the 2020s — Zhuang Liu, Hanzi Mao, Chao-Yuan Wu, Christoph Feichtenhofer, Trevor Darrell & Saining Xie, 2022

    ConvNeXt: take a ResNet and apply transformer-era training recipes and design choices one at a time, measuring after each. It recovers vision-transformer performance, which makes it the controlled dismantling of the architecture-versus-recipe confound. Read the step-by-step table; it is the whole paper.

    Core Free Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M14
  35. R-45 Flamingo: a Visual Language Model for Few-Shot Learning — Jean-Baptiste Alayrac et al., 2022

    The frozen-backbone pattern that now dominates vision-language modelling: keep a strong image encoder and a strong language model fixed, train a small connector between them. The perceiver resampler and gated cross-attention are the specific mechanisms, and the design rationale generalises to audio and video.

    Free Advanced ~66 pp
    Used in M14
  36. R-46 How Powerful Are Graph Neural Networks? — Keyulu Xu, Weihua Hu, Jure Leskovec & Stefanie Jegelka, 2018

    The 1-Weisfeiler–Lehman upper bound on message-passing GNNs, with a followable proof, plus GIN as the architecture that attains it. A rare thing in deep learning: a sharp, provable statement about what an architecture cannot do, with concrete consequences for molecular modelling.

    Core Free Advanced ~17 pp
    Used in M15
  37. R-47 Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks — Thomas N. Kipf & Max Welling, 2016

    The GCN, derived as a first-order approximation to spectral graph convolution and then simplified into something you can implement in ten lines. The paper that made graph neural networks accessible, and still the right starting point before the more expressive variants.

    Free Intermediate ~14 pp
    Used in M15
  38. R-48 Geometric Deep Learning — Michael M. Bronstein, Joan Bruna, Taco Cohen & Petar Veličković, 2021

    The blueprint paper, which derives convolutions, attention and message passing as consequences of assuming different symmetry groups. It is long and it is the most intellectually satisfying framing of architecture design available — read the first three chapters even if you skip the rest.

    Free Advanced ~156 pp
    Used in M15
  39. R-49 Human-Level Control Through Deep Reinforcement Learning — Volodymyr Mnih et al., Nature, 2015

    DQN. Read the methods section for the replay buffer and the target network — both are direct mitigations of the deadly triad, and understanding them that way rather than as tricks is what makes the rest of deep RL legible. (Nature 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Advanced ~13 pp
    Used in M16
  40. R-50 Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms — John Schulman, Filip Wolski, Prafulla Dhariwal, Alec Radford & Oleg Klimov, 2017

    Short, and the algorithm you will meet again as the optimiser inside RLHF on the language-model page. The clipped surrogate objective is a practical approximation to a trust region; read section 3, and note that later analyses attribute much of PPO's advantage to code-level details rather than to the clip itself.

    Core Free Advanced ~12 pp
    Used in M16
  41. R-51 Deep Reinforcement Learning That Matters — Peter Henderson et al., 2017

    Results varying by more than the claimed improvement across random seeds, implementation details outweighing algorithmic differences, and reported curves selected from the best runs. Read it before trusting any deep-RL result, including your own — and note that its methodological criticisms apply well beyond RL.

    Core Free Intermediate ~10 pp
    Used in M16
  42. R-52 Neural Tangent Kernel: Convergence and Generalization in Neural Networks — Arthur Jacot, Franck Gabriel & Clément Hongler, 2018

    In the infinite-width limit, gradient-descent training is kernel regression with a fixed kernel. Dense — read the setup and the main theorem, skip the proofs first time. Its importance is as much in its limits as its content: real networks learn features, and the NTK regime by construction does not.

    Free Advanced ~10 pp
    Used in M17
  43. R-53 The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis: Finding Sparse, Trainable Neural Networks — Jonathan Frankle & Michael Carbin, 2018

    A dense network contains a sparse subnetwork that trains to the same accuracy from the same initialisation. Read it for the experimental design: the random-reinitialisation control is what makes the claim meaningful, and without it the result would be unremarkable.

    Core Free Advanced ~42 pp
    Used in M17
  44. R-54 The Implicit Bias of Gradient Descent on Separable Data — Daniel Soudry, Elad Hoffer, Mor Shpigel Nacson, Suriya Gunasekar & Nathan Srebro, 2017

    The clean case: gradient descent on separable data with logistic loss converges in direction to the maximum-margin solution, with no explicit regularisation. This is what implicit bias looks like when it can be proved, and it is the template for what a deep version would have to establish.

    Free Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M17
  45. R-55 Toy Models of Superposition — Nelson Elhage et al., Anthropic, 2022

    Superposition demonstrated in models small enough to understand exactly: networks store more features than they have dimensions by using non-orthogonal directions, which is possible because features are sparse. The geometric structures features arrange themselves into are a genuinely surprising empirical finding.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M18
  46. R-56 In-Context Learning and Induction Heads — Catherine Olsson et al., Anthropic, 2022

    The strongest available link between a specific identified circuit and a macroscopic capability: induction heads form during a visible phase change in training, and that phase change coincides with the emergence of in-context learning. Six independent lines of evidence, laid out separately.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M18
  47. R-57 Sparse Autoencoders Find Highly Interpretable Features in Language Models — Hoagy Cunningham, Aidan Ewart, Logan Riggs, Robert Huben & Lee Sharkey, 2023

    Train an overcomplete autoencoder with an L1 penalty on a model's activations to recover a sparse, more monosemantic feature basis. The method that made feature extraction practical at scale, and the current main tool of the interpretability programme — read it knowing that whether these features are the right decomposition is still open.

    Free Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M18
  48. R-58 Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples — Ian J. Goodfellow, Jonathon Shlens & Christian Szegedy, 2014

    FGSM, and the linear-explanation argument: adversarial vulnerability arises from the model being too linear in high dimensions, not too nonlinear. That framing was counter-intuitive and has largely held up, and the attack itself is four lines of code.

    Core Free Intermediate ~11 pp
    Used in M19
  49. R-59 Adversarial Examples Are Not Bugs, They Are Features — Andrew Ilyas et al., 2019

    The most conceptually interesting result in adversarial machine learning: the perturbations correspond to genuinely predictive but non-robust features present in the data. The experiments constructing datasets from only robust or only non-robust features are the evidence, and they are worth studying as experimental design.

    Core Free Advanced ~36 pp
    Used in M19
  50. R-60 On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks — Chuan Guo, Geoff Pleiss, Yu Sun & Kilian Q. Weinberger, 2017

    Modern networks are systematically over-confident, and — contrary to the classical picture — miscalibration increases with capacity. Temperature scaling, a single parameter fitted on validation data, largely fixes it in-distribution. Read the discussion of why it fails under shift, which is when you needed it.

    Core Free Intermediate ~14 pp
    Used in M19
  51. R-61 Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks — Robert Geirhos et al., 2020

    The survey that named the failure mode and gave it a taxonomy: models exploiting correlations that hold in the training data and not in the world. The catalogue of real examples — texture over shape, the ruler in melanoma photographs, the hospital token in radiographs — is the part that changes how you build datasets.

    Core Free Intermediate ~28 pp
    Used in M19
  52. R-62 Obfuscated Gradients Give a False Sense of Security — Anish Athalye, Nicholas Carlini & David Wagner, 2018

    Seven of eight defences at one conference broken by adaptive attacks, with a diagnostic checklist for spotting gradient masking. The reason robustness claims require adaptive evaluation, and the reason the sensible default assumption about a new defence is that it does not work.

    Core Free Advanced ~12 pp
    Used in M19
  53. R-63 AI Models Collapse When Trained on Recursively Generated Data — Ilia Shumailov et al., Nature, 2024

    Training successive generations on generated data degrades distribution coverage, with the tails disappearing first — demonstrated across language models, VAEs and Gaussian mixtures, so it is a property of the recursion rather than of one architecture. The real constraint on synthetic data as an answer to data exhaustion. (Nature 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Intermediate ~8 pp
    Used in M21
  54. R-64 Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models — Niklas Muennighoff et al., 2023

    What happens when you run out of unique data: repeated epochs help for roughly four passes, then decay toward no benefit. It extends the Chinchilla laws to the data-constrained regime, which is the regime frontier training is now in, and gives the quantitative answer rather than a rule of thumb.

    Free Advanced ~48 pp
    Used in M21

Articles, essays & blogs

Several of the best explanations in this field were never published as papers. These are the ones that changed how people think rather than what they knew.

  1. R-71 The Illustrated Transformer — Jay Alammar, 2018

    The standard first explanation of the transformer, and deservedly so — it walks a single token through the whole stack with a diagram at every step. Read it before the paper, then implement, then read the paper. The companion illustrated posts on BERT and on GPT-2 are equally good.

    Core Free Beginner ~30 min
    Used in M09
  2. R-72 The Annotated Transformer — Harvard NLP, updated 2022

    The paper's text interleaved with a complete working implementation, line by line. The right thing to read immediately after R-71 and immediately before writing your own — and the place to check when your implementation disagrees with the reference and you cannot see why.

    Core Free Intermediate
    Used in M09
  3. R-73 Understanding LSTM Networks — Christopher Olah, 2015

    The diagrams everyone uses. It builds the LSTM cell gate by gate, motivating each from what the previous one cannot do, and it is the reason the cell-state-as-conveyor-belt mental model is universal. Ten minutes, and it makes M08 straightforward.

    Core Free Beginner ~15 min
    Used in M08
  4. R-74 A Recipe for Training Neural Networks — Andrej Karpathy, 2019

    Twenty minutes, and the most useful document on this page for anyone who has a run that is not working. Its central instruction — become one with the data, then overfit a single batch before anything else — is the discipline that separates a debugging session from a hyperparameter sweep.

    Core Free Intermediate ~20 min
    Used in M20
  5. R-75 A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits — Nelson Elhage et al., Anthropic, 2021

    The document that founded mechanistic interpretability, and the source of the residual-stream framing — every block reads from and writes to a shared channel — which is the most useful way to think about the transformer even if you never do interpretability. Read the QK/OV decomposition carefully; it is the analytical tool everything else uses.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M09 M18
  6. R-76 Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits — Chris Olah et al., Distill, 2020

    Where the circuits programme started, in vision models, with curve detectors traced neuron by neuron and verified by intervention. Still the clearest statement of the three claims the whole field rests on — features are the units, circuits are the computation, and universality across models — and of what evidence each would need.

    Core Free Intermediate
    Used in M18
  7. R-77 A Gentle Introduction to Graph Neural Networks — Benjamin Sanchez-Lengeling, Emily Reif, Adam Pearce & Alexander B. Wiltschko, Distill, 2021

    Interactive, and the fastest route to the message-passing mental model — you can vary the aggregator and the depth and watch the representations change. Do this before the papers in M15; the formalism is much easier once you have manipulated it.

    Free Beginner ~40 min
    Used in M15
  8. R-78 The Annotated S4 — Sasha Rush & Sidd Karamcheti, 2022

    The structured state-space model implemented line by line in JAX with the derivations inline. State-space parameterisations are genuinely hard to follow from the papers, and this is the only treatment that makes the HiPPO matrix and the convolutional-versus-recurrent duality concrete.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M10
  9. R-79 The Bitter Lesson — Richard S. Sutton, 2019

    One page, and the argument this entire page is downstream of: methods that scale with computation beat methods that encode human knowledge. Be able to state it and to criticise it — its examples are all perception or evaluation problems with cheap simulators or abundant data, which is a real limit on its scope.

    Core Free Beginner ~1 p
    Used in M21
  10. R-80 How to Scale Your Model — Jacob Austin et al., Google DeepMind, 2025

    A systems-level treatment of exactly M11's arithmetic — rooflines, collectives, sharding strategies and how to reason about where a large run's time actually goes — worked through rather than asserted. TPU-centric in its examples and general in its reasoning. The best single document on this subject.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M11
  11. R-81 The Ultra-Scale Playbook — Hugging Face, 2025

    Distributed training from one GPU to thousands, with runnable configurations for each parallelism strategy and measured throughput for each. The practical counterpart to R-80: that one explains the reasoning, this one shows what the code and the numbers look like.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M11
