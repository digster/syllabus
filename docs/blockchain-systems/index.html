What this topic is

A blockchain is a replicated state machine whose replicas are run by mutually distrusting parties, any of whom may profit by lying. Everything distinctive about the field follows from that one constraint. Classical distributed systems assume a known, fixed set of participants and a fault model where nodes crash; a permissionless chain has neither, so it has to buy agreement with something scarce — hash power, staked capital — and then defend that purchase against an adversary with a budget. The result is a discipline that sits across cryptography, distributed consensus, programming-language design and mechanism design, and does not fully belong to any of them.

Three ideas carry most of the weight. Hash-linked data structures make history tamper-evident, so disagreement about the past becomes detectable rather than arguable. Sybil-resistant leader election — proof of work, then proof of stake — makes agreement about the present possible without an identity registry. And deterministic, metered execution makes a shared computer possible at all, because every node must reach byte-identical state from the same inputs or the network forks. This page teaches the protocol layer: how those mechanisms work, what they provably cannot do, and where the real systems deviate from the papers. The economic layer built on top — automated market makers, lending, stablecoins, NFTs, DAOs — is a separate syllabus at DeFi, NFTs and On-Chain Economics.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes you can program in at least one systems or scripting language, that you have met big-O notation and basic probability, and that you have some idea what a hash function and a public key are. It does not assume a cryptography course, a distributed systems course, or any prior contact with cryptocurrency.

  • No cryptography background — patch it with the first six chapters of A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography
  • No distributed systems background — patch it with Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming, chapters 1–3
  • Never written a smart contract — patch it with Cyfrin Updraft before M10
  • No prior exposure to the ecosystem at all — read Bitcoin's Academic Pedigree first; it is nine pages and it will save you from thinking any of this was invented in 2008
  • No sense of why institutions care about any of this — patch it with 15.S12 Blockchain and Money, lectures 1–4

Not covered here: trading, price analysis, portfolio construction or anything about whether to buy a token; token launch and marketing mechanics; enterprise and permissioned ledgers beyond the one module that explains why they usually should not be blockchains at all (M06); and the application-layer economics of DeFi, NFTs and DAOs, which live on the sibling page rather than being thinned into a section here.

How to use this

  • Working knowledge (≈70 h): M01–M04, M06–M07, M09–M10, M12–M13. That is enough to read a protocol spec, write a contract that will not be drained on day one, and understand what a rollup actually does. Skip M05, M15, M17–M20 on this route and do the M12 exercise as your project.
  • Full course (≈205 h): every module in order, every checkpoint answered out loud before moving on. The order is a dependency order, not a difficulty order — M06 is harder than M09 and comes first anyway, because M07 is unreadable without it.
  • Application developer who wants the layer below: start at M09, then M12, M13, M14, M16. Use M02, M04 and M07 as reference when a term you do not know appears.
  • Distributed systems background already: skim M06, read M04 carefully — the interesting part is what Nakamoto consensus gives up relative to PBFT and why anyone accepted that trade — then go to M07, M13 and M16.
  • Cryptography background already: M02 is revision; M15 is not, because the interesting content is proof-system engineering economics rather than the primitives. Start at M03.

What you'll be able to do

  • Derive why the longest-chain rule converges, state the three Bitcoin Backbone properties, and explain precisely what "six confirmations" buys you against an attacker with a given hash-rate share.
  • Explain why FLP does not stop Bitcoin or Raft from shipping, and name exactly what each of them gives up to get around it.
  • Trace a transaction from wallet signature through mempool, block proposal, execution and finalisation, naming which component can censor it at each hop.
  • Read raw EVM bytecode in a debugger, compute a contract's storage slot for a given mapping key, and say what gas is actually paying for.
  • Find reentrancy, access-control and oracle-manipulation bugs in a contract by reading it, and write an invariant fuzz test that catches the class rather than the instance.
  • Explain what an optimistic rollup's seven-day challenge window is protecting against, and why a ZK rollup does not need one.
  • Say what a blob is, why EIP-4844 priced it separately from calldata, and what data-availability sampling proves versus what it assumes.
  • Compare Solana's account-declaration model, Aptos's Block-STM and Sui's object model as three different answers to the same parallel-execution question, and say when each degrades.
  • Evaluate a bridge's trust model in one paragraph and place it correctly on the light-client-to-multisig spectrum.
  • Read an EIP, follow the All Core Devs discussion around it, and predict whether it will ship.

Syllabus

M01

Digital cash before Bitcoin, and the problem none of it solved

≈5 h

Bitcoin is usually taught as an origin, which makes every design choice in it look arbitrary. It is better taught as an answer to a twenty-year-old question that several very good cryptographers had already failed to answer. Start here and M04 arrives as a result you can evaluate rather than a fact you have to accept — and you acquire the habit, useful for the whole page, of asking what specifically a mechanism is defending against.

Concepts

  • The double-spend problem stated precisely — a bearer digital token is a bitstring, bitstrings copy for free, so possession cannot imply exclusivity without a referee
    • Why the obvious fix (a trusted server holding the ledger) is not a bad answer, and remains the right answer for most systems. The interesting question is what changes when you refuse to have that server
    • Chaum's DigiCash and blind signatures: solved payer privacy against the bank while keeping the bank. Understanding what it did and did not solve isolates the actual gap
  • Proof of work as a rate limiter, invented for something else entirely
    • Dwork and Naor's 1992 pricing function for spam, then Back's hashcash — a partial hash preimage as a stamp. Note that in both, the work is priced per-message and never accumulates into a shared history
    • The move Nakamoto makes that hashcash does not: chaining the work so that the cost of rewriting history grows with its depth
  • Dai's b-money and Szabo's bit gold as the two closest near-misses
    • Both propose broadcast, both propose proof of work, both leave the identity of the ledger-maintaining set as an assumption. Locate the exact sentence where each one assumes away Sybil resistance
    • Szabo's earlier work on smart contracts (1996) is where the vending-machine analogy and the phrase itself come from, two decades before anything could run one
  • Byzantine agreement as the underlying question, and why the permissionless setting is not the classical one
    • Classical BFT assumes a known participant set of size n with at most f faults. "Permissionless" means n is unknown and an attacker can inflate their share of it for free — the Sybil attack, named by Douceur in 2002
    • Proof of work is best understood not as a consensus algorithm but as a Sybil-resistance mechanism that makes voting weight expensive; the consensus rule is the separate longest-chain rule on top

Work through

  • Bitcoin's Academic Pedigree — the whole thing. It is the single best map of which parts of Bitcoin were new and which were assembled from existing literature.
  • Hashcash — sections 1–3. Note the absence of any chaining.
  • b-money — the whole proposal is two pages. Find the Sybil assumption.
  • Bit Gold — read alongside b-money and list the differences.
  • Smart Contracts: Building Blocks for Digital Free Markets — Szabo's framing of contracts as protocol rather than prose.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapter 1 and the "History of cryptocurrencies" section of chapter 10.
  • 15.S12 Blockchain and Money — lectures 1–4, for the institutional framing of what problem anyone thought this was solving.

Practice

Implement hashcash in twenty lines: find a nonce such that SHA-256 of (message ‖ nonce) has k leading zero bits. Measure wall-clock time for k = 16, 20, 24 and confirm it roughly doubles per bit. Then answer in writing: what would you have to add to this to make a shared ledger out of it? You should end up reinventing chaining and a fork-choice rule. Done when your write-up names both.

Where people go wrong

Believing that proof of work is the consensus mechanism. It is not — it is a Sybil-resistance mechanism that assigns voting weight in proportion to a cost. The consensus mechanism is the fork-choice rule ("follow the chain with the most accumulated work") plus the validity rules. Conflating them makes proof of stake look like a different consensus algorithm, when it is mostly a different answer to the same Sybil question with a genuinely different fork-choice rule bolted on. Keeping the two layers separate in your head is what makes M04 and M07 comparable.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state in one sentence what b-money and bit gold each left unspecified, and explain why a system that solves double-spending with a trusted server is not "cheating" — it is the correct engineering answer under a trust assumption that most applications actually have.

M02

Cryptographic primitives, at the level a protocol engineer needs them

≈10 h

Every later module leans on this one, and the leaning is load-bearing rather than decorative: Merkle proofs are why light clients exist, signature malleability is why SegWit exists, and elliptic-curve group structure is why KZG commitments in M14 work at all. You do not need to be able to prove these primitives secure. You do need to know exactly what each one guarantees, what it does not, and what breaks when the guarantee is misread.

Concepts

  • Cryptographic hash functions and the three properties, kept distinct
    • Preimage, second-preimage and collision resistance are different, and protocols depend on different ones. Birthday bound: collision resistance is only n/2 bits, which is why 256-bit hashes give 128-bit collision security
    • Length-extension on Merkle–Damgård constructions, and why Bitcoin's double-SHA256 was an over-cautious defence against it. Keccak/SHA-3's sponge construction does not have the problem
  • Merkle trees and the inclusion proof
    • Why an inclusion proof is O(log n) and what it does not prove: that a transaction is absent, that the tree is well-formed, or that the root is the right root. Absence needs a sorted tree or a different structure — this is why Ethereum uses a Merkle Patricia trie, covered in M09
    • Bitcoin's Merkle tree duplicates the last hash on odd levels, producing CVE-2012-2459: two distinct block bodies with the same root. Read the fix and note that a "trivial" construction detail was a consensus bug
  • Digital signatures over elliptic curves, and the three schemes that matter
    • ECDSA on secp256k1: why k-reuse leaks the private key immediately (the Sony PS3 failure, later repeated by wallet implementations), and why signature malleability — negating s yields another valid signature — broke transaction-id stability until SegWit
    • Schnorr: linear, so signatures aggregate. This is the whole reason Taproot exists. MuSig2 and the two-round nonce problem
    • BLS: pairing-based, aggregates non-interactively across different messages, and is therefore what the Ethereum beacon chain uses to compress hundreds of thousands of attestations. Slower to verify individually, much cheaper in aggregate
  • Key derivation and the wallet stack
    • BIP-32 hierarchical deterministic derivation, hardened versus non-hardened paths, and the concrete disaster when an xpub plus one non-hardened child private key reveals the master key
    • BIP-39 mnemonics as an encoding of entropy plus a checksum, not as a password. The passphrase ("25th word") is the password
  • Commitment schemes and verifiable randomness
    • Hiding and binding as the two properties; hash commitments versus Pedersen commitments and why the latter's homomorphism matters for M15 and M19
    • VRFs — Algorand and Cardano use them for private leader election; the point is that you can prove you won without letting anyone predict who wins next

Work through

  • A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography — chapters 8 (hashes), 13 (signatures) and 15 (elliptic curves). Skip the security proofs on a first pass; read the definitions carefully.
  • The Cryptopals Crypto Challenges — sets 1–2 now, and set 8 once you have implemented ECDSA. Breaking a primitive teaches its guarantee better than reading the definition does.
  • CS251: Cryptocurrencies and Blockchain Technologies — the cryptographic-background lectures, if you would rather have this material presented than look it up.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapter 1 in full. It is the tightest treatment of exactly the subset a chain needs.
  • Programming Bitcoin — chapters 1–4. Song makes you implement finite field arithmetic and secp256k1 from scratch, which is the only way this becomes concrete.
  • Bitcoin Improvement Proposals — BIP-32, BIP-39 and BIP-340 (Schnorr). Read BIP-340's design rationale section especially.
  • Upgrading Ethereum — the BLS signatures section of part 2, for why the beacon chain made a different choice from Bitcoin.

Practice

Implement ECDSA sign and verify over secp256k1 using only a big-integer library — no crypto library. Then deliberately reuse a nonce across two signatures and recover the private key from the two signature pairs algebraically. Then build a Merkle tree over 1,000 leaves, produce an inclusion proof, and verify it. Done when your nonce-reuse recovery prints the original key, and when you can state the proof size for 1,000,000 leaves without computing it.

Where people go wrong

Treating a Merkle inclusion proof as proof that a transaction is valid. It proves only that the transaction was committed to under that root. Whether the root is on the canonical chain is a consensus question (M04), and whether the transactions under it obey the rules is a validity question that a light client cannot answer at all without either downloading the state or trusting someone. This gap is the entire reason fraud proofs and data-availability sampling exist in M13 and M14 — and misreading it is why people assert that light clients have "the same security as full nodes", which is false in a specific, nameable way.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why Schnorr signatures made Taproot possible and ECDSA did not, using the word "linear" and meaning it; and say what an attacker learns from an extended public key plus one non-hardened child private key.

M03

Bitcoin as a running system: UTXOs, Script, and the rules that are not consensus rules

≈10 h

Bitcoin is the smallest complete example of everything on this page, and it is small enough to hold entirely in your head — which Ethereum is not. Learn the state model, the transaction format and the validity rules here and M09's account model reads as a deliberate departure with known costs, rather than as "how blockchains work". The policy-versus-consensus distinction introduced here is one of the most transferable ideas in the field and is almost never taught explicitly.

Concepts

  • The UTXO model as the state representation
    • State is a set of unspent outputs, not a map of balances. Transactions consume outputs entirely and create new ones; "change" is an output back to yourself, which is why bad wallets leak your balance
    • Consequences that fall straight out: transactions are trivially parallelisable because their read/write sets are explicit, there is no global nonce, and there is also no way to express "the current price" — statefulness has to be simulated. Hold this thought until M17, where Solana and Sui both re-derive the explicit-read/write-set idea for parallel execution
  • Script: a deliberately non-Turing-complete stack language
    • No loops, no recursion, bounded execution — so no gas metering is needed. This is a real design position, not a limitation Ethereum later fixed. Read it as the alternative that lost, and know its argument
    • P2PKH, P2SH, P2WPKH and P2TR as an evolution of where the script lives and who pays for it. The migration to committing to the script rather than revealing it is the recurring move
  • Transaction structure and the malleability story
    • Inputs, outputs, witnesses, locktime, sequence. Why txid instability blocked payment channels, and how SegWit's witness-excluding txid unblocked the Lightning Network
    • Taproot (BIP-340/341/342): key-path spends look identical to ordinary payments, script-path spends reveal only the branch taken. Privacy and fee savings from the same change
  • Policy versus consensus — the distinction that generalises to every chain
    • Consensus rules decide whether a block is valid and are enforced by everyone; policy rules decide what an individual node relays and mines and are local. Standardness, dust limits and minimum relay fee are policy
    • Why this matters: a transaction can be perfectly valid and still unrelayable by most of the network. Every "censorship" question on any chain is really a question about whose policy applies at which hop
    • RBF and CPFP as fee-bumping mechanisms that exist entirely in policy space, and the full-RBF controversy over whether zero-confirmation transactions were ever a thing

Work through

  • Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System — all nine pages, twice. Once for the idea, once noticing what it does not specify.
  • Mastering Bitcoin — chapters 6 (transactions), 7 (authorization and authentication) and 8. This is the current third edition and covers Taproot; older copies do not.
  • Grokking Bitcoin — chapters 2–5 if the material is new. Rosenbaum builds the system incrementally and the diagrams are unusually good.
  • Bitcoin developer documentation — the transaction and script reference sections, used as a lookup rather than read straight through.
  • Bitcoin Optech — the Topics index, particularly "Transaction relay policy" and "Package relay".
  • aantonop educational videos — the security-model and fork talks, for the same material explained without a book's pacing.

Practice

Construct and sign a raw P2WPKH transaction by hand on signet — build the sighash yourself, sign it, serialise it, and broadcast. Then decode someone else's Taproot spend from mainnet and say whether it was key-path or script-path, and how you can tell. Done when your signet transaction confirms and you can explain every byte of it.

Where people go wrong

Assuming that because a transaction is valid, the network will carry it. Validity is a consensus property; relay is a policy decision made independently by every node on the path. Transactions get stuck not because they break rules but because they are non-standard, below the dust limit, or below a relay-fee floor — and the failure mode looks like the network being broken rather than a policy mismatch. The same confusion, one layer up, is why people describe Ethereum builders excluding a transaction as a protocol-level censorship event when it is a policy-level one; the difference decides whether the fix is a fork or a competing builder.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why Bitcoin's designers chose a language without loops and what that costs, and state two things SegWit changed that had nothing to do with block size.

M04

Nakamoto consensus, analysed rather than described

≈12 h

This is the module where the field acquired a theory. The whitepaper's security argument is a gambler's-ruin calculation that assumes an honest majority and a synchronous network, and it is not sufficient. The Backbone papers gave the protocol an actual security statement with named properties and explicit assumptions, and Eyal and Sirer showed the honest-majority claim was wrong in a way nobody had noticed for five years. Doing this properly is what lets you evaluate a consensus claim rather than repeat one.

Concepts

  • The protocol restated: longest (most-work) chain, difficulty retargeting, coinbase subsidy plus fees
    • Difficulty adjustment every 2016 blocks as a negative feedback loop with a two-week lag — and the death-spiral scenario when hash rate leaves faster than the adjustment can follow, which is why smaller PoW chains fail in ways Bitcoin does not
    • The whitepaper's section 11 as a random walk. Reproduce the calculation and see what it assumes: instant propagation, a static attacker fraction, no fee-driven behaviour
  • The Bitcoin Backbone properties — the vocabulary the field actually uses
    • Chain growth: honest chains lengthen at a minimum rate. Chain quality: honest blocks make up a minimum fraction of any long enough segment. Common prefix: honest parties' chains agree once you drop the last k blocks
    • Common prefix is where "six confirmations" comes from, and it is a probabilistic statement with a parameter. There is no depth at which a Nakamoto chain is final — this is the precise sense in which it differs from M06's BFT protocols
  • Selfish mining, and what it did to the honest-majority claim
    • Withhold a found block, publish it to orphan the honest one, and gain revenue share above your hash share. Eyal and Sirer's threshold is well below 50% and depends on how well you propagate — read the γ parameter carefully
    • Why it has not been observed at scale: pool reputation, the difficulty of the propagation advantage, and the fact that a detectable attack on the asset you are paid in is self-harming. This is an economic argument, not a protocol guarantee, and you should be able to say so
  • The 51% attack costed properly
    • What it can do (reorder, double-spend, censor) and cannot do (steal coins, change the issuance schedule, forge signatures). The list of things it cannot do is the important half
    • Rental-market attacks on small chains: Ethereum Classic and Bitcoin Gold were both reorged for real money. Compute the cost against exchange confirmation policies to see why it worked
  • Where the synchrony assumption hides
    • Block interval is chosen to be large relative to propagation delay so orphan rate stays low. This is a network-layer assumption doing consensus-layer work, and it is why M05 comes next

Work through

  • The Bitcoin Backbone Protocol: Analysis and Applications — sections 1–5. Do not skip the model section; the assumptions are the content.
  • Majority is not Enough: Bitcoin Mining is Vulnerable — the whole paper. Work the revenue algebra rather than reading the conclusion.
  • Foundations of Blockchains — the lectures on longest-chain consensus and its security analysis. Roughgarden's treatment is the clearest bridge between the informal and formal versions.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapters 2 and 5, for the mining ecosystem as it actually operates.
  • Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System — section 11 again, now with the Backbone vocabulary in hand.

Practice

Simulate a longest-chain network: n miners, a propagation delay, an attacker with fraction q of hash power, and a victim who waits k confirmations. Measure double-spend success rate as a function of q and k, and compare with the whitepaper's closed form. Then add selfish-mining behaviour for the attacker and plot revenue share against hash share. Done when your simulation reproduces the whitepaper's numbers for small q, and when your selfish miner beats proportional revenue at a hash share below 0.5.

Where people go wrong

Saying a transaction is "final" after six confirmations. Common prefix is a probabilistic guarantee, and the probability depends on the attacker's hash share, which is not observable in advance. Six confirmations was a rule of thumb calibrated against an assumed small attacker and a specific transaction value; it is not a protocol constant, and for a large enough payment it is not enough. The related error is treating finality in a PoS chain (M07) as the same kind of object — there it is a protocol-level property with slashing behind it, which is a genuinely different guarantee and not merely a faster one.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the three Backbone properties from memory and say which one "six confirmations" is about; and explain why selfish mining is profitable in theory yet rarely observed, without using the word "because miners are honest".

M05

The peer-to-peer layer, and the assumptions it is quietly holding up

≈7 h

M04's security argument rests on a synchrony assumption — blocks reach everyone fast relative to the block interval — and that assumption is a networking property, not a consensus one. This module is placed immediately after M04 rather than at the start precisely so you already know what breaks when it fails: orphan rate rises, chain quality degrades, and the selfish miner's γ improves. A networking module read before you know what is being gossiped teaches plumbing; read here it teaches an attack surface.

Concepts

  • Gossip as the transport, and its costs
    • Flooding gives robustness and terrible bandwidth efficiency: a transaction is announced to every peer by every peer. Erlay replaces most of that with set reconciliation and cuts relay bandwidth substantially at the cost of latency
    • Compact block relay (BIP-152): send short transaction ids and let the peer reconstruct from its mempool. Works because mempools mostly agree — and degrades exactly when they do not, which is during the congestion you most wanted it for
  • Propagation delay as a consensus parameter
    • Orphan/uncle rate is the observable proxy for how far the network is from the synchrony assumption. Decker and Wattenhofer measured this on Bitcoin; the relationship between block size, propagation time and orphan rate is the core argument in the block size debate (M21)
    • Why the block interval cannot simply be reduced: at short intervals, propagation delay becomes a large fraction of the interval, orphan rate rises, and effective security falls even though nominal hash rate is unchanged
  • Eclipse and partition attacks
    • Heilman et al.'s eclipse attack: monopolise a victim's peer connections and you control their view of the chain, enabling double-spends against them and hash-power amplification for you. The fixes were mundane — more buckets, better address management, anchor connections — which is the lesson
    • Partition attacks at the BGP layer (Apostolaki et al., "Hijacking Bitcoin"). Physical network topology is not uniformly random and a small number of ASes carry most traffic
  • The mempool as a real data structure with policy attached
    • Eviction, minimum relay fee, ancestor and descendant limits, and package relay. The mempool is not a queue and is not globally consistent — every node has a different one
    • Why this matters upward: the "public mempool" is the substrate MEV searchers watch (M16), and private order flow is precisely the decision to stop using it
  • Peer discovery and its centralisation
    • DNS seeds, hardcoded seeds, addr gossip. Ethereum's discv5 and the ENR record. Note that discovery bootstraps through a handful of operator-run endpoints on every chain — a soft centralisation that nobody has removed

Work through

  • Eclipse Attacks on Bitcoin's Peer-to-Peer Network — the whole paper, including the countermeasures section.
  • Bandwidth-Efficient Transaction Relay for Bitcoin — sections 1–4, for what flooding actually costs and how reconciliation fixes it.
  • Bitcoin Optech — the "Compact block relay", "Erlay" and "Package relay" topic pages.
  • Mastering Bitcoin — chapter 8 on the network, for the concrete message types.
  • Ethereum developer documentation — the networking-layer pages on discv5 and devp2p, to see the same problems solved differently.

Practice

Run a node (Bitcoin Core on signet, or a Geth/Reth pair on a testnet) and instrument it: log peer count, inbound versus outbound, and time-to-first-announcement for new blocks. Then measure your own block propagation delay distribution over a day. Done when you can state your node's median and p99 delay, and say what orphan rate that delay would imply at a 10-second block interval.

Where people go wrong

Treating "the mempool" as a global object. There is no such thing — every node has its own, they differ by policy and by propagation timing, and tools that show you "the mempool" are showing you one node's view. Reasoning that assumes a shared mempool produces wrong answers about fee estimation, about whether a transaction was censored, and about what a searcher can actually see. The strongest version of this error is assuming private order flow is a recent innovation; it is just the observation that the shared-mempool abstraction was never real.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why halving the block interval does not double throughput at constant security, in terms of propagation delay and orphan rate; and describe how an eclipse attack converts a networking weakness into a double-spend.

M06

Classical Byzantine agreement, and the impossibility results Nakamoto walked around

≈12 h

Proof of stake is a BFT protocol wearing blockchain clothes, and it is unreadable without this vocabulary — finality, quorum, view change, accountable safety all come from here. This module also gives you the tools to evaluate the claims made about every new chain, because almost all of them are restatements of the safety-versus-liveness trade that DLS formalised in 1988. Put it after M04 so you can see Nakamoto consensus as one point in this design space rather than as the default.

Concepts

  • The system models, stated precisely, because everything depends on which one you are in
    • Synchronous (known bound on delay), asynchronous (no bound), partially synchronous (a bound exists but is unknown, or holds after some unknown GST). Almost every real protocol lives in the third
    • Crash faults versus Byzantine faults, and why the bound moves from n > 2f to n > 3f. Derive the 3f+1 bound rather than memorising it — the argument is a two-page indistinguishability sketch
  • FLP: no deterministic protocol solves consensus in an asynchronous system with even one crash fault
    • Work through the bivalent-configuration argument. The result is about termination, not safety — you can always stay safe by not deciding
    • The three standard escapes: randomisation (Ben-Or, and every DAG-BFT protocol), partial synchrony (PBFT, Tendermint, HotStuff, Raft), and failure detectors. Name which escape any given protocol used and you have read half its design
  • PBFT and its descendants
    • Pre-prepare, prepare, commit as a two-round quorum protocol; why two rounds and not one; view change as the expensive part, at O(n³) message complexity in the original
    • Tendermint's simplification into a round-based lock-and-precommit protocol with immediate finality; HotStuff's linear view change and the pipelining that makes it practical at large n. Libra/Diem, Aptos and Sui all descend from HotStuff for this reason
  • Accountable safety, which is the property that makes proof of stake possible at all
    • If safety fails, at least f+1 participants provably signed conflicting statements and can be identified from the evidence alone. This is what slashing punishes in M07 — without it, a stake-based system has nothing to confiscate
    • The availability-accountability dilemma: a protocol cannot be both live under asynchrony and accountably safe. Ethereum's answer is to run two protocols at once, which M07 explains
  • CAP as it actually applies here, not as the slogan
    • A partition forces a choice between answering possibly-stale (available) and refusing to answer (consistent). Nakamoto chose availability — it always extends a chain — and got probabilistic consistency. Tendermint chose consistency and halts under partition. Neither is a bug
    • The corollary worth carrying: any product claiming "instant finality and always available" is either assuming synchrony or trusting someone
  • When you should not use a blockchain at all
    • Wüst and Gervais's decision tree: if the writers are known and mutually trusting, you want a database with signatures. Permissioned "blockchains" are usually PBFT with a marketing budget, and this module is where you acquire the vocabulary to say so precisely

Work through

  • Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process — the whole paper. It is nine pages and the argument is worth reconstructing on paper.
  • Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony — sections 1–3 for the model definitions, which are the most-cited part.
  • Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance — the whole paper, focusing on view change.
  • HotStuff: BFT Consensus in the Lens of Blockchain — sections 1–5, for how linear view change is bought.
  • Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming — chapters 2 and 5, for abstractions and consensus done rigorously.
  • Do you need a Blockchain? — the whole paper; it is short and it is the antidote to a lot of enterprise nonsense.
  • Foundations of Blockchains lecture videos — lectures 2–7, a self-contained bootcamp on Dolev–Strong, FLP and Tendermint. Watch these if the papers above are heavy going.

Practice

Implement Tendermint-style consensus for four nodes over local sockets: propose, prevote, precommit, commit, with a lock rule and a round timeout. Then kill one node and confirm it still decides; kill two and confirm it halts rather than forking. Done when the three-node network is stuck but has not disagreed — you should be able to point at the precommit lock that prevents the fork.

Where people go wrong

Reading FLP as "consensus is impossible" and concluding that Paxos, Raft, PBFT and Tendermint have somehow beaten it. They have not. FLP says no deterministic protocol can guarantee termination in a fully asynchronous model. Every shipped protocol keeps safety unconditionally and gives up guaranteed termination under asynchrony — it stalls instead of forking. Once you see that every one of them made the same trade, the marketing distinction between "our consensus is Byzantine fault tolerant" claims collapses into the two questions worth asking: which model, and what happens when it does not hold.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive why Byzantine agreement needs n > 3f while crash agreement needs only n > 2f; and explain what accountable safety gives you that ordinary safety does not, and why proof of stake would not work without it.

M07

Proof of stake in production: Gasper, slashing and weak subjectivity

≈12 h

Ethereum's consensus is not a textbook BFT protocol and not Nakamoto consensus; it is two protocols composed, and almost every wrong statement about it comes from analysing one half in isolation. With M04 and M06 both in hand you can see exactly what LMD-GHOST is for (liveness, a chain that always grows) and what Casper FFG is for (accountable finality), and why the composition needs the awkward parts — weak subjectivity, the inactivity leak — rather than treating them as warts.

Concepts

  • The stake-based Sybil answer and its two new problems
    • Nothing-at-stake: signing every fork is free when your resource is not consumed. Solved by slashing, which requires accountable safety from M06
    • Long-range attacks: old validators whose stake is withdrawn can costlessly rewrite ancient history. Not solvable in-protocol — the answer is weak subjectivity, below
  • Gasper as a composition
    • LMD-GHOST as fork choice: greedy heaviest observed subtree using each validator's latest message. Always produces a head, hence liveness under asynchrony
    • Casper FFG as the finality gadget: justify then finalise checkpoints at epoch boundaries with a 2/3 supermajority, with the two slashing conditions (double vote, surround vote) that make safety accountable
    • Why both: FFG alone stalls under partition, GHOST alone never finalises. The composition is the answer to M06's availability-accountability dilemma — accept that under partition you get availability without finality, and recover finality when the partition heals
  • Slots, epochs, committees and attestations
    • 12-second slots, 32-slot epochs, committee sampling so no validator attests to everything, BLS aggregation so the signatures fit. Every one of these is a scaling compromise with a security cost you should be able to name
    • The 33% / 50% / 66% thresholds and what each one buys an attacker: prevent finality, control fork choice, finalise anything. Note these are stake thresholds, not validator-count thresholds
  • The inactivity leak
    • If finality stalls for four epochs, offline validators bleed stake until the online set is again a 2/3 supermajority. This is how the chain heals from a partition that takes a third of validators offline — and it is a deliberate decision to prefer eventual finality over preserving everyone's balance
  • Weak subjectivity, stated honestly
    • A syncing node cannot distinguish the real chain from a fabricated long-range history using the protocol alone; it needs a recent trusted checkpoint. This is a real, permanent trust assumption that proof of work does not have, and pretending otherwise is the most common dishonesty in the PoS-versus-PoW argument
    • The honest counter: the checkpoint requirement is weak (weeks old is fine), it is easy to obtain from many independent sources, and PoW has its own non-protocol assumption in the form of "which chain is the real one" after a contentious fork
  • The known attacks
    • Balancing and bouncing attacks from Neu, Tas and Tse; ex-ante reorgs; the proposer-boost mitigation and what it costs. These are not hypothetical — the paper directly changed the specification
    • Timing games: proposers delaying block release to capture more MEV, at the cost of attestation reliability. An economically rational behaviour the protocol does not forbid, and a live problem

Work through

  • Upgrading Ethereum — parts 2 and 3 in full. This is the single best reference on the beacon chain and it is free.
  • Combining GHOST and Casper — the whole paper. It is the specification's actual source.
  • Casper the Friendly Finality Gadget — sections 2–4 for the slashing conditions and their proofs.
  • Three Attacks on Proof-of-Stake Ethereum — all of it; then check which mitigations shipped.
  • Ethereum Proof-of-Stake under Scrutiny — for a systematic view of the assumptions.
  • Ouroboros: A Provably Secure Proof-of-Stake Blockchain Protocol — sections 1–4, as the contrasting design that took provable security as the starting constraint.
  • Algorand — for VRF-based private leader election, a third distinct answer.

Practice

Implement Casper FFG's slashing detection: given a stream of validator votes (source and target checkpoints), flag every double vote and every surround vote. Run it against a synthetic stream containing both. Then, on paper, trace one epoch of LMD-GHOST fork choice through a network split where 40% of validators see one head and 60% see another, and say when finality resumes. Done when your detector finds every violation and produces no false positives on honest votes.

Where people go wrong

Describing proof of stake as "just like proof of work but with money instead of electricity". The Sybil-resistance layer is analogous; the consensus layer is not. Ethereum has explicit, accountable finality with a slashable safety proof, which no proof-of-work chain has at any depth. It also has weak subjectivity, which no proof-of-work chain needs. Treating the two as interchangeable makes it impossible to say anything correct about either — including the genuinely open question of whether stake concentration (M08) is a worse centralisation vector than hash-rate concentration.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why Gasper needs two protocols rather than one, in terms of M06's availability-accountability dilemma; and state exactly what a node with no recent checkpoint cannot determine, and why that is a permanent property rather than an implementation gap.

M08

Validator economics and where staking actually centralises

≈8 h

M07 gave you the protocol thresholds; this module asks who is standing at them. The protocol's security argument assumes stake is distributed among independent parties with uncorrelated failures, and the deployed reality is a small number of liquid-staking protocols and exchanges. That gap is not a footnote — it is the difference between the security the specification proves and the security the network has, and it is the most-argued live question in Ethereum research.

Concepts

  • The issuance curve and what it is trying to buy
    • Issuance falls as total stake rises, deliberately, to target a stake ratio rather than a yield. Add EIP-1559 base-fee burn and net issuance can go negative — the "ultrasound money" framing, which is a consequence of a security parameter rather than a design goal
    • The live argument about reducing issuance: too much staked means too much of the supply is illiquid and captured by LST intermediaries; too little means cheap attacks. Nobody agrees on the target and the debate is worth reading as an example of a protocol parameter with no clean answer
  • Reward composition and why it drives behaviour
    • Consensus rewards (attestation, proposal, sync committee) are protocol-issued and near-uniform. Execution-layer rewards — priority fees and MEV — are lumpy, capturable and much larger in variance. This asymmetry is why solo staking is high-variance and pooling is rational
    • EIP-7251 raised the max effective balance to 2,048 ETH, letting large operators consolidate validators; read what it fixed (attestation load) and what it concentrates
  • Penalties, and the correlation penalty specifically
    • Inactivity penalties are small and linear; slashing includes a correlated component that scales with how much other stake was slashed in the same window. The design intent is to punish shared infrastructure — a single operator failing 10,000 validators is a systemic event, ten thousand independent failures are not
    • Whether this actually deters concentration, given that the correlation penalty is a tail risk against a certain fee income, is an open empirical question
  • Liquid staking and the intermediation layer
    • An LST turns staked ETH into a transferable claim, which is enormously useful and also creates a governance token that sits between stakers and validators. Lido's share of staked ETH has repeatedly approached thresholds the community treats as dangerous, and the response has been social rather than protocol-level
    • Distributed validator technology (DVT) as the technical mitigation: split one validator key across operators with threshold signatures, so a "validator" is not a single machine or a single company
    • Exchange staking as the other concentration: custodial, opaque, and regulatorily exposed in a way that is not a protocol risk but is a correlated-failure risk
  • Proof-of-work's mirror problem, for calibration
    • Mining pools concentrated hash rate to a comparable degree, and pool operators — not miners — chose what to mine. The centralisation pressure is not unique to stake; the difference is that switching pools is instant while unstaking is not

Work through

  • Upgrading Ethereum — the incentives and rewards sections of part 2, for exactly how each reward is computed.
  • Time is Money: Strategic Timing Games in Proof-of-Stake Protocols — the whole paper; it is the cleanest demonstration that reward structure drives protocol-adjacent behaviour.
  • Protocol Priorities Update for 2026 — for where the Foundation currently places staking centralisation among its concerns.
  • ethresear.ch — the issuance-curve and MEV-burn threads. Read the disagreements, not the summaries.
  • clientdiversity.org — for the current operator and client distribution, which is the empirical input to all of this.

Practice

Build a spreadsheet or script that computes a solo validator's expected annual return and its variance, given current issuance, average priority fees and MEV distribution, then compare against an LST's net return after its fee. Compute how many validators you would need for solo-staking variance to fall below the LST's. Done when you can state that number and explain why it is the economic argument for pooling — independent of any argument about convenience.

Where people go wrong

Reading validator count as a decentralisation metric. A million validators operated by twenty entities on three cloud providers running two client implementations is not a decentralised set — it is a correlated one, and the protocol thresholds in M07 are about stake and about correlated failure, not about key count. The metrics that matter are operator concentration, client diversity and hosting diversity, and all three are worse than the validator count suggests. The same error in reverse: assuming that because Lido is a DAO rather than a company, its share is not a concentration.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why the correlation penalty exists and what behaviour it is trying to price; and say what would actually happen — protocol-level, step by step — if a single staking provider controlling 34% of stake went offline simultaneously.

M09

The EVM and the account model: a shared computer with a meter on it

≈12 h

This is the pivot from "how do we agree" to "what are we agreeing about". Ethereum's contribution was not a better consensus algorithm — it was a deterministic, metered, globally-shared execution environment, and every design decision in it is downstream of two constraints: every node must reach byte-identical state, and computation must be paid for or the network dies to a while(true). Read the EVM as the answer to those two constraints and the odd parts stop being odd.

Concepts

  • Accounts instead of UTXOs, and what the trade actually was
    • Externally-owned accounts and contract accounts; nonce, balance, storage root, code hash. The account model makes stateful contracts natural and makes parallelism hard, which is the exact inverse of M03's trade — and the reason M17's chains reintroduce explicit read/write sets
    • Replay protection by nonce rather than by output consumption, and the queueing behaviour that follows: one stuck low-fee transaction blocks every later transaction from that account
  • The Merkle Patricia trie, and why it is not a plain Merkle tree
    • It is a key-value map with authenticated lookups, so it can prove absence as well as presence — which a plain Merkle tree cannot, per M02. The price is a deep, pointer-chasing structure with terrible disk locality, which is the root cause of Ethereum's state-growth problem in M20
    • Four tries in play: state, storage (one per contract), transaction, receipt. Know what each commits to and which one a light client actually queries
  • The virtual machine itself
    • 256-bit words (chosen for hash and elliptic-curve friendliness, expensive for everything else), a stack limited to 1024, ephemeral memory, persistent storage. Read a dozen opcodes in evm.codes with their gas costs beside them
    • CALL versus DELEGATECALL versus STATICCALL: DELEGATECALL runs someone else's code against your storage and with your msg.sender. This single opcode is what makes proxy upgradeability possible and what killed the Parity multisig — you cannot understand M10 or M12 without it
    • Storage layout: slot for a plain variable, keccak256(key ‖ slot) for a mapping entry, packed slots for small types. Compute one by hand and read it out of a live contract
  • Gas as a metering and pricing mechanism, and the two things it conflates
    • Gas costs are meant to track real resource consumption — CPU, memory, disk, state growth. They do so badly, and repricing forks (EIP-150, EIP-1884, EIP-2929) exist because mispriced opcodes are a DoS vector. Access lists and warm/cold pricing came out of this
    • EIP-1559: a protocol-set base fee that burns, plus a priority tip. It targets 50% block fullness and adjusts the base fee multiplicatively. It did not reduce fees — it made them predictable, and the empirical work says so plainly
    • Refunds, and why SELFDESTRUCT and the gas-token trick had to be neutered
  • Precompiles and the EOF direction
    • Precompiled contracts as an escape hatch for operations too expensive in EVM bytecode — ecrecover, modexp, the BN254 pairing that makes on-chain SNARK verification affordable in M15
    • EOF (EVM Object Format): code/data separation, static jumps, versioning. Read what problem it solves — jump-destination analysis and the impossibility of validating code ahead of time — rather than the feature list

Work through

  • Ethereum Yellow Paper — sections 2–4 and 9, plus appendix H. Dense and worth it; it is the actual definition.
  • Ethereum Whitepaper — the sections arguing for Turing-completeness and for gas, to see which constraints were chosen deliberately and which were discovered later.
  • Mastering Ethereum — chapters 6, 7 and 13. Pre-Merge, so treat all consensus content as historical, but the EVM and account-model chapters remain the best prose explanation.
  • evm.codes — work through the opcode reference and run the interactive playground on a small program.
  • Remix IDE — deploy a five-line contract and step its execution in the debugger, watching the stack and storage change per opcode.
  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — EIP-1559 and EIP-2929 in full, including rationale.
  • Empirical Analysis of EIP-1559 — for what actually changed after deployment versus what was predicted.
  • Ethereum execution specs — the state transition function in Python, which is more readable than the Yellow Paper for implementation questions.

Practice

Write an EVM interpreter for a subset: stack ops, arithmetic, MSTORE/MLOAD, SSTORE/SLOAD, JUMP/JUMPI with jump-destination validation, and gas accounting. Run a hand-assembled loop through it and confirm it halts on out-of-gas. Then, separately, use cast storage against a live mainnet ERC-20 to read one holder's balance out of raw storage, computing the slot yourself. Done when your computed slot matches the value the contract reports.

Where people go wrong

Believing EIP-1559 was a fee reduction. It replaced a first-price auction with a base fee that adjusts to demand plus a tip, which made fees predictable and burned the base fee. Average cost is set by demand for blockspace and did not fall. The related and more damaging error is thinking gas costs measure computation: they are an administered price schedule that approximates resource cost, they have been wrong before, and every repricing fork exists because a mispriced opcode became a denial-of-service vector.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the storage slot of balances[addr] for a mapping at slot 3 without looking it up; and explain precisely what DELEGATECALL changes about the execution context, and why that makes it both the foundation of upgradeable contracts and a loaded gun.

M10

The contract programming model: Solidity, ABI and upgradeability

≈10 h

M09 gave you the machine; this is the layer people actually write, and its abstractions leak in specific, exploitable places. Solidity looks like an ordinary language and is not: every external call is a potential re-entry, every storage write costs real money, and the deployment is immutable unless you built an escape hatch — in which case the escape hatch is now your largest attack surface. Learn this before M12 so the security module can be about classes of bug rather than about syntax.

Concepts

  • The ABI as the calling convention
    • Four-byte function selectors as truncated keccak hashes — hence selector collisions, which are findable and have been used in real attacks on proxy dispatch. Static versus dynamic encoding and where the offsets point
    • Events and topics as the read path: logs are not readable from inside the EVM, they exist for off-chain indexers. Indexed parameters, bloom filters, and why "just emit an event" is a design decision about who can query your contract
  • Solidity's traps that come from the machine below
    • Every external call transfers control. Checks-Effects-Interactions is not a style rule, it is the only ordering that survives re-entry — and M12 shows what happens when it is violated
    • Visibility, payable, receive/fallback, and the fact that a contract can be forced to receive ether via SELFDESTRUCT so address(this).balance is never a reliable invariant
    • Integer overflow: unchecked before 0.8.0, reverting after, and unchecked blocks bringing the old behaviour back on purpose for gas
  • Upgradeability, and why it is the hardest ordinary decision in the field
    • Transparent proxy, UUPS and the Diamond pattern, all built on DELEGATECALL. Storage-slot collisions between proxy and implementation, and EIP-1967's fixed pseudo-random slots as the fix
    • Initialiser functions instead of constructors, and the initialisation-front-running failure that keeps recurring
    • The real question is not technical: an upgradeable contract is a contract whose owner can replace its logic, so "immutable and trustless" and "upgradeable" are mutually exclusive claims. Timelocks and multisigs bound the risk without removing it
  • The development loop as it is actually run
    • Foundry: forge for build and test in Solidity, anvil for a local node, cast for raw RPC. Mainnet forking as the standard integration-test technique — you test against real deployed protocol state, not mocks
    • Property and invariant testing with forge's fuzzer, which matters more than unit tests here because the bugs are about reachable states, not about individual functions
    • Gas profiling and the optimisations that are worth it (storage packing, avoiding repeated SLOAD) versus the ones that are folklore
  • Other languages, briefly, so you know the space
    • Vyper's deliberate restrictions (no inheritance, no inline assembly, bounded loops) as a security-through-simplicity position, and the fact that it has had its own compiler-level exploits. Yul as the intermediate representation you will read in optimised output

Work through

  • Solidity documentation — the language reference plus the "Security Considerations" page, which is short and dense.
  • Foundry — the Foundry Book front to back on testing, fuzzing and forking. This is the toolchain the rest of the page assumes.
  • Cyfrin Updraft — the Solidity and Foundry tracks, if you have not written contracts before.
  • OpenZeppelin Contracts documentation — read the proxy and access-control implementations as reference code, not as a dependency to import blindly.
  • Solidity by Example — for quick, correct patterns when you need one.
  • Speedrun Ethereum — the first three challenges, as the build-something counterweight to reading a language reference.
  • Mastering Ethereum — chapters 7 and 9 for the conceptual framing, allowing for its age.

Practice

Write a small vault contract (deposit, withdraw, share accounting), deploy it behind a UUPS proxy, and then upgrade it to a version with an extra state variable — inserted in the wrong position so it collides with existing storage. Observe the corruption, then fix the layout and re-do the upgrade correctly. Done when you can point at the exact slot that was clobbered and explain why appending is safe and inserting is not.

Where people go wrong

Treating a deployed contract as a program and its callers as users. Every external call hands control to code that may call back into you, may revert to grief you, may be a contract pretending to be an EOA, and may be deployed after you. Solidity's syntax hides this — token.transfer(to, amt) looks like a method call and is a control transfer to an arbitrary program. Nearly every bug class in M12 is a consequence of writing single-threaded code in a re-entrant, adversarial environment.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a proxy needs EIP-1967's specific storage slots rather than slot 0; and say what an auditor means by "this contract is not upgradeable but the oracle it reads is", and why that sentence matters.

M11

Account abstraction: making an account a program

≈7 h

The externally-owned account from M09 is a hard-coded authentication policy — one secp256k1 key, no batching, no sponsorship, no recovery — and it is the single largest source of unrecoverable user loss in the ecosystem. Account abstraction replaces that policy with code. It sits here because it needs M09's account model and M10's contract model, and because it is a clean case study in shipping a protocol change through an out-of-protocol layer first and enshrining it afterwards.

Concepts

  • What the EOA actually fixes in place, and why it is a problem
    • One signature scheme, one signer, gas payable only in the native token, one operation per transaction, and key loss equals total loss. Each of these is a product constraint that no other financial system accepts
  • ERC-4337 as an alternative transaction pipeline built entirely in contract space
    • UserOperations in a separate mempool; bundlers assemble them into a single call to a singleton EntryPoint; the account contract validates. Nothing about this required a hard fork, which is the design's whole point
    • Paymasters: a third party sponsors gas, so users can pay in an ERC-20 or not pay at all. The validation-time DoS problem this creates, and the storage-access restrictions in the spec that solve it — a good example of an economic problem forcing an ugly technical rule
    • Aggregators for BLS-style signature aggregation across UserOperations
  • EIP-7702, which shipped in the Pectra upgrade in May 2025
    • An EOA can, per-transaction, set its code to point at a contract's implementation — so an existing address gains contract behaviour without migrating to a new address. This is the answer to 4337's biggest adoption problem: every existing user already has an EOA with history, balances and approvals attached
    • The new hazards: delegating to a malicious implementation drains the account, and the security model for "which contract is my EOA currently pointing at" is now a thing wallets must display. Read the security-considerations section of the EIP specifically
  • What this unlocks, concretely
    • Batched approve-and-swap in one atomic transaction; session keys with scoped permissions and expiry; social recovery via guardians; passkey/secp256r1 signing so phone secure-enclaves work. Note that secp256r1 needed a precompile (RIP-7212) to be affordable — abstraction is not free
  • The centralisation question nobody has settled
    • Bundlers and paymasters are infrastructure operators who can censor. A UserOperation mempool with two bundlers is a worse censorship story than the transaction mempool it replaced, and the answer so far has been "more bundlers, eventually"

Work through

  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — ERC-4337 and EIP-7702 in full, rationale and security considerations included.
  • Ethereum developer documentation — the account abstraction pages, for the current shipped state rather than the proposal state.
  • Building on Ethereum in 2026 — for what has actually been adopted, which lags the specifications considerably.
  • Foundry — used here to write and test a minimal account contract against a local EntryPoint.

Practice

Implement a minimal ERC-4337 account: validateUserOp checking a signature and paying the EntryPoint, plus an execute that performs a batched call. Deploy it on a testnet with a public bundler and send a UserOperation that approves a token and swaps it in one operation. Then add a session key restricted to one target contract and a one-hour expiry. Done when the session key can perform the allowed call and reverts on any other.

Where people go wrong

Assuming account abstraction removes trust rather than moving it. A sponsored transaction means a paymaster decided to pay for you and can decline; a bundled UserOperation means a bundler chose to include it and can drop it; an EIP-7702 delegation means an implementation contract you approved once can now act with your account's authority. The user-experience gain is real and the trust surface is genuinely larger, which is a trade worth making consciously — and worth reading as the same pattern as M13's sequencers and M16's builders.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why ERC-4337 needed a separate mempool rather than reusing the existing one; and say what EIP-7702 gives an existing user that a fresh 4337 smart account cannot.

M12

Smart contract security: the bug classes, the exploit record, and the tools that find them

≈14 h

Contracts hold bearer assets, run in public, and cannot be patched without a mechanism you had to build in advance. That combination makes this the highest-consequence module on the page, and the one where the gap between reading and doing is widest. The goal is not a checklist — checklists find yesterday's bugs. It is to internalise the adversary's method, which is to look for a reachable state where an invariant you assumed was global is only locally true.

Concepts

  • Re-entrancy, properly generalised
    • Classic single-function re-entry (The DAO, 2016, 3.6M ETH, and the fork that created Ethereum Classic); cross-function re-entry through a shared state variable; cross-contract re-entry through a shared dependency
    • Read-only re-entrancy, which is the one that still catches good teams: a view function returns a stale value mid-callback, and a third protocol reading it prices something wrong. Nothing is mutated by the attacker in the victim contract at all
    • Checks-Effects-Interactions and reentrancy guards as mitigations, and why a guard on one contract does not protect a reader of it
  • Access control and privilege
    • Missing or wrong modifiers; uninitialised proxies (the second Parity multisig failure — a library was initialised by a stranger who then invoked SELFDESTRUCT, freezing 513k ETH permanently); tx.origin used for authentication and why it is always wrong
    • Privileged-role inventory as an audit technique: enumerate every function an owner, guardian or upgrader can call, and ask what a compromised key does. Most "hacks" of large protocols in the last few years were key compromises, not contract bugs
  • Arithmetic and accounting
    • Overflow before 0.8.0 and inside unchecked; precision loss and rounding direction — round in the protocol's favour, always, and know which way that is; the ERC-4626 inflation/donation attack where a first depositor manipulates the share price
  • Oracle and price manipulation
    • Spot price from an AMM pool is a manipulable number, and a flash loan makes manipulating it free of capital cost. bZx, Harvest, Cheese Bank, and dozens since. The fix is not "use a TWAP" — it is to price the manipulation cost against the profit, which M10 of the sibling page treats economically
  • The tooling stack, and what each tool can and cannot see
    • Static analysis (Slither): fast, finds known patterns, high false-positive rate, blind to protocol logic. Fuzzing (Foundry invariant tests, Echidna, Medusa): finds reachable states, needs you to write the invariant — and writing the right invariant is the actual skill
    • Symbolic execution and formal verification (Certora, KEVM, halmos): proves properties over all inputs within a model. Expensive, and the model is where the bugs hide. Use for the two or three invariants that matter most
    • Audits, contests and bug bounties as an economic layer: what a competitive audit finds that a firm does not, and vice versa
  • Reading the exploit record as a discipline
    • Work post-mortems backwards: the transaction, the invariant it broke, the assumption that made the invariant look safe. Rekt's leaderboard and the SoK on DeFi attacks give you several hundred worked cases, which is more instructive than any list of bug categories

Work through

  • Ethernaut — every level. It is the fastest route from "I know what re-entrancy is" to "I can find it".
  • Damn Vulnerable DeFi — all challenges. These are protocol-logic bugs rather than syntax bugs, which is the harder and more realistic class.
  • Smart contract security (ethereum.org) — the whole page as an index into the primary sources.
  • OWASP Smart Contract Top 10 — the current list, used as a coverage check on your own review process.
  • Slither — run it on your own M10 vault and read every finding, including the false positives.
  • Foundry — the invariant-testing chapter, which is the highest-leverage part of the book.
  • Secureum — the Epoch 0 slides and the RACE quizzes; the quizzes are calibrated harder than most audits.
  • Etherscan — take one exploit transaction from a post-mortem and read it in the state-diff and internal-trace views until you can narrate what happened.
  • Flash Boys 2.0 — for why the mempool is an adversary, not just a queue.

Practice

Take your M10 vault and write an invariant test asserting that total assets always equal the sum of user claims, then fuzz it with Foundry until it breaks — and it should, because share-price rounding drifts. Fix the rounding direction and re-run. Then implement a read-only re-entrancy attack against a second contract that reads your vault's share price during a callback. Done when you have one exploit transaction that steals funds without ever calling a state-changing function on the victim.

Where people go wrong

Believing an audit makes a contract safe. Audits are time-boxed sampling by humans with incomplete context; most large losses since 2021 have been in audited code, and a substantial share were not contract bugs at all but compromised keys, malicious dependencies, or governance capture. The second, subtler error is writing tests that confirm intended behaviour. The adversary does not care about intended behaviour. Write invariants — statements that must hold in every reachable state — and let a fuzzer look for the states you did not imagine.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain read-only re-entrancy to someone who already knows classic re-entrancy, and say why a nonReentrant modifier on the victim does not prevent it; and state the strongest invariant of a lending pool in one sentence.

M13

Scaling and the rollup-centric roadmap

≈11 h

Every node executing every transaction is what makes verification cheap and throughput low, and no amount of engineering removes that trade directly. Rollups sidestep it by moving execution off-chain while keeping data and dispute resolution on-chain — so the L1 verifies rather than executes. This module needs M09 (what execution is) and pays into M14 (data availability is the binding constraint) and M16 (sequencing is where the money is).

Concepts

  • The trilemma stated as an engineering constraint rather than a slogan
    • Scalability, security and decentralisation trade because "decentralisation" concretely means a consumer machine can validate. Raise throughput and you raise the hardware floor; raise the hardware floor and the validating set shrinks. Everything else is a way to break the link between throughput and verification cost
    • The failed alternatives are instructive: sharding execution (abandoned as too complex), state channels (works, but only for fixed participant sets — Lightning is the surviving success), Plasma (fell to the data-availability problem, which is exactly what M14 is about)
  • What makes a rollup a rollup
    • Execution off-chain, data on-chain, and a proof system that lets L1 reject an invalid state root. Drop the data and you have a validium; drop the proof and you have a sidechain with good marketing
    • The security claim reduces to: can a user reconstruct state and exit without the operator's cooperation? Every stage classification is a formalisation of that one question
  • Optimistic rollups
    • Assume validity, allow a challenge window; interactive fraud proofs bisect the disputed execution down to a single instruction the L1 can adjudicate. Arbitrum's multi-round bisection versus Optimism's Cannon; the seven-day window as a function of censorship-resistance assumptions, not of proving time
    • Why the window cannot simply be shortened: it must exceed the time an attacker could censor challengers on L1
  • ZK (validity) rollups
    • Prove every state transition, so no challenge window and withdrawals are as fast as proving plus finality. The cost moves to proving time and proving cost, which M15 covers. Type 1 through Type 4 zkEVMs as a spectrum from bytecode-equivalent-and-slow to language-compatible-and-fast
  • The parts that are not decentralised yet, stated plainly
    • Almost every production rollup has a single sequencer that orders transactions, and an upgrade multisig that can change the contracts. L2Beat's stage framework exists to make this legible: Stage 0 is operator-run, Stage 1 has a working proof system plus a Security Council, Stage 2 is code-governed. As of 2026 the population is still concentrated at Stage 0 and 1 — check the current table rather than trusting this sentence
    • Forced-inclusion and escape hatches as the mechanism that makes a centralised sequencer tolerable: you can submit directly to L1 and the rollup must include you. Verify this exists before believing a rollup's decentralisation claim
    • Based rollups, where L1 proposers do the sequencing, as one live proposal to remove the sequencer entirely

Work through

  • An Incomplete Guide to Rollups — the whole post. It remains the clearest single explanation of the design space.
  • Fraud and Data Availability Proofs — sections 1–4, for why fraud proofs need data and what happens when they do not get it.
  • L2Beat — the Stages page and then two individual rollup risk pages read in full. This is the best-maintained empirical source in the ecosystem.
  • Arbitrum documentation — the "Inside Arbitrum" section on the fraud-proof protocol and the delayed inbox.
  • OP Stack documentation — the fault-proof and derivation-pipeline pages, for the contrasting implementation.
  • Endgame — for why the roadmap concluded that block production centralises and validation must not.

Practice

Take one production rollup and answer, with contract addresses as evidence: who can upgrade the bridge contracts and on what timelock; is there a working permissionless proof system today; can you force-include a transaction if the sequencer censors you, and what does that cost; and if the sequencer vanished permanently, what is the exact sequence of calls that gets your funds out? Done when every answer cites a verified contract or a specification section, not a blog post.

Where people go wrong

Treating "rollup" as a security category. It is a category of architecture; the security depends on whether the proof system is live, whether the data is really on L1, and who holds the upgrade keys. A "ZK rollup" whose prover is a single permissioned server and whose contracts sit behind an unlimited multisig has the trust model of a company, not of Ethereum, regardless of the cryptography inside it. The stage framework exists because this confusion was widespread and expensive.

Checkpoint

M14

Data availability: the constraint the whole roadmap now turns on

≈10 h

M13 ended with the observation that a rollup's security reduces to whether its data is really published. That makes data publication the scarce resource, and it is the reason Ethereum's roadmap since 2022 has been mostly about data rather than about computation. This module also closes a loop with coding theory: the erasure codes here are the same Reed–Solomon constructions used for storage reliability, read as a commitment rather than as redundancy.

Concepts

  • The data availability problem, stated as a distinct problem
    • A block producer publishes a state root and claims the data is available. If it is not, nobody can construct a fraud proof — so validity proofs do not help either, because users still cannot reconstruct their own balances. Availability is not validity and not integrity; it is a third property
    • Why you cannot prove availability directly: a prover can always release data to the challenger and withhold it from everyone else. The only escape is sampling by many independent parties
  • Erasure coding as the enabling trick
    • Extend n chunks to 2n with Reed–Solomon so any n reconstruct the whole. Now withholding requires hiding more than half the extended data, which random samplers detect with probability approaching 1 after a handful of queries. Compute that probability yourself — it is the heart of the mechanism
    • Fraud proofs of incorrect erasure coding, and why 2D encoding reduces their size — the point at which the construction stops being just a storage code
  • KZG polynomial commitments, and why they were chosen here
    • Constant-size commitment, constant-size opening proof at any point, homomorphic. That combination means a sampler can verify a single cell against the commitment without the rest of the block. The price is a trusted setup — the KZG Ceremony was run with over 140,000 participants precisely to make the "at least one honest participant" assumption credible
  • What actually shipped, in order
    • EIP-4844 (Dencun, March 2024): blobs as a separate transaction type with their own fee market, pruned after ~18 days, never accessible to the EVM. Separate pricing was the key design decision — rollup data no longer competes with execution for the same resource
    • PeerDAS (Fusaka, December 2025): validators sample columns rather than downloading every blob, which is what makes raising the blob count safe. Blob capacity then ramps via blob-parameter-only forks rather than full hard forks
    • Full danksharding as the destination: 2D sampling across the whole blob matrix, with proposers who never hold the full data. Not shipped; treat every timeline you read as a guess
  • The alternative DA layers and the trade they represent
    • Celestia, EigenDA, Avail: cheaper data with a different and smaller security set. A rollup posting data to an external DA layer is not an Ethereum rollup in the security sense, and the honest description is "as secure as the weaker of the two"

Work through

  • Fraud and Data Availability Proofs — the whole paper this time, including the 2D construction.
  • Constant-Size Commitments to Polynomials and Their Applications — sections 1–3, for the KZG scheme itself.
  • Proto-Danksharding FAQ — the whole document; it is the clearest account of why blobs are shaped the way they are.
  • PeerDAS on ethereum.org — for what actually shipped in Fusaka and what the ramp schedule is.
  • Danksharding on ethereum.org — for the destination architecture.
  • Danksharding Workshop (Devcon VI) — the workshop session, which covers the sampling argument at whiteboard level.

Practice

Implement Reed–Solomon extension over a prime field: take n data chunks, extend to 2n, then delete a random half and reconstruct. Then simulate sampling — an adversary hides a fraction f of the extended data, s samplers each draw k random cells — and plot detection probability against k for f just above 1/2. Done when your plot shows why fewer than 30 samples per client is enough, and when you can state the assumption about sampler independence that the whole argument rests on.

Where people go wrong

Assuming a validity proof makes data availability unnecessary. A ZK rollup with withheld data is provably correct and completely unusable: the state transitions are valid, and no user can compute their own balance or construct a withdrawal. This is precisely why Plasma failed and why validiums carry a real, nameable risk rather than a theoretical one. The second error is treating blobs as cheap permanent storage — they are pruned after about eighteen days, deliberately, and anything needing permanence must be reconstructed and stored by someone off-protocol.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why erasure coding turns "is this data available" from unanswerable into a sampling problem; and say why blob data is priced in its own fee market rather than as calldata, and what that changed for rollup costs.

M15

Zero-knowledge proofs, from arithmetization to the proving-cost economy

≈16 h

Succinct proofs are the one primitive that breaks the "everyone re-executes everything" constraint from M13, and they are also the hardest material on this page. The goal here is not to derive a pairing-based argument from scratch — it is to know what each proof system costs, what it assumes, and why a zkEVM is a different engineering problem from a SNARK. Placed after M13 so you already know what anyone is paying for.

Concepts

  • The shape of the whole construction, before any specific scheme
    • Computation → arithmetic circuit or execution trace → polynomial identity → commit to polynomials → prove identities at random points. Every modern system is this pipeline with different choices at each stage; learning the pipeline first stops the schemes blurring together
    • Interactive proofs, the Fiat–Shamir transform to make them non-interactive, and the fact that Fiat–Shamir is where implementations actually get broken (the weak-Fiat–Shamir attacks against under-specified transcripts)
  • Arithmetization, which is where the engineering lives
    • R1CS (Groth16, Marlin), Plonkish with custom gates and lookup arguments (PLONK, Halo2, plonky2), AIR (STARKs). Lookups are the practical breakthrough: they make range checks and bitwise operations affordable, which is the difference between a toy circuit and a zkEVM
    • Circuit size as the cost driver. Non-native field arithmetic — proving secp256k1 ECDSA inside a BN254 circuit, for example — costs orders of magnitude more than it looks like it should, and this single fact shapes what gets built
  • The three families and their real trade-offs
    • Groth16: smallest proofs (~200 bytes), cheapest verification, per-circuit trusted setup. Still used where the circuit is fixed and verification cost dominates
    • PLONK/Halo2: universal updatable setup, larger proofs, far better developer ergonomics. The workhorse
    • STARKs: no trusted setup, hash-based so plausibly post-quantum, larger proofs, faster proving at scale. Recursion is natural
    • Trusted setup honestly: toxic waste, powers-of-tau ceremonies, the 1-of-n honesty assumption, and why "trustless" is the wrong word for a setup with 140,000 participants but "trusted third party" is also wrong
  • Recursion and aggregation
    • Proving the verification of a proof: batches many proofs into one, enables continuous provers and cheap final on-chain verification. Cycles of curves, accumulation schemes, and folding (Nova) as the cost-reduction frontier
  • zkEVMs, and why the taxonomy matters
    • Type 1 (fully Ethereum-equivalent, slowest), Type 2, Type 2.5, Type 3, Type 4 (compile a high-level language, fastest, semantics diverge). The choice is a bet about whether developer compatibility or proving cost is the binding constraint
    • Proving cost economics: hardware acceleration, proof markets, and the question of whether proving centralises into a few industrial operators — the same shape of question as builders in M16
  • Uses beyond scaling
    • Private transactions (M19), light clients proving consensus, proof of solvency, and identity claims. Note that zero-knowledge is often not the point — succinctness is; many "ZK" systems in production hide nothing

Work through

  • Proofs, Arguments, and Zero-Knowledge — chapters 1–5 and 8–10. This is the reference; work the exercises in chapter 4.
  • Zero Knowledge Proofs MOOC — the whole lecture series. It is taught by the people who built these systems.
  • ZK Whiteboard Sessions — the first five sessions, as the gentler parallel track to Thaler.
  • Zero Knowledge Proofs Explained — watch this first if the paper stack looks forbidding; it establishes the pipeline shape without the algebra.
  • PLONK — sections 1–4. Read it after Thaler's chapter on polynomial IOPs, not before.
  • Scalable, Transparent, and Post-Quantum Secure Computational Integrity — sections 1–3 for the STARK design goals.
  • On the Size of Pairing-Based Non-Interactive Arguments — for Groth16, once the pipeline is familiar.
  • How trusted setups work — the clearest plain-language account of what the ceremony assumes.
  • Zero Knowledge Canon — as the curated index for going deeper on any one branch.
  • Circom — the documentation, used as the practical on-ramp to writing a circuit.
  • ZK Hack — the under-constrained-circuit puzzles, which teach the bug class that ordinary testing cannot see.
  • Zero Knowledge Podcast — the Boneh and Thaler episodes, for how practitioners talk about these trade-offs when not writing a paper.

Practice

Write a Circom circuit proving you know a preimage of a Poseidon hash, compile it, run the trusted setup, generate a proof and verify it — then deploy the generated Solidity verifier and verify on a testnet, measuring gas. Then write a second circuit that proves knowledge of a Merkle path into a fixed root, and measure how constraint count grows with tree depth. Done when you can state the marginal constraint cost per tree level and explain why SHA-256 inside a circuit costs so much more than Poseidon.

Where people go wrong

Assuming "zero-knowledge" means "private". In almost every rollup the property being used is succinctness — a short proof that a long computation was done correctly — and the inputs are entirely public. Calling these systems ZK is an accident of terminology that has confused a generation of readers. The second error is treating proof verification cost as the system's cost: verification is cheap and constant, proving is expensive and scales with the computation, and the economics of who runs provers is where centralisation risk actually sits.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what a trusted setup produces, what "toxic waste" is, and what an attacker who kept it can do; and say why a Type 1 zkEVM is slower than a Type 4 in terms of what each one has to prove.

M16

Block production, proposer–builder separation and the MEV plumbing

≈9 h

Ordering transactions is valuable, and whoever orders them captures that value. Left alone, this centralises block production into whoever is best at extraction — which is the opposite of what the protocol wants from its validator set. This module covers the plumbing that the ecosystem built in response: who builds blocks, who relays them, and what the protocol is trying to enshrine. The market — sandwiching, arbitrage, order-flow auctions and who loses money — is M12 of the sibling page, and the two are meant to be read together.

Concepts

  • Why extractable value is structural rather than a bug
    • Any system where a party chooses transaction order in a market with price-sensitive state has an ordering premium. It exists in traditional finance too; blockchains make it visible, permissionless and measurable, which is why the literature exists here
    • The vocabulary shift from "miner" to "maximal" extractable value, and why the change matters after the Merge
  • The supply chain as actually deployed
    • User → wallet/RPC → (public mempool or private order flow) → searcher → builder → relay → proposer. Each hop is a business, each hop can censor, and the proposer at the end is the only one the protocol knows about
    • mev-boost as out-of-protocol PBS: the proposer commits to a header before seeing the body, the relay holds the body and swaps it in. Relays exist purely to solve that trust gap and they are unpaid, unaccountable infrastructure — a genuinely uncomfortable dependency for a large share of Ethereum blocks
  • What centralises, and the measurements
    • Builder market share is highly concentrated, because building is a scale game: exclusive order flow plus better optimisation compounds. Track the current distribution rather than trusting a number in a syllabus
    • Timing games from M08 reappear here: proposers who delay maximise the auction and degrade attestation reliability
    • OFAC-compliant relays after the Tornado Cash sanctions produced a measurable censorship rate — the concrete case study for why censorship resistance needs a mechanism, not a norm
  • The protocol's answers, in flight
    • ePBS (EIP-7732): enshrine the proposer/builder split so no relay is needed. It is a headliner of the Glamsterdam upgrade, targeted for late 2026 — check the current status, this is a moving object
    • Inclusion lists / FOCIL (EIP-7805): let attesters force transactions into the next block, so a builder cannot censor unilaterally. Deliberately kept out of Glamsterdam to limit interaction risk and pushed to the following upgrade
    • Encrypted mempools (threshold encryption, delay encryption, trusted hardware) as the more ambitious answer: if the builder cannot read the transaction, it cannot front-run it. All approaches currently trade latency or add a trust assumption
    • MEV burn proposals to route extraction into the burn rather than to proposers, changing who is paid rather than whether extraction happens

Work through

  • Flash Boys 2.0 — the whole paper; it is the founding document and its consensus-instability argument is the part most often skipped.
  • Quantifying Blockchain Extractable Value — for the measurement methodology and magnitudes.
  • Flashbots Writings — the PBS and relay posts, read as primary sources from the people running the infrastructure.
  • mevboost.pics — current builder and relay market share. Look at it before forming any view.
  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — EIP-7732 (ePBS) and EIP-7805 (FOCIL).
  • ethresear.ch — the ePBS and inclusion-list threads, for the disagreements that the EIPs summarise away.
  • Time is Money: Strategic Timing Games in Proof-of-Stake Protocols — re-read here in its MEV framing.

Practice

Pull a week of blocks and label each by builder and relay from the payload headers. Compute the market-share distribution and the HHI. Then pick one block, decode its transactions, and identify a sandwich or a backrun if present — the signature is a pair of transactions from the same address bracketing a victim swap in the same block. Done when you can produce the concentration figure and one annotated bundle explaining who paid whom.

Where people go wrong

Thinking MEV is an Ethereum problem that a better chain design eliminates. Any chain with a leader and a price-sensitive state has it; chains with faster blocks have it in a different shape, not a smaller one, and chains with a single sequencer have simply given all of it to one party. The realistic goal is not elimination but distribution and censorship resistance — deciding who captures the value and ensuring nobody can use their position to exclude you. Reading the roadmap items in this module as attempts at elimination will make all of them look like failures.

Checkpoint

M17

Alternative L1 architectures: the same problem, four different answers

≈11 h

Everything so far has one worked example, and a reader who stops here will believe an account-model EVM chain with sequential execution is what a blockchain is. It is one point in a design space. This module needs M07 and M09 as the baseline being departed from, and it is where M03's UTXO insight pays off: the chains that execute in parallel all had to reintroduce explicit read/write sets, which is what Bitcoin had in 2009 and Ethereum gave up.

Concepts

  • The parallel-execution problem, stated once so all four answers are comparable
    • Sequential execution wastes cores. To parallelise you must know which transactions conflict — which means either declaring state access up front (pessimistic) or executing speculatively and re-running on conflict (optimistic). Every design below picks one
    • The honest caveat that vendors omit: under high contention on one hot contract, parallel execution gains little and optimistic schemes can lose to re-execution overhead. Throughput benchmarks with uncorrelated synthetic transfers measure nothing about a real DEX
  • Solana: declare everything, run it hot
    • Sealevel requires every transaction to declare the accounts it reads and writes, so the scheduler can partition by conflict — the pessimistic answer. Programs are stateless and data lives in accounts, closer to an operating-system process model than to Ethereum's contract-owns-its-storage model
    • Proof of History as a verifiable delay-based clock that lets validators agree on ordering without round-trips, layered under Tower BFT. Local fee markets so congestion in one program does not price out the whole chain — a direct response to a real failure mode
    • What it costs: high hardware requirements for validators, historical liveness incidents, and a much smaller set of parties who can run one. This is the trilemma being paid in the decentralisation column, explicitly
  • Move chains: make the type system carry the safety
    • Move's linear resource types make assets values that cannot be copied or silently dropped — a whole class of M12 bugs becomes a compile error. The Move Prover lets you state and check invariants as part of the ordinary build
    • Sui's object-centric model: transactions touching only owned objects skip consensus entirely and settle by simple Byzantine broadcast, with full consensus reserved for shared objects. Narwhal for mempool, Bullshark for ordering
    • Aptos's Block-STM: optimistic parallel execution with software transactional memory, dynamic dependency estimation and deterministic re-execution. The contrast with Sealevel — optimistic versus pessimistic — is the cleanest comparison on this page
  • Cosmos: give every application its own chain
    • The application-specific chain thesis: instead of sharing a global VM, build a sovereign chain with the Cosmos SDK over CometBFT (Tendermint from M06), and connect chains with IBC — a genuine light-client protocol, not a multisig bridge, which makes it the security benchmark M18 measures others against
    • The cost: every chain needs its own validator set and its own security budget. Interchain security and replicated security exist to rent security from a larger chain, which reintroduces the shared-security question Cosmos set out to avoid
  • DAG-based consensus as the current research direction
    • Separate data dissemination from ordering: Narwhal builds a DAG of certified batches, Tusk or Bullshark impose an order on it with zero extra messages. This is the main structural idea distinguishing recent high-throughput designs from PBFT descendants

Work through

  • Solana: A New Architecture for a High Performance Blockchain — for Proof of History as originally argued, allowing for its age.
  • Solana documentation — the accounts model and transaction-processing pages, which are where Sealevel becomes concrete.
  • Block-STM — the whole paper. The evaluation section's contention analysis is the honest part.
  • Narwhal and Tusk — sections 1–4, for the mempool/ordering split.
  • Bullshark: DAG BFT Protocols Made Practical — for the zero-message-overhead ordering rule.
  • The Move Book — the resources and abilities chapters, which are the point of the language.
  • Sui documentation — the object model and consensus pages, for the owned-versus-shared distinction.
  • Cosmos SDK documentation — the architecture overview and module system.
  • IBC Protocol — the light-client and packet-lifecycle specifications.

Practice

Write the same trivial application — a token with transfer — three times: as a Solidity contract, as a Solana program with explicit account declarations, and as a Move module using a linear resource type. Then answer in writing: in which of the three is it possible to accidentally create tokens from nothing, and what specifically prevents it in the others? Done when you can point at the compiler mechanism, not the programmer's care.

Where people go wrong

Comparing chains by advertised TPS. Published figures are measured on uncorrelated synthetic transfers, sometimes in a lab, and say nothing about behaviour under contention on one hot contract — which is the only load that matters for a DEX, a mint or a liquidation cascade. The questions that actually differentiate these designs are: what is the validator hardware floor, what happens under contention, is finality accountable, and how many independent parties are running it. TPS answers none of them, and every one of these chains has a real engineering argument that the number obscures.

M18

Bridges, interoperability and shared security

≈8 h

M17 leaves you with many chains, and the moment there is more than one, moving value between them becomes the single most exploited component in the ecosystem — Ronin, Wormhole, Nomad, Poly and others account for a large fraction of all funds ever stolen on-chain. The reason is structural: a bridge must attest to facts about a foreign chain, and doing that without trusting anyone requires verifying that chain's consensus, which is expensive. Most bridges skip it.

Concepts

  • What a bridge is actually asserting
    • "Event E happened on chain A" — a claim about a foreign state machine. The trust spectrum runs from verifying A's consensus in a light client on B (trust-minimised), through economic bonds and optimistic challenge periods, to an external committee signing (trusted)
    • Lock-and-mint versus burn-and-mint versus liquidity-network designs, and the fact that the wrapped asset's backing is only as good as the custody arrangement holding the original
  • The exploit record, read for the pattern
    • Ronin: five of nine multisig keys compromised via social engineering — no contract bug at all. Wormhole: a signature-verification flaw let an attacker forge a validator-approval message. Nomad: a botched initialisation made every message valid, and the exploit became a crowd. Poly Network: a privileged-role confusion
    • The pattern: the bugs are in the attestation layer, not the token logic, and several were operational rather than in code. This should change what you look at first when evaluating one
  • Light-client bridges, and why they are rare
    • IBC as the working example: each chain runs a light client of the other and verifies headers, with the security reducing to the two chains' consensus plus a liveness assumption on relayers. It works because Tendermint has fast, accountable finality and cheap header verification
    • Why the same approach is hard between heterogeneous chains: verifying Ethereum consensus on another chain means checking BLS aggregate signatures over a large validator set, which is expensive — succinct proofs (M15) of consensus are the current route, and sync committees are the pragmatic shortcut
  • Shared security and restaking
    • EigenLayer's proposition: let staked ETH be re-pledged to secure other services, with additional slashing conditions. It converts Ethereum's security budget into a rentable resource for bridges, DA layers and oracles
    • The risk everyone names and few quantify: correlated slashing across services, and the systemic question of what happens when a large share of stake is committed to obligations that can fail together. This is an unresolved argument, not a solved design
  • The L2 case, which is different and often conflated
    • A rollup's canonical bridge is not a bridge in this sense — it is the L1 verifying the L2's state transitions directly (M13), so there is no external attestation. Third-party "fast bridges" between L2s are liquidity networks that front you funds and are a genuinely different trust object, even though the user interface is identical

Work through

  • SoK: Communication Across Distributed Ledgers — the whole paper; it is the taxonomy the rest of the literature uses.
  • IBC Protocol — the light-client specification, as the trust-minimised reference point.
  • Arbitrum documentation — the bridging section, to see how a canonical rollup bridge differs from an external one.
  • rekt.news — the bridge entries on the leaderboard. Read four post-mortems and classify each failure as code, key management or operational.
  • Possible futures of the Ethereum protocol — the sections on interoperability and chain abstraction.

Practice

Pick three bridges with different designs and write a one-page trust analysis of each: exactly who must be honest for your funds to be safe, how many of them, what they are bonded with, and what the recovery path is if they are not. Then rank them and defend the ranking against the objection "but that one has never been hacked". Done when each analysis names a specific set of keys, validators or contracts rather than a brand.

Where people go wrong

Assuming a wrapped asset is the asset. Wrapped BTC on Ethereum is a claim on a custodian; a bridged USDC on an L2 may be canonical (issued by the issuer) or third-party (backed by locked tokens on another chain), and the two trade at the same ticker in the same wallet interface while having entirely different failure modes. Users have repeatedly discovered this during a depeg rather than before one, and the interface will not tell you — you have to read the token contract.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why IBC can be trust-minimised while most Ethereum bridges are not, in terms of the cost of verifying the counterparty's consensus; and say what specifically restaking adds to a bridge's security and what new correlated risk it introduces.

M19

Privacy on a public ledger

≈9 h

A public chain publishes every transaction forever, which makes it the most surveillable financial system ever built — pseudonymity is not anonymity, and clustering heuristics defeat it routinely. This module needs M15 because every serious answer is built on succinct proofs, and it is placed here rather than earlier because the legal material only makes sense once you know exactly what the technology does and does not do.

Concepts

  • Why pseudonymity fails in practice
    • Common-input-ownership and change-output heuristics cluster addresses into entities; exchange KYC provides the identity anchor; timing and amount correlation links the rest. Chain-analysis firms do this commercially and it works well
    • The account model is worse than UTXO here — address reuse is the default rather than a mistake
  • The two design families
    • Ring signatures and confidential transactions (Monero): hide sender in an anonymity set, hide amounts with Pedersen commitments plus range proofs, hide recipient with stealth addresses. Privacy by default, which matters — an optional privacy feature has a small anonymity set by construction
    • Shielded pools (Zcash): a commitment tree of notes plus nullifiers to prevent double-spends, with a zk-SNARK proving a valid spend without revealing which note. Strong, and undermined by low shielded-pool usage — the anonymity set is only as big as the people using it
  • Privacy on a chain that was not designed for it
    • Mixers and shielded pools as contracts (Tornado Cash's design: deposit a commitment, withdraw with a proof of membership plus a nullifier). Association sets and proofs-of-innocence as the compliance-compatible variant
    • Stealth addresses (ERC-5564) for receiving without a public link; private mempools as transaction-level rather than settlement-level privacy
  • The legal layer, stated as fact rather than opinion
    • OFAC sanctioned Tornado Cash's addresses in August 2022 — the first sanctioning of software rather than persons. In Van Loon (Fifth Circuit, November 2024) the court held that the immutable smart contracts were not "property" of a foreign national and so could not be designated; OFAC delisted the addresses in March 2025
    • The developer-liability question is separate and still live in several jurisdictions. What is settled is narrower than either side's summary of it
  • The adjacent cryptography, and its current maturity
    • FHE for computing on encrypted state, MPC for shared secrets across parties, and trusted execution environments as the fast-but-trusting option. All three appear in production marketing well ahead of their production readiness; read benchmark numbers rather than announcements

Work through

  • Zerocash: Decentralized Anonymous Payments from Bitcoin — sections 1–4, for the commitment/nullifier construction that everything else copies.
  • Proofs, Arguments, and Zero-Knowledge — the zero-knowledge chapters specifically, now read for privacy rather than succinctness.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapter 6 on anonymity, which is still the clearest account of the deanonymisation heuristics.
  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — ERC-5564 stealth addresses, as the minimal-privacy-on-Ethereum proposal.
  • Possible futures of the Ethereum protocol — the privacy sections, for where the roadmap places this.

Practice

Take a public address with a few hundred transactions and cluster it: apply common-input-ownership and change heuristics, and see how many distinct addresses you can attribute to the same entity. Then, separately, build a toy shielded pool — a Merkle tree of commitments, nullifiers, and a circuit proving membership without revealing the leaf — reusing your M15 Circom work. Done when your deanonymisation write-up states its confidence and its false-positive risk honestly, and when a double-spend attempt against your pool fails on the nullifier check.

Where people go wrong

Believing a mixer provides privacy independent of how many people use it. Anonymity is a set size, not a property of the cryptography: a shielded pool with twelve daily users gives you a one-in-twelve guess, no matter how good the proof system is. Timing, amounts and the behaviour immediately before and after deposit routinely reduce that set further. This is why privacy-by-default designs are structurally stronger than privacy-as-a-feature, and why usage statistics are the number to look at first.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what a nullifier is and why a shielded pool cannot work without one; and state precisely what the Van Loon holding decided and what it did not.

M20

Running the network: clients, state growth and the road to statelessness

≈8 h

The security arguments in M04 and M07 all quietly assume many independent parties can afford to validate. That assumption is under continuous pressure from state growth, and it is the reason a chunk of the roadmap is about data structures rather than about features. This module needs M09's trie material, and it is where the abstract phrase "decentralisation" becomes a number: the cost of the machine required to check the chain yourself.

Concepts

  • Client diversity as a live safety property
    • If one execution or consensus client holds a supermajority, a consensus bug in it finalises an invalid chain rather than halting. The safe threshold is below 33% per client; the ecosystem has repeatedly been above it, and the two real incidents (the 2016 Geth/Parity split, and the Prysm-dominance scares) are the evidence
    • Why diversity is hard to buy: the dominant client is usually the best-documented and best-supported, so every individual operator's rational choice is the collectively bad one
  • State growth as the binding cost
    • State is the part that cannot be pruned — every account and storage slot must be available to execute the next block. It grows monotonically, disk requirements grow with it, and random-access reads against a deep Merkle Patricia trie are the profiling hotspot in every client
    • History versus state: EIP-4444 expires old history at the protocol level and pushes it to archival parties (portal network, individual archives). This is a deliberate decision that full history is not every node's job
  • Verkle and binary trees, and what problem they solve
    • Witness size, not lookup speed. A stateless client needs a proof of every state item a block touches; MPT witnesses are far too large to gossip. Verkle trees use vector commitments to shrink witnesses by roughly an order of magnitude; the more recent direction favours binary trees with hash-based commitments for post-quantum reasons — check the current state of that decision rather than assuming Verkle
  • Statelessness and its endgame
    • Weak statelessness: block producers hold state, validators verify with witnesses. This decouples the cost of proposing from the cost of validating, which is exactly the M13/Endgame principle applied to the L1
    • State expiry proposals, address-period schemes, and the resurrection problem — what happens to a contract nobody touched for two years
  • Light clients and the RPC centralisation nobody planned
    • Sync committees make Ethereum light clients feasible; Helios verifies rather than trusts an RPC endpoint. This matters because the practical reality is that most wallets and dapps read the chain through a handful of commercial RPC providers, which is a censorship and integrity surface that no amount of consensus decentralisation addresses

Work through

  • clientdiversity.org — current distribution, then the "why it matters" material.
  • Verkle trees — Buterin's explainer, for why witness size is the metric.
  • Ethereum roadmap — the statelessness and Verge sections.
  • Possible futures of the Ethereum protocol — The Verge and The Purge sections in full.
  • Ethereum consensus specs — the light-client specification, for what a sync committee actually proves.
  • Ethereum developer documentation — the node and client architecture pages, for the execution/consensus client split in practice.

Practice

Sync a full node from scratch — an execution client plus a consensus client — and record wall-clock time, peak disk, peak memory and final database size. Then run a light client against the same network and compare what each can answer. Done when you can state the current cost in hours and gigabytes for a full sync, and name one query the light client cannot serve without trusting someone.

Where people go wrong

Counting nodes as a decentralisation measure. What matters is how many independent parties validate and how cheap it is to join them — a network with 10,000 nodes that all run one client on one cloud provider and read through three RPC endpoints is centralised in every way that can actually fail. The related mistake is assuming a hardware requirement that is affordable today stays affordable: state grows monotonically, so a floor that excludes nobody now excludes hobbyists in five years unless something is done, which is precisely what this module's roadmap items are for.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a supermajority client is a safety risk rather than merely a liveness risk; and say what a stateless validator needs to receive along with a block, and why witness size — not proof time — is the binding constraint.

M21

Protocol governance: how a change to an immutable system actually ships

≈8 h

Every mechanism on this page was chosen by a process, and the process is not in any specification. There is no on-chain vote for protocol changes on either Bitcoin or Ethereum; there is a rough-consensus process among client teams, researchers and node operators, backstopped by the fact that users choose which software to run. Read the two defining crises — the block size war and the DAO fork — as the empirical evidence for what that process can and cannot do.

Concepts

  • Soft forks and hard forks, defined by validity rather than by drama
    • A soft fork tightens the rules, so old nodes still accept new blocks — backwards compatible, and deployable by a miner/validator majority. A hard fork loosens or changes them, so everyone must upgrade or split. This is the whole distinction and it decides who has to coordinate
    • Activation mechanisms and their politics: BIP-9 version bits, BIP-8, the user-activated soft fork, Ethereum's coordinated block-height forks. The activation mechanism is a statement about who is allowed to veto
  • The processes as they run
    • BIPs and the Bitcoin mailing list; EIPs, All Core Devs calls, Ethereum Magicians and ethresear.ch. Follow one EIP from draft to inclusion and observe that the decisive conversations happen on calls and in threads, not in the document
    • Client teams as the real veto point: an EIP nobody implements does not ship, and this is a small number of organisations. Whether that is a governance flaw or the only thing preventing capture is a genuine open argument
  • The block size war, 2015–2017
    • The substance — larger blocks raise throughput and raise the cost of running a node (M05 and M20's material, argued in public for two years) — plus the process question of whether miners, exchanges, businesses or users decide. SegWit activated via UASF pressure; Bitcoin Cash forked off; the small-block position held
    • The transferable lesson: economic nodes, not hash power, ultimately determined which chain carried the name. Read this against M04's assumption that miners decide
  • The DAO fork, 2016
    • A re-entrancy bug (M12) drained a large contract; the community hard-forked to reverse it; the minority who refused became Ethereum Classic. This is the field's cleanest test of "code is law" as a claim about what actually happens, and the answer was no
    • The precedent question that still has no settled answer: what size and kind of loss justifies a state-changing fork, and who decides
  • Upgrade cadence today
    • Named upgrades bundling EIPs — Dencun (blobs), Pectra (EIP-7702, EIP-7251), Fusaka (PeerDAS), Glamsterdam (ePBS, block-level access lists), with FOCIL pushed to the following upgrade. Bundling is how scope is contained, and scope fights are the main reason dates move
    • Blob-parameter-only forks as a lighter-weight mechanism for tuning a single parameter without a full upgrade cycle — a process innovation as much as a technical one

Work through

  • The Blocksize War — the whole book. Bier serialised it free; it is the best-documented account of a governance crisis in this field.
  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — EIP-1 (the process itself), then follow one recent EIP through its whole history.
  • Ethereum Magicians — pick a contentious EIP thread and read it end to end.
  • Ethereum Protocol on YouTube — one All Core Devs call in full. It is less formal than you expect, and that is the point.
  • Bitcoin Optech — the newsletter archive across a soft-fork activation period, for the contrasting culture.
  • Ethereum Foundation Blog — the upgrade announcement posts for Dencun, Pectra and Fusaka, read as the official record of what each bundle actually contained.
  • Protocol Priorities Update for 2026 — for how priorities are currently being set and communicated.

Practice

Pick an EIP that was proposed and did not ship. Reconstruct its history: who proposed it, what the objection was, where the objection was raised, and what the deciding argument turned out to be. Write it up in a page. Done when you can name the specific forum where it stalled and the specific technical or social objection that stopped it — "lack of consensus" is not an answer.

Where people go wrong

Believing that because a chain has no formal governance, it has no governance. It has an informal one with real veto points — client teams, large staking operators, exchanges deciding which fork keeps the ticker — and informal power is harder to audit than formal power, not weaker. The opposite error, common among people arriving from token governance (M18–M19 of the sibling page), is assuming on-chain voting would be an improvement; the evidence there is that token voting concentrates power faster and more quietly than rough consensus does.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a soft fork needs only a majority of block producers while a hard fork needs everyone, and what that implies about who can veto each; and say what the DAO fork established as precedent and what it deliberately left unsettled.

M22

The frontier: what is unsolved, contested, or about to change

≈8 h

A syllabus that ends at the current implementation teaches the reader to be confidently out of date in eighteen months. This module is the list of things that are genuinely open — where the research is live, the arguments are unresolved, and any statement you read (including in this syllabus) has a shelf life. It exists so you leave knowing which of your beliefs to hold loosely.

Concepts

  • Single-slot finality, and what it would cost
    • Today finality takes two epochs — roughly 13 minutes. SSF would finalise every slot, removing reorgs and the weak-subjectivity window's practical bite. The obstacle is signature aggregation across the full validator set every 12 seconds, which is why it is entangled with validator-set-size proposals and with the Beam Chain redesign discussion
  • Enshrined PBS and censorship resistance
    • Whether ePBS plus inclusion lists actually delivers censorship resistance, or relocates the problem into the builder market, is not settled — and it is being decided by shipping rather than by proof. Track Glamsterdam and the upgrade after it
  • Post-quantum migration, the deadline nobody has scheduled
    • ECDSA and BLS both fall to a sufficiently large quantum computer; hash-based signatures and STARKs do not. The problem is not the primitive — replacements exist — it is that funds in addresses whose owners are gone cannot be migrated, and every migration plan is therefore also a confiscation policy. This is a governance problem wearing a cryptography costume
    • Timelines are unknowable; the engineering response is to prefer hash-based constructions in new designs, which is part of why the state-tree direction moved away from pairing-based commitments
  • Based and native rollups, and the L2 endgame
    • Based rollups hand sequencing to L1 proposers, removing the sequencer as a trusted party; native rollups would have the L1 verify EVM execution directly via a precompile. Both would collapse much of M13's trust surface, and both are proposals rather than systems
    • The unresolved economic question: if L2s capture the fees and L1 sells only data, what funds L1 security in the long run? Nobody has a convincing answer and the honest position is that this is a live risk
  • Proving as an industry
    • If real-time proving of every L1 block becomes cheap enough, the distinction between L1 and a ZK rollup blurs. If proving hardware centralises into a handful of operators, the ecosystem has re-created the builder problem in a new layer. Both are plausible; watch cost-per-proof trends rather than announcements
  • The questions that are not technical
    • Whether fee revenue can ever replace issuance as a security budget on a chain with fixed supply — the Bitcoin security-budget argument, which has a hard deadline and no agreed solution
    • Whether any of this decentralises in practice or converges on a few large operators at every layer: staking, building, sequencing, proving, RPC. The consistent empirical finding across M08, M16, M13 and M20 is concentration; the consistent hope is that verification stays cheap enough for it not to matter. That is the field's central open bet and you should be able to argue both sides

Work through

  • Possible futures of the Ethereum protocol — the whole series. It is the most structured statement of what remains open.
  • Protocol Priorities Update for 2026 — current priorities, which differ from the 2022 roadmap in instructive ways.
  • Ethereum roadmap — including the single-slot-finality and statelessness pages.
  • Verifiable Delay Functions — for the randomness problem that SSF and leader election keep running into.
  • ethresear.ch — the current front page. Whatever is being argued there is more current than this page.
  • Ethereum Protocol Fellowship wiki — the study-group material, which is the best-organised map of open protocol work.
  • Devcon Archive — the most recent research talks, which run about a year ahead of written material.
  • Beam Chain keynote, Devcon SEA — a live example of a roadmap item entering the discourse. Note how much of it is provisional.

Practice

Write a two-page position paper on the long-run security budget question for a fixed-supply chain: state the arithmetic, state the strongest argument that fee revenue suffices, state the strongest argument that it does not, and commit to a view. Then do the same exercise for "will proving centralise". Done when a reader who disagrees with your conclusion would still say you stated their side fairly.

Where people go wrong

Treating roadmap items as scheduled deliverables. Sharded execution was on the roadmap for years and was abandoned; Verkle trees were the settled answer to statelessness until the post-quantum argument reopened it; FOCIL was in Glamsterdam until scope risk pushed it out. The research process is genuinely open-ended, and the correct posture toward any named future upgrade is that its scope will change and its date will slip. Cite the specification, not the announcement — and re-check anything in this module against the current source before repeating it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why post-quantum migration is harder as a governance problem than as a cryptography problem; and name three things in this syllabus that you expect to be wrong within two years, with a reason for each.

Capstone

Build a working chain, then break someone else's. Two projects, and the second is not optional — building teaches you the happy path, and attacking is the only thing that teaches you which invariants were load-bearing.

  • Project 1 — a chain from scratch: a peer-to-peer node with gossip, an account-model state machine with a metered VM (a dozen opcodes is enough), a mempool with fee-based ordering, and a consensus layer. Implement it twice: once with longest-chain proof of work, once with a Tendermint-style BFT protocol over a fixed validator set. Run both across at least four processes with an injected network partition.
  • Draws on: M02, M03, M04, M05, M06, M07, M09.
  • Done when: the PoW version survives a partition by forking and reconverging on heal; the BFT version survives it by halting and resuming without ever finalising two conflicting blocks; and you can produce the log evidence for both claims.
  • Stretch: add a light client that verifies headers and Merkle proofs only, then add an optimistic rollup on top — an off-chain sequencer posting batches plus state roots, an on-chain challenge window, and a working fraud proof. Then attack it: withhold the batch data and demonstrate that the fraud proof cannot be constructed.

Project 2 — an adversarial protocol review. Take a deployed protocol you did not write, with at least $10M in it, and produce a review that a security firm would recognise: the full privileged-role inventory with addresses, the invariants stated formally, an invariant fuzz suite that runs against a mainnet fork, the oracle dependencies with their manipulation costs, and the upgrade path with its timelock. Finish with the one paragraph that matters: given all of the above, what is the cheapest way to steal from this?

  • Draws on: M09, M10, M11, M12, M13, M16.
  • Done when: your fuzz suite runs green against the current deployment and you can name a specific state — reachable or not — in which a stated invariant fails.

Mastery check

  • I can state the three Bitcoin Backbone properties and explain which one confirmation depth is a statement about.
  • I can derive the n > 3f bound for Byzantine agreement and explain why crash faults need only n > 2f.
  • I can explain what FLP forbids and name the specific escape each of PBFT, Tendermint and Nakamoto consensus used.
  • I can explain why Gasper composes two protocols, and what each one contributes.
  • I can state what weak subjectivity is, why proof of stake needs it, and why that is a permanent property rather than a bug.
  • I can compute the storage slot of a mapping entry by hand and read the value out of a live contract.
  • I can explain what DELEGATECALL changes about execution context and how it enabled both proxies and the Parity freeze.
  • I can describe read-only re-entrancy and explain why a reentrancy guard on the victim does not stop it.
  • I can explain what an optimistic rollup's challenge window is measuring, and why a validity proof does not remove the need for data availability.
  • I can explain how erasure coding plus random sampling converts data availability into a detectable property, and state the assumption the argument rests on.
  • I can distinguish succinctness from zero-knowledge and say which one rollups actually use.
  • I can describe the MEV supply chain hop by hop and name who can censor at each hop.
  • I can compare Sealevel, Block-STM and Sui's object model as three answers to parallel execution, and name where each degrades.
  • I can evaluate a bridge's trust model by naming the specific parties who must be honest.
  • I can explain why a supermajority client is a safety risk, not just a liveness risk.
  • I can explain why a soft fork needs only a producer majority and a hard fork needs universal upgrade.

Where to go next

  • DeFi, NFTs and On-Chain Economics — the direct continuation. This page taught the machine; that one teaches what was built on it and whether it works: automated market makers, lending and liquidation, stablecoins, oracles, MEV as a market rather than as plumbing, NFTs, DAOs and the regulatory perimeter. M16 here and its M12 are deliberately two halves of one subject.
  • Information Theory — M14's erasure coding is Reed–Solomon read as a commitment rather than as redundancy, and the sampling argument is a coding-theory argument. The information-theory page develops the machinery properly, along with the channel-coding results that explain why those codes have the parameters they do.
  • Distributed systems, properly — M06 is a tour of the results a blockchain engineer needs, not a course. Replication, linearizability, consistency models, and the practical engineering of consensus in systems where the participants are not adversarial is a whole discipline, and it makes the permissionless case legible as a special case rather than as the norm.
  • Applied cryptography — M02 and M15 use primitives as black boxes with stated guarantees. The security definitions, reduction proofs and implementation pitfalls behind those guarantees are their own field, and the gap between "the scheme is secure" and "this implementation is secure" is where most real cryptographic failures live.
  • Programming language theory and formal methods — M12's verification tools and M17's Move type system are both PL results applied to money. Type systems, abstract interpretation, model checking and separation logic are the upstream material, and this is the most under-supplied skill in the ecosystem relative to demand.
  • Mechanism design and auction theory — EIP-1559, proposer–builder separation and order-flow auctions are auction designs, and the field has a century of results about what those designs do under strategic behaviour. Roughgarden's work on transaction fee mechanism design is the direct bridge from this page.
