Generative AI Beyond Language

Learning a distribution well enough to draw new samples from it is a harder problem than predicting the next token, and the field spent a decade producing four incompatible answers before diffusion won. This syllabus follows that lineage properly — autoregressive models, VAEs, flows, GANs, score matching, diffusion and flow matching — and then applies it: images, video, audio, 3D scenes, molecules and proteins. It ends on the parts that remain unsolved, of which evaluation is the most consequential.

What this topic is

Generative modelling is the problem of learning a probability distribution from samples well enough to draw new ones. Stated that way it sounds like density estimation, and for low-dimensional data it is — but a 512×512 image lives in a space of nearly 800,000 dimensions where the data occupies a vanishingly thin manifold, and every method in this syllabus is a different bargain struck with that fact. Autoregressive models get exact likelihoods and pay in sequential sampling. VAEs get a latent space and pay in a bound rather than the likelihood itself. Flows get both exact likelihood and fast sampling and pay in architectural constraint. GANs abandon likelihood entirely for sample quality and pay in training instability. Diffusion converts one hard problem into thousands of easy ones and pays in sampling cost — which is the bargain that turned out to be the right one.

The reason to learn the whole lineage rather than only diffusion is that diffusion is not a departure from it. Score matching came out of energy-based models, the denoising objective is a VAE-style variational bound, the sampler is an SDE solver, and flow matching is the same object rewritten as a vector field — a rewriting that made the training objective simpler and now dominates new work. Meeting diffusion at the end of that chain makes it comprehensible; meeting it first makes it a recipe. The second half of the page is application, where the interesting content is what each modality forces you to change: video needs temporal consistency, audio needs a codec, 3D needs a rendering equation in the loop, and molecules need equivariance to rotation.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes the deep-learning fundamentals from this library's Deep Learning page — backpropagation, optimisers, normalisation, convolutions, the transformer, and enough training practice to debug a run. It also assumes probability at the level of densities, change of variables, KL divergence and expectations, and calculus including partial derivatives and the divergence of a vector field. Stochastic differential equations appear in M08 and are developed from scratch there; you do not need them in advance.

  • The transformer and U-Net architectures, both of which appear as diffusion backbones in M12 — Deep Learning M07 and M09 cover them, and Understanding Deep Learning Chapters 10 and 12 are the compressed version.
  • KL divergence, variational bounds and the reparameterisation trick — M03 needs all three. Deep Learning Chapters 3 and 19 supply them.
  • CLIP and contrastive learning, assumed from M09 onward as the conditioning mechanism — Deep Learning M14.
  • Basic numerical integration — Euler and Heun methods — since every diffusion sampler is an ODE or SDE solver and M09 treats them as such.

Not covered here: language models. Autoregressive text generation, tokenisation, pretraining, RLHF, inference serving and everything else in that stack is on Generative AI: Large Language Models. The one deliberate exception is discrete diffusion, which M22 treats because it belongs to this page's mathematical lineage even though its application is text. Also absent: the deep-learning fundamentals themselves, and the classical statistical density estimation — kernel density estimation, mixture models, the curse of dimensionality — which is on Classical Machine Learning and which M01 assumes as background rather than teaching.

Syllabus

M01

The generative modelling problem, and the taxonomy of answers

≈5 h

Every model family in this syllabus is a different resolution of the same tension between three things you cannot have at once: exact likelihood, fast sampling, and an unconstrained architecture. Installing that trilemma now means every later family arrives as a considered position rather than as a new technique, and it makes the diffusion modules' design choices legible.

Concepts

  • What "learning a distribution" means and why it is hard here
    • Density estimation versus sampling: some methods give you p(x), some give you samples, and very few give both cheaply
    • The manifold hypothesis — real data occupies a low-dimensional manifold in a high-dimensional ambient space, so the true density is degenerate and any model assigning density to the ambient space is modelling mostly empty regions
    • Maximum likelihood and its consequence: KL(data ‖ model) is mode-covering, so a likelihood-trained model puts mass everywhere the data is, including between modes, which is why likelihood-based samples look blurry
    • The reverse KL is mode-seeking, which is what GANs approximately optimise and why their samples are sharp and their coverage is poor. This one asymmetry explains a decade of the field's behaviour
  • The taxonomy, which is the module's export
    • Explicit and tractable: autoregressive models, normalizing flows — exact likelihood, at the cost of sequential sampling or architectural constraint
    • Explicit and approximate: VAEs (variational bound), energy-based models (unnormalised, needing MCMC), diffusion (a bound that is tight enough)
    • Implicit: GANs — no density at all, only a sampler, and therefore no likelihood to evaluate or compare
    • The generative trilemma: high sample quality, mode coverage, and fast sampling. Until diffusion, every family gave up one; diffusion gave up speed and the last five years have been spent buying it back, which is what M10 and M11 are
  • Conditioning, which is what makes any of it useful — class-conditional, text-conditional, image-conditional, and the general framing of a generative model as a sampler from p(x | c)
  • The evaluation problem, flagged now and developed in M20 — there is no held-out likelihood you can trust and no metric that captures perceptual quality, so this field's central methodological difficulty is that it cannot reliably measure its own progress

Work through

  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — Chapter 1, for the problem framed the way the rest of this page will use it.
  • Deep Learning — Chapter 20 sections 20.9–20.14, which is the pre-diffusion taxonomy laid out by people who built several of the families.
  • Deep Generative Modeling — Chapter 1, for the taxonomy with the tradeoffs tabulated.
  • CS236: Deep Generative Models — lecture 1 and 2, which set up exactly this framing as a course.
  • CMU 10-799: Diffusion and Flow Matching — the schedule page alone is worth reading now: it shows which of this page's twenty-two modules a current graduate course treats as one week's material.
  • Awesome Diffusion Models — skim the top-level categories, not the entries. The shape of the bibliography is the taxonomy this module is arguing for, arrived at independently.

Practice

On a 2D toy distribution you can plot — a mixture of eight Gaussians in a ring, or a two-moons dataset — fit four models: a Gaussian mixture by EM, a small autoregressive model over discretised coordinates, a simple normalizing flow, and a small GAN. Plot samples and, where possible, the learned density. You are done when you can see mode-covering blur in the likelihood-trained models and mode dropping in the GAN, in your own figures.

Where people go wrong

Comparing models across families by likelihood. GANs have no likelihood at all; VAEs report a lower bound whose tightness is unknown and varies with the model; flows and autoregressive models report exact values that are not comparable across different discretisations of the data. A likelihood table spanning families is measuring several different quantities and presenting them in one column. The families are comparable on samples and on downstream use, and barely otherwise.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: place a model you have not met before in the taxonomy from a description of its training objective, and predict from that placement whether its failure will look like blur or like missing modes.

M02

Autoregressive models and discrete latents

≈6 h

Autoregressive modelling is the one family that transfers directly from the language page, and it is worth seeing applied to pixels and audio because the failure — sequential sampling that costs one forward pass per dimension — is what motivates everything else. It also introduces vector quantisation, which is how autoregressive modelling was rescued for images and which reappears as the audio codec in M15 and as the latent space in M12.

Concepts

  • Autoregression on non-text data
    • Impose an ordering — raster scan for images, time for audio — and factorise the joint into conditionals. Exact likelihood, stable training, no adversarial game
    • PixelRNN and PixelCNN: masked convolutions enforcing the causal structure, and the blind-spot problem that gated variants fixed
    • WaveNet: dilated causal convolutions giving an exponentially growing receptive field over raw audio samples, and the sampling cost — one forward pass per audio sample, which at 16 kHz is why the original was unusable in real time
    • The ordering is arbitrary and the model is sensitive to it, which is an honest weakness of the whole family on data with no natural order
  • Vector quantisation, the fix that made this practical
    • VQ-VAE: an encoder producing continuous vectors, a learned codebook, nearest-neighbour quantisation, and a decoder — turning an image into a small grid of discrete codes
    • The straight-through estimator for backpropagating through the non-differentiable quantisation, plus the commitment loss that keeps the encoder near the codebook
    • Codebook collapse — most entries unused — and the standard mitigations: exponential moving average updates, dead-code restarting, and lower-dimensional codes
    • The two-stage recipe: compress to discrete tokens, then model those tokens autoregressively. A 256×256 image becomes a 32×32 grid of codes, which is a thousandfold reduction in sequence length
    • VQ-GAN adding a perceptual and adversarial loss to the autoencoder, which sharpens reconstructions and is the direct ancestor of M12's latent diffusion
  • Where autoregressive generation still wins — exact likelihood for compression and anomaly detection, native handling of variable-length outputs, and a training objective with no instability at all
  • The unification worth noting for M22: once data is tokenised, images, audio and text are the same modelling problem, which is the argument behind every unified multimodal model

Work through

  • Neural Discrete Representation Learning — the VQ-VAE paper. Read the straight-through estimator and the commitment loss carefully; both recur throughout this page.
  • WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio — dilated causal convolutions, and the sampling-cost problem stated plainly by its authors.
  • Deep Generative Modeling — the autoregressive chapter, which is the cleanest textbook treatment with code.
  • CS236: Deep Generative Models — the autoregressive lectures, for the likelihood framing done properly.
  • CS236 lecture notes — the autoregressive-models chapter, for the chain-rule factorisation stated once and cleanly before the architectures pile up.

Practice

Train a VQ-VAE on a small image dataset and inspect the codebook: how many entries are actually used, and what happens to reconstructions as you shrink the codebook or the latent grid. Then fit a small autoregressive model over the discrete codes and sample from it. You are done when you can show codebook collapse happening, fix it with EMA updates or dead-code restarts, and state the compression ratio your latent grid achieves.

Where people go wrong

Ignoring codebook utilisation. A VQ-VAE that uses 40 of its 1,024 codes will still train, still reconstruct passably, and will cripple the second stage — the autoregressive model over the codes has almost no vocabulary to work with, and the failure shows up as poor samples rather than as an error. Log the number of active codes every epoch; it is a one-line diagnostic that catches a silent and common failure.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why autoregressive image models were displaced despite having exact likelihoods and stable training, in terms of sampling cost — and explain what vector quantisation changes about that calculation.

M03

Variational autoencoders and the ELBO

≈6 h

The VAE is where latent-variable generative modelling becomes trainable by gradient descent, and its central object — the evidence lower bound — is the same bound that diffusion's training loss turns out to be. Reading DDPM in M07 without having derived an ELBO first means taking its loss on faith; having derived one here means recognising it. The VAE is also the encoder in M12's latent diffusion, so this is not only history.

Concepts

  • The latent-variable model and the intractability that motivates everything
    • p(x) = ∫ p(x | z) p(z) dz is intractable for any interesting decoder, and so therefore is the posterior p(z | x)
    • Variational inference: introduce an approximate posterior q(z | x) and optimise a bound instead of the thing you wanted
  • The ELBO, derived rather than quoted
    • Via Jensen's inequality, and equivalently as log p(x) − KL(q ‖ p(z|x)) — which makes the gap explicit: the bound is loose by exactly the divergence between your approximate and true posteriors
    • The two-term reading: a reconstruction term and a KL term pulling q toward the prior. Every VAE pathology is a fight between those two
    • The reparameterisation trick — sample ε from a fixed distribution and compute z = μ + σ ⊙ ε — which makes the sampling step differentiable. This is the single technical move that made VAEs trainable, and it is worth deriving why the naive score-function estimator has too much variance
  • The pathologies, and what each tells you
    • Blurry reconstructions: a Gaussian likelihood with fixed variance is a squared-error loss, which under multimodal uncertainty produces the average of the plausible outputs. Not a fixable bug, a consequence of the likelihood choice
    • Posterior collapse: with a sufficiently expressive decoder the model ignores z entirely, driving the KL term to zero and turning the VAE into an unconditional decoder. KL annealing, free bits and weakened decoders are the standard responses
    • The prior–aggregate-posterior mismatch: sampling from the prior lands in regions the decoder never saw during training, producing worse samples than reconstructions
    • β-VAE and the disentanglement literature, plus the later result that unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive biases — a useful corrective
  • Where VAEs remain the right tool — as an autoencoder providing a compact continuous latent space for another model to work in, which is exactly the role in M12, and where the blur is acceptable because the second model supplies the detail

Work through

  • Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes — the original. Read section 2 for the derivation and the reparameterisation argument; it is short and repays care.
  • An Introduction to Variational Autoencoders — Kingma and Welling's own tutorial, which is clearer than the original paper and covers the later developments.
  • Deep Generative Modeling — the VAE chapter, with runnable code and the pathologies demonstrated.
  • Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning — Chapters 9 and 10, for the variational-inference foundations underneath the ELBO.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 17, which derives the ELBO with the clearest figures in any textbook — read it if the bound's two-term decomposition does not yet feel forced rather than chosen.
  • CS236 assignments — homework 2, the VAE problem set. Do the written half: the reparameterisation gradient is one of those derivations that has to pass through your own hand once.

Practice

Train a VAE on a small image dataset and produce four diagnostics: reconstructions, samples from the prior, a 2D latent-space traversal, and the KL term per latent dimension over training. Then deliberately induce posterior collapse by making the decoder much more powerful, and fix it with KL annealing. You are done when you can show the per-dimension KL going to zero in the collapsed run and recovering after the fix.

Where people go wrong

Trying to fix VAE blur with a bigger decoder. The blur comes from the likelihood: a Gaussian with fixed variance makes the loss squared error, and squared error under genuine multimodal uncertainty is minimised by the mean of the modes — which is a blurry image. More capacity produces a sharper mean, not a sample from a mode. The fixes are a different likelihood (discretised logistic, or an autoregressive decoder), an adversarial or perceptual loss, or a different model family entirely.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive the ELBO two ways and state exactly what the gap between it and the true log-likelihood is, and explain posterior collapse in terms of which term of the bound is winning.

M04

Normalizing flows

≈5 h

Flows are the family that refuses to approximate: exact likelihood, exact inference, exact sampling, achieved by requiring every layer to be invertible with a tractable Jacobian determinant. That constraint is severe and is why flows lost on images — but the continuous-time version is the direct mathematical ancestor of flow matching in M10, so this module is load-bearing rather than historical.

Concepts

  • The change-of-variables formula, which is the whole method
    • log p(x) = log p(z) + log |det ∂f/∂x| for an invertible f — exact, with no bound and no approximation
    • The cost: computing a determinant is O(d³) in general, so the entire design problem is finding expressive invertible layers whose Jacobian is triangular or otherwise cheap
  • The architectures, each a different way of buying a cheap determinant
    • Coupling layers (NICE, RealNVP): split the dimensions, transform half conditioned on the other half, giving a triangular Jacobian whose determinant is a product of diagonal entries. Invertible by construction and cheap in both directions
    • Glow's invertible 1×1 convolutions as a learned permutation between coupling layers
    • Autoregressive flows (MAF, IAF) and the asymmetry that matters: one is fast to evaluate and slow to sample, the other the reverse, and you pick based on what you need
    • Multi-scale architectures and dequantisation — the detail that discrete pixel values need noise added to be modelled by a continuous density at all, without which likelihoods are meaningless
  • Continuous normalizing flows, which is the part that matters downstream
    • Neural ODEs: define the transformation as the solution of dx/dt = v(x, t), so invertibility is automatic (integrate backwards) and the log-determinant becomes an integral of the divergence of v
    • The cost that killed them in practice: training requires simulating the ODE and backpropagating through the solver, which is slow and unstable
    • This is exactly the object flow matching in M10 learns to train without simulation, which is why that module is a breakthrough rather than a refinement
  • Why flows lost on images and where they still win — the invertibility constraint means the latent space has the same dimension as the data, so there is no compression, and the per-layer expressiveness is limited. They remain the right tool in physics and in variational inference, where exact likelihood is the requirement

Work through

  • Density Estimation Using Real NVP — coupling layers, the triangular-Jacobian trick, and the multi-scale architecture.
  • Normalizing Flows for Probabilistic Modeling and Inference — the survey, which organises the whole family by how each buys its determinant.
  • Neural Ordinary Differential Equations — continuous-time flows, and the adjoint method for backpropagating through a solver.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — its treatment of continuous flows, which sets up M10 directly.
  • Flow-based Deep Generative Models — the Jacobians written out for NICE, RealNVP and Glow side by side, which turns the whole family into one question — which invertible map has a cheap determinant.

Practice

Implement a RealNVP-style flow with coupling layers on 2D toy data and plot both the samples and the learned density, which you can do exactly because the likelihood is exact. Then verify invertibility numerically — encode and decode a batch and check the reconstruction error is at machine precision — and check your log-determinant against an autograd-computed Jacobian determinant. You are done when both checks pass to numerical precision, because a flow with a subtly wrong determinant trains fine and reports meaningless likelihoods.

Where people go wrong

Reporting likelihoods on discrete data without dequantisation. Pixel values are integers, and a continuous density can assign unbounded likelihood to a discrete set — a model can achieve arbitrarily good bits-per-dimension by concentrating spikes on the 256 integer values, which is not modelling anything. Adding uniform noise (or variational dequantisation) before density estimation is not a cosmetic step; without it the numbers are not comparable to anything.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why flow architectures are constrained in a way that VAEs and diffusion models are not, and name the specific quantity that has to stay cheap — and then say what a continuous normalizing flow changes about that constraint.

M05

Generative adversarial networks

≈6 h

GANs produced the first genuinely photorealistic image synthesis and dominated for six years before losing to diffusion, and the reasons for both are instructive. The family abandons likelihood entirely in favour of a learned loss, which buys sharpness and costs stability and coverage — and understanding that trade explains why adversarial losses survive as a component inside almost every system on this page, including latent diffusion's autoencoder.

Concepts

  • The adversarial game
    • Generator and discriminator in a minimax game; the theoretical result that at the optimum the generator matches the data distribution and the discriminator is everywhere 1/2
    • The original objective minimises the Jensen–Shannon divergence, which is mode-seeking — the reverse-KL asymmetry from M01, and the direct source of both the sharpness and the mode dropping
    • The non-saturating loss as the practical fix for vanishing generator gradients early in training
    • No likelihood, therefore no principled model comparison and no way to detect that a mode is missing except by looking
  • Why they are hard to train, precisely
    • Mode collapse: the generator finds one output the discriminator cannot reject and produces variations of it. A rational response to the objective, not a bug
    • Non-convergence and oscillation — the game has no descent direction, so the dynamics can cycle indefinitely
    • Discriminator overpowering, vanishing gradients, and the balance that has to be maintained by hand
    • Wasserstein GAN: replace JS with the earth-mover distance, which gives useful gradients even when the distributions do not overlap; weight clipping and then the gradient penalty as the ways to enforce the Lipschitz constraint. The clearest example of a loss change fixing a dynamics problem
    • Spectral normalisation as the cheaper standard, and the two-time-scale update rule with its convergence argument
  • The architectural lineage — DCGAN's conventions, progressive growing, StyleGAN's mapping network and adaptive instance normalisation and the disentangled W space, and StyleGAN2's fixes for the characteristic droplet artefacts
  • Why GANs lost and what they still win
    • Diffusion beat them on quality and diversity simultaneously, and trains without an adversarial game at all — which removed the field's main source of engineering pain
    • What survives: one-step sampling (GANs are fast, which is the property M11 spends a whole module recovering for diffusion), the adversarial loss as a perceptual component inside autoencoders and distillation, and a genuinely useful latent space for editing

Work through

  • Generative Adversarial Networks — the original. The minimax formulation and the optimality proof are three pages and worth doing properly.
  • Wasserstein GAN — read sections 2 and 3 for why the divergence choice determines whether gradients exist at all.
  • Analyzing and Improving the Image Quality of StyleGAN — StyleGAN2, for the architecture at its peak and for a good example of diagnosing artefacts to their architectural cause.
  • GANs Trained by a Two Time-Scale Update Rule Converge to a Local Nash Equilibrium — the TTUR convergence result, and the paper that introduced FID.
  • From GAN to WGAN — the derivation from the minimax objective to the Jensen–Shannon divergence, and then to why that divergence says nothing when supports do not overlap.
  • Generative Adversarial Networks (NIPS 2016 tutorial) — read as an argument that lost. Goodfellow names mode collapse and non-convergence as the open problems and forecasts the programme that would fix them; it never was, and diffusion routed around it.

Practice

Train a DCGAN on a small dataset and deliberately induce mode collapse — an unbalanced learning rate will usually do it. Plot samples over training and count distinct modes on a dataset where you know how many there are (MNIST digits, or a 2D mixture). Then switch to WGAN-GP and repeat. You are done when you have a mode-count-over-training plot for both and can explain the difference by reference to the divergence each optimises.

Where people go wrong

Reading a stable, decreasing generator loss as progress. In a two-player game the losses are relative to an opponent that is also changing, so both losses can look healthy while the generator produces one image — and they can look terrible while samples improve. The only reliable signals are the samples themselves and a coverage metric. This is the fundamental reason GANs were unpleasant to work with, and why a family with a stable scalar loss won as soon as one was good enough.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain mode collapse as a rational response to the objective rather than an optimisation failure, and say what Wasserstein GAN changed about the gradients when the two distributions have disjoint support.

M06

Energy-based models and score matching

≈7 h

This is the module that makes diffusion comprehensible, and it is placed before DDPM on purpose. Meeting diffusion first as a variational autoencoder with a thousand steps makes flow matching look like a different subject; meeting it after score matching makes both look like one thing — learning the gradient of a log-density and following it. The intractable normalising constant, and the trick that sidesteps it, is the key that opens the next four modules.

Concepts

  • Energy-based models and the partition function
    • p(x) = exp(−E(x)) / Z, where Z is an integral over the whole space. Any network can be an energy function, which is maximal architectural freedom — and Z is intractable, which is the price
    • Maximum likelihood requires sampling from the model to estimate the gradient of log Z; contrastive divergence and short-run MCMC as the approximations, with their known biases
  • Score matching, the idea that changes everything
    • The score is ∇ₓ log p(x) — the gradient of the log-density with respect to the data, not the parameters. Because Z does not depend on x, it vanishes under this gradient. The intractable constant simply disappears
    • Explicit score matching and its integration by parts into a form requiring the trace of the Hessian, which is still expensive
    • Denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011): add noise of known scale, and the score of the noised distribution is available in closed form from the noise you added. Training reduces to predicting the noise — which is, exactly, the DDPM objective in M07
    • Sliced score matching as the other route past the Hessian
  • Sampling with the score alone
    • Langevin dynamics: repeatedly step along the score and add noise, converging to the distribution. You can sample without ever knowing the density
    • The two problems that made naive score-based sampling fail — the score is badly estimated in low-density regions (which is almost everywhere in high dimensions, by the manifold hypothesis), and Langevin dynamics mixes very slowly between separated modes
    • The fix, which is the whole idea of diffusion: use multiple noise scales. Large noise smooths the distribution so the score is well estimated everywhere and modes are connected; small noise recovers the detail. Anneal from large to small
    • Noise-conditional score networks: one network taking the noise level as input, trained across all scales at once
  • The connection to hold on to — a diffusion model is a noise-conditional score network, and sampling from one is annealed Langevin dynamics. M07's derivation arrives at the same place from a completely different direction, and recognising that is the point of this module

Work through

  • Generative Modeling by Estimating Gradients of the Data Distribution — Song and Ermon's NCSN paper. The diagnosis of why single-scale score matching fails is the most valuable part.
  • Generative Modeling by Estimating Gradients of the Data Distribution (blog) — Yang Song's own long-form explanation, with the figures that make the multi-scale argument obvious.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — its score-matching chapter, which is the cleanest textbook derivation.
  • Deep Learning — Chapter 18, for the partition-function problem and the older approximations in context.
  • But how do AI images and videos actually work? — the two-dimensional toy where the score field and the sample trajectories are visible at once — watch it before the score-matching algebra, not after.

Practice

On a 2D mixture of Gaussians, compute the true score analytically and plot it as a vector field. Then train a network by denoising score matching at a single noise level and compare the learned field to the truth — it will be good near the data and wrong far from it. Run Langevin dynamics from a random start and watch it fail to find the distant modes. Then train a noise-conditional network across five noise scales and run annealed Langevin. You are done when the annealed version recovers all the modes and the single-scale version does not.

Where people go wrong

Confusing the score with the gradient used in training. ∇ₓ log p(x) is a gradient with respect to the data, and it is the object the network outputs; the gradient with respect to the parameters is what the optimiser uses. They are unrelated quantities that share a symbol, and the confusion makes every derivation in the next three modules unreadable. Say "the score is a vector field over data space, one arrow per point" until it is automatic.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why score matching eliminates the partition function, derive the denoising score matching objective, and state the two failure modes of single-scale score-based sampling that multiple noise scales fix.

M07

Diffusion I: the denoising formulation

≈8 h

This is the central module of the page. Diffusion's move is to convert one intractable problem — sample from a complex distribution — into a thousand easy ones, each of which is "remove a little noise". The derivation reaches the same objective M06 arrived at from score matching, by an entirely different route, and seeing both is what makes the family feel inevitable rather than lucky.

Concepts

  • The forward process, which has no learned parameters at all
    • A fixed Markov chain adding Gaussian noise over T steps, with a variance schedule β₁…β_T, ending at approximately pure noise
    • The closed form that makes training practical: because Gaussians compose, x_t can be sampled directly from x₀ in one step as √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1−ᾱ_t) ε. No simulation needed, so each training step samples a random t
    • Schedule choice — linear, cosine, and why the cosine schedule's slower destruction of information at the start improved results measurably
    • Signal-to-noise ratio as the schedule-independent way to reason about where in the process you are
  • The reverse process and the derivation
    • The key fact: for small enough steps the reverse conditional is also Gaussian, so a network need only predict its mean
    • The variational bound, which is a sum of KL divergences between tractable Gaussians — an ELBO exactly like M03's, over a much longer chain
    • The reparameterisation that made it work: instead of predicting the mean, predict the noise ε that was added. The loss collapses to a simple mean-squared error between the true and predicted noise, with the weighting terms dropped — a simplification that is not the exact bound and empirically works better
    • The three equivalent parameterisations — predict ε, predict x₀, or predict the velocity v — and why v-prediction is preferred at high noise and for distillation
    • The connection to M06 stated explicitly: the ε-prediction network is a scaled score estimate, so a diffusion model is a noise-conditional score network by another name
  • What actually gets trained, in practice
    • Sample x₀, sample t uniformly, sample ε, form x_t in closed form, predict ε, take a gradient step. The entire training loop is six lines
    • Timestep embeddings — sinusoidal, projected, and injected into every block — since one network must handle every noise level
    • Loss weighting across timesteps, min-SNR weighting, and why uniform weighting over-weights the easy high-noise steps
  • Why it works when GANs struggled — a stable regression objective with no adversarial game, a decomposition of a hard problem into easy ones, and mode coverage inherited from the likelihood-based bound. The cost is T forward passes to sample, which M09 through M11 exist to reduce

Work through

  • Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models — the paper that made diffusion work. Read the derivation in section 3 and then section 3.2, where the simplified objective is introduced with an honest note that it is not the bound.
  • Understanding Diffusion Models: A Unified Perspective — Calvin Luo's tutorial, which derives the variational and score views side by side and is the single best explanation for a reader who has done M03 and M06.
  • What Are Diffusion Models? — Lilian Weng's post, the standard reference implementation of the mathematics in prose, with every equation numbered and derived.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — Chapters 2–3, which is the MIT course's treatment and the most rigorous free source.
  • Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models — the cosine schedule, learned variances, and the ablations that established several current defaults.
  • What are Diffusion Models? (video explainer) — twenty animated minutes on the forward and reverse processes. Use it to make the DDPM notation legible; it will not give you the derivation.
  • Diffusion Models | Paper Explanation | Math Explained — the variational bound term by term, including the reparameterisation that collapses the KL into the epsilon-prediction MSE — the step most explainers skip.
  • The Annotated Diffusion Model — DDPM in one notebook with each equation beside the code that computes it. The reference to check your own implementation against when the samples come out grey.
  • labml.ai annotated DDPM — line-by-line PyTorch with every tensor shape stated. When training runs but sampling is wrong, diff your noise-schedule indexing and posterior variance against this.
  • minDiffusion — under two hundred self-contained lines, trainable on CIFAR-10 in a couple of hours. Read it in one sitting to settle what is essential and what is framework scaffolding.

Practice

Implement DDPM from scratch on a small image dataset — forward process, timestep embedding, a small U-Net, the simplified loss, and ancestral sampling. Then verify the forward process empirically: sample x_t both by iterating the chain and by the closed form and check the distributions match. Finally, visualise the reverse process as a grid of intermediate steps. You are done when your two forward-sampling routes agree statistically and your reverse grid shows structure emerging from noise around the same fraction of the way through every time.

Where people go wrong

Believing the simplified loss is the variational bound. DDPM drops the timestep-dependent weighting terms that the ELBO derivation produces, and the result is a different objective that empirically generates better samples and worse likelihoods. This matters practically: if you are reporting bits-per-dimension you need the full bound, and if you are generating images you want the simplified one. Papers are not always explicit about which they used, and the two are not comparable.

Checkpoint

M08

Diffusion II: the continuous-time SDE view

≈7 h

M07's diffusion has a thousand discrete steps because someone chose a thousand. The continuous formulation removes that arbitrariness, unifies DDPM with M06's score-based models as two discretisations of one SDE, and produces the probability-flow ODE — a deterministic sampler with the same marginals, which is what makes fast sampling, exact likelihoods and clean inversion possible. Almost every practical advance after 2021 comes from this reframing.

Concepts

  • Diffusion as a stochastic differential equation
    • The forward process in continuous time: dx = f(x, t) dt + g(t) dw, with a drift and a diffusion coefficient. DDPM and score-based models are two choices of f and g — variance-preserving and variance-exploding respectively
    • Anderson's reverse-time SDE: the reverse process is another SDE whose drift contains the score ∇ₓ log p_t(x). This is the theorem the whole field rests on, and it says that if you know the score at every noise level you can run time backwards
    • Enough SDE background to read this — Brownian motion, Itô integrals, and why the reverse process needs a term the forward one does not
  • The probability-flow ODE, which is the practical payoff
    • There exists a deterministic ODE whose marginal distributions at every time exactly match the SDE's. Same distribution, no noise in the sampler
    • What determinism buys: far fewer steps with a good solver, exact likelihood computation via the instantaneous change-of-variables formula, and a unique noise-to-sample mapping that makes inversion and interpolation well defined — which M18's editing depends on
    • The connection to M04's continuous normalizing flows: the probability-flow ODE is a continuous normalizing flow, trained by score matching instead of by simulation. This is the sentence that ties the page together
  • Sampling as numerical integration, which is the right frame
    • Euler–Maruyama for the SDE, Euler and Heun for the ODE; higher-order and exponential integrators exploiting the semi-linear structure
    • Predictor–corrector sampling: an ODE step followed by Langevin correction, combining the speed of the deterministic path with the error correction of the stochastic one
    • Discretisation error as the thing that limits step count, which reframes "how many steps do I need" as a solver question rather than a model question
  • The design-space unification — Karras et al.'s reformulation separating the noise schedule, the network preconditioning, the loss weighting and the sampler into independent choices, which turned a collection of papers into a small set of orthogonal knobs and produced better results by tuning them separately

Work through

  • Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations — the unification. Read sections 3 and 4 for the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE; this is the most important paper on the page after DDPM.
  • Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models — Karras et al., which separates the orthogonal design choices and is the paper practitioners actually use.
  • Generative Modeling by Estimating Gradients of the Data Distribution (blog) — Song's post covers the SDE view with better figures than the paper.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — its SDE chapter, which develops the stochastic-calculus background from scratch.
  • Diffusion and Score-Based Generative Models — Yang Song presenting the unification himself, in the order this page uses: score matching, annealed Langevin, forward SDE, probability-flow ODE, likelihoods.
  • Perspectives on diffusion — eight simultaneous readings of the same model. Read it at the end of this module — fluency here means holding several at once, because every paper silently picks one.

Practice

Take your M07 model and sample from it three ways: ancestral (SDE) sampling, an Euler probability-flow ODE solver, and a Heun solver. Plot sample quality against the number of function evaluations for all three across 5 to 1,000 steps. Then verify the ODE's determinism — the same initial noise must always give the same image — and use it to interpolate between two samples in noise space. You are done when Heun visibly beats Euler at low step counts and your interpolation produces a smooth path rather than a crossfade.

Where people go wrong

Treating the number of sampling steps as a property of the model. It is a property of the solver and the error you will tolerate: the same trained network gives poor samples in 20 Euler steps and good ones in 20 Heun steps, and better again with an exponential integrator. Papers reporting "our model needs 1,000 steps" are usually reporting their sampler's convergence, and swapping the solver is free.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: write the reverse-time SDE and identify the score term in it, explain what the probability-flow ODE preserves and what it discards, and say why the ODE formulation is what makes inversion well defined.

M09

Samplers and guidance

≈7 h

Two things separate a research diffusion model from a usable one: sampling in tens of steps rather than thousands, and following a text prompt closely. Both are inference-time interventions requiring no retraining, and classifier-free guidance in particular is the single most important trick in applied diffusion — it is why text-to-image works, and its artefacts at high scales are the most recognisable failure mode in the field.

Concepts

  • Fast samplers
    • DDIM: a non-Markovian forward process with the same marginals, giving a deterministic sampler with a tunable stochasticity parameter η, and 20–50 step sampling from a model trained with a thousand
    • DPM-Solver and its variants, exploiting the semi-linear structure of the probability-flow ODE for high-order steps
    • Ancestral versus deterministic sampling, and the practical observation that stochasticity helps at high step counts (it corrects accumulated error) and hurts at low ones
    • Timestep spacing and the Karras schedule, which matters more at low step counts than the solver does
  • Guidance, the mechanism that makes conditioning work
    • Classifier guidance: add the gradient of a separately trained noisy-image classifier to the score, steering toward a class. Works, and needs a classifier trained on noisy data at every level
    • Classifier-free guidance: train one model with conditioning randomly dropped, then at sampling time extrapolate — ε̂ = ε_uncond + w(ε_cond − ε_uncond). No extra model, one hyperparameter, and it transformed conditional generation
    • What it actually does: samples from a sharpened distribution p(x|c) p(c|x)^(w−1), trading diversity for prompt adherence. It is not sampling from the conditional distribution and knowing that explains the artefacts
    • The artefacts at high scale: oversaturated colours, blown-out contrast, simplified compositions and reduced diversity. Dynamic thresholding and guidance rescaling as the standard corrections, and guidance intervals — applying guidance only in the middle of the trajectory — as the more recent one
    • Negative prompts as classifier-free guidance with the unconditional branch replaced by a conditional one, which is why they work at all
  • Other inference-time control — self-attention guidance, perturbed attention guidance, and the general pattern of steering the trajectory using a deliberately degraded version of the model as the thing to move away from
  • The practical tuning surface a user actually has: sampler, step count, guidance scale, schedule and seed — five knobs whose interactions are strong, which is why reported "best settings" rarely transfer between models

Work through

  • Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models — DDIM. The non-Markovian construction and the η parameter, plus the observation that the deterministic case gives a meaningful latent space.
  • Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance — three pages, one equation, and the technique that everything downstream depends on.
  • Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis — classifier guidance and the architectural ablations, and the paper that ended the GAN era.
  • Elucidating the Design Space of Diffusion-Based Generative Models — reread here for the sampler and schedule analysis specifically.
  • Diffusers — its scheduler implementations, which are the practical index of every sampler in this module with consistent interfaces.
  • Guidance: a cheat code for diffusion models — why classifier-free guidance works, why it is not a Bayesian update on anything, and why a technique with no principled justification became mandatory in production.
  • Denoising Diffusion-based Generative Modeling: Foundations and Applications — the sampler section, which lays out the whole design space with the tradeoffs named rather than one sampler at a time.
  • EDM reference implementation — read generate.py against the paper's Algorithm 2. The second-order correction is four lines, and seeing that is the fastest cure for treating samplers as mystical.
  • k-diffusion — the sampler library the open-source ecosystem actually runs. The differences between DPM-Solver++ variants are three lines each; read them adjacently.
  • Hugging Face Diffusion Models Course — unit 2's exercise — steering an unconditional model with a hand-written loss at sampling time — is the cheapest way to feel what guidance does before the formalism.

Practice

On a class-conditional model you train yourself, sweep guidance scale from 1 to 20 at a fixed seed and produce a grid. Measure both a quality metric and a diversity metric across the sweep — you should see quality peak and then fall while diversity declines monotonically. Then implement dynamic thresholding and show it extending the usable range. You are done when you can point at the scale where saturation artefacts begin and explain them in terms of what guidance does to the distribution.

Where people go wrong

Raising the guidance scale to improve prompt adherence and not noticing what it costs. Guidance samples from a sharpened distribution, so higher scales mean less diversity and, past a point, visible oversaturation and simplified composition — and because the artefacts appear gradually, people calibrate to them. Always evaluate guidance with a diversity metric alongside a quality one, and generate several seeds per prompt before concluding a scale is right.

M10

Flow matching and rectified flow

≈7 h

Flow matching is the reformulation that has displaced the DDPM parameterisation in new work, and it is best understood as the completion of M04's continuous normalizing flows: the same object, trained without simulating the ODE. The construction is simpler than diffusion's, the training objective is a plain regression onto a straight line, and the resulting paths are easier to integrate in few steps — which is why the newest large image and video models are trained this way.

Concepts

  • The reframing
    • Instead of a noising process to reverse, specify a probability path from noise to data and learn the velocity field that transports along it. The model outputs a vector, not a noise estimate
    • The problem: the marginal velocity field that generates the path is intractable, exactly as the marginal score was in M06
    • The conditional flow matching insight, which is the whole paper: regressing onto the conditional velocity — the one that transports a single noise sample to a single data sample — has the same gradient as regressing onto the intractable marginal one. So you can train on pairs you can construct trivially
  • The construction, which is startlingly simple
    • Pick x₀ from noise and x₁ from data, interpolate linearly x_t = (1−t)x₀ + t x₁, and train the network to predict the constant velocity x₁ − x₀. That is the entire training loop
    • The straight conditional paths are the point: a straight path is exactly integrable in one step, so the more the learned marginal field resembles straight lines, the fewer solver steps you need
    • Diffusion as a special case — a particular curved probability path — which makes flow matching a generalisation rather than a competitor
    • Optimal-transport couplings, pairing noise and data samples to reduce path crossing rather than pairing them at random
  • Rectified flow and reflow
    • Rectified flow trains on the same linear interpolation; the reflow procedure then retrains on the model's own noise-to-sample pairs, which straightens the paths further because the pairing is now the one the model actually produces
    • Iterating reflow trades a little quality for paths straight enough to integrate in very few steps, which is the bridge to M11
  • Why the field moved — a simpler objective with no noise schedule to design, better-conditioned training at high noise, straighter trajectories, and a formulation that extends naturally to non-Gaussian sources and to data on manifolds, which matters for M17's molecules

Work through

  • Flow Matching for Generative Modeling — the paper. The conditional-flow-matching theorem in section 3 is the whole contribution; make sure you understand why the gradients coincide.
  • Flow Straight and Fast: Learning to Generate and Transfer Data with Rectified Flow — rectified flow and the reflow procedure, arrived at independently.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — Chapters 2 and 4, which teach flow matching first and diffusion as a special case. The clearest available treatment.
  • MIT 6.S184: Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — the course, with labs that implement flow matching from scratch.
  • Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis — the Stable Diffusion 3 paper, for flow matching applied at scale with the timestep-sampling details that matter in practice.
  • Flow Matching: Simplifying and Generalizing Diffusion Models — Lipman's own framing, delivered to an audience that already knows diffusion. Watch after M08; the argument needs the probability-flow ODE in place.
  • Flow Matching for Generative Modeling (NeurIPS 2024 tutorial) — the long version, out to Riemannian manifolds and discrete state spaces. The generalisation section is where flow matching stops being a model and becomes a recipe.
  • flow_matching library documentation — the authors' API mirrors the mathematics — probability path, scheduler, solver. Check your hand-rolled implementation against it on a toy before scaling anything.
  • TorchCFM — run the two-dimensional notebooks with independent and optimal-transport couplings. The difference is visible in the trajectories long before any sample-quality number moves.
  • MIT 6.S184 labs — the three graded labs, which build SDE simulation, conditional flow matching and score matching in the same dependency order as M06 to M10.

Practice

Implement conditional flow matching on 2D toy data — twenty lines — and plot the learned velocity field and the sample trajectories from noise to data. Then implement DDPM on the same data and plot its trajectories for comparison; the flow matching paths should be visibly straighter. Measure sample quality against number of solver steps for both. You are done when flow matching wins clearly at low step counts and you can explain the difference in terms of path curvature.

Where people go wrong

Assuming straight conditional paths mean the learned field is straight. Each training pair is transported along a straight line, but the marginal field the network learns is an average over all pairs passing near a point, and that average is generally curved — which is precisely why plain rectified flow still needs multiple solver steps and why reflow exists. Confusing the two leads people to expect one-step generation from a model that cannot do it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the conditional flow matching theorem and why it makes the intractable objective trainable, and explain what reflow does to the noise-data pairing that straightens the learned field.

M11

Distillation and few-step generation

≈6 h

Diffusion's one structural disadvantage against GANs is sampling cost, and this module is the five-year effort to remove it — from fifty steps to four to one. It matters commercially (interactive image generation, real-time video) and conceptually, because the methods reveal what the multi-step trajectory was actually buying. It is placed after flow matching because straight paths are what make one-step generation plausible at all.

Concepts

  • Trajectory distillation
    • Progressive distillation: train a student to take one step matching the teacher's two, then repeat — halving the step count each round from 1,000 to 4. Clean, and it degrades below about four steps
    • Why v-prediction from M07 matters here: the ε parameterisation becomes badly conditioned at very low step counts, and the velocity parameterisation does not
  • Consistency models, the conceptual advance
    • Train a function that maps any point on a probability-flow ODE trajectory directly to that trajectory's origin. If it satisfies that self-consistency, one evaluation generates a sample
    • Consistency distillation from a pretrained diffusion model, and consistency training from scratch without a teacher
    • The multi-step option: alternate a jump to the origin with re-noising, trading a few evaluations for quality — so consistency models are not exclusively one-step
    • Latent consistency models applying this in a latent space, which is what put few-step generation into production
  • The flow-map framing that unifies the recent work — consistency trajectory models mapping between any two times on a trajectory, and MeanFlow-style approaches learning average velocity over an interval. These are the current frontier and the CVPR-tutorial literature treats them as one family
  • Adversarial distillation — adding a discriminator to the distillation objective, which is what gets one- and two-step models to look genuinely sharp. The honest reading is that the GAN objective from M05 returned as the component that fixes the perceptual quality distillation loses
  • What is lost, which papers under-report
    • Diversity falls: few-step models produce visibly less variation across seeds for the same prompt, and FID-style metrics that reward realism do not capture it
    • Guidance interacts badly with distillation, since guidance is usually baked in at a fixed scale, removing a control the user had
    • Fine detail and text rendering degrade first, which is a good diagnostic for how much distillation cost you

Work through

  • Progressive Distillation for Fast Sampling of Diffusion Models — the halving procedure and the v-parameterisation, which is where that parameterisation comes from.
  • Consistency Models — the self-consistency property, both training routes, and the multi-step sampling option.
  • CVPR 2026 Tutorial on Principles of Diffusion Models — for the flow-map framing that unifies consistency models, CTM and MeanFlow into one family, which is the current organising view.
  • Diffusers — the LCM and turbo pipelines, for running few-step models and comparing them against their teachers directly.
  • The paradox of diffusion distillation — if a one-step student matches a fifty-step teacher, what were the other forty-nine steps for? The answer reframes distillation as evidence about optimisation, not a trick.

Practice

Take a pretrained diffusion model and its distilled few-step counterpart. Generate 500 images from each for the same twenty prompts across many seeds, and measure both a realism metric and a diversity metric — pairwise distance between samples of the same prompt is enough. You are done when you can quantify the diversity loss, which will be substantial and is the thing distillation papers report least.

Where people go wrong

Evaluating a distilled model on FID alone and concluding it matched its teacher. FID is computed over a large set of generations from many prompts and is largely insensitive to whether a single prompt produces one image or fifty different ones — so a model that has collapsed to a single mode per prompt can score well. Measure within-prompt diversity separately; it is where distillation's real cost sits, and it is the property users notice.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain the self-consistency property that lets a consistency model generate in one step, and name three things that degrade under aggressive distillation that a realism metric will not detect.

M12

Architectures: U-Net, DiT and latent diffusion

≈6 h

Everything so far specified an objective and left the network abstract. This module is the network, and it contains the single decision that made large-scale image generation affordable: run diffusion in a compressed latent space rather than on pixels. That one change cut the compute by roughly an order of magnitude and is why open text-to-image models exist at all.

Concepts

  • The U-Net, and why this shape
    • Encoder–decoder with skip connections at every resolution: the model must output something the same size as its input while reasoning at multiple scales, and the skips preserve high-frequency detail the bottleneck would lose
    • The diffusion-specific additions — residual blocks with timestep embeddings injected, self-attention at the lower resolutions where it is affordable, and group normalisation
    • Cross-attention for conditioning: the text embedding enters as keys and values while the image features are queries, which is the mechanism by which a prompt reaches the pixels
  • The transformer replacement
    • DiT: patchify the latent, treat patches as tokens, use a plain transformer with adaptive layer norm conditioned on timestep and class. No convolutions, no U-Net skips
    • The result that mattered: DiT scales predictably with compute in a way the U-Net did not, which is why every recent large model uses a transformer backbone
    • adaLN-zero conditioning — initialising the conditioning modulation to zero so each block starts as the identity — which is a small trick with a large effect on trainability
    • MMDiT and the joint text–image attention streams used in current models, where both modalities attend to each other rather than one conditioning the other
  • Latent diffusion, the decision that changed the economics
    • Train an autoencoder (from M02 and M03's lineage) to compress images by a factor of 8 in each spatial dimension, then run diffusion entirely in that latent space
    • The compute argument: a 512×512 image becomes a 64×64×4 latent, which is a 48-fold reduction in the elements the diffusion model must process
    • The autoencoder is trained with a combination of reconstruction, perceptual and adversarial losses plus a mild latent regulariser — the adversarial component from M05 doing exactly the job it is good at
    • The cost, which is a hard ceiling: the autoencoder's reconstruction quality bounds the whole system. Fine text, faces at small scale and high-frequency texture are lost in the encoder and no amount of diffusion capacity recovers them. Higher-channel-count autoencoders in recent models are the response
    • Diagnosing it: encode and decode an image without any diffusion at all, and whatever is lost is your ceiling
  • Cascaded diffusion as the alternative — generate small, then super-resolve with further diffusion stages — and the tradeoff against latent diffusion in quality, complexity and inference cost

Work through

  • High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models — the Stable Diffusion paper. Read section 3 for the autoencoder design and the cross-attention conditioning.
  • Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers — DiT, and specifically the scaling analysis that justified abandoning the U-Net.
  • Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis — for MMDiT and the current architecture at scale.
  • Simple Diffusion: End-to-End Diffusion for High Resolution Images — the counter-argument that pixel-space diffusion can work with the right adjustments, which is worth reading against the latent consensus.
  • Diffusers — read the U-Net and DiT model source alongside the papers; the conditioning plumbing is clearer in code.
  • Generative modelling in latent space — what the autoencoder is actually buying — perceptual compression, not information compression — and how the latent regularisation choice propagates into the generator's failures.
  • Diffusion is spectral autoregression — why the reverse process resolves low spatial frequencies first, and therefore why a noise schedule tuned at one resolution is wrong at another.
  • Coding Stable Diffusion from scratch in PyTorch — five hours writing the whole latent-diffusion stack — VAE, text encoder, cross-attention U-Net, samplers, guidance. Do it with an editor open.
  • denoising-diffusion-pytorch — read as a menu of what people actually turn on: self-conditioning, v-parameterisation, min-SNR weighting, each traceable to its paper via the commit history.
  • Hugging Face forums — search before you post. The memory arithmetic and scheduler-behaviour questions recur, and the answers are usually more accurate than the tutorials that caused them.

Practice

Measure the autoencoder ceiling. Take a latent diffusion model's autoencoder, encode and decode a set of images with no diffusion involved, and compute reconstruction metrics. Do it separately for images with fine text, faces at small scale, and high-frequency texture. Then generate images of those same categories with the full model. You are done when you can show that the generation failures on text and small faces are already present in the reconstruction, which locates the bottleneck definitively.

Where people go wrong

Attributing latent-diffusion artefacts to the diffusion model. Illegible small text, mangled faces at a distance and lost fine texture are frequently autoencoder failures, not sampler or model failures — they are gone before diffusion starts. The two-minute reconstruction test tells you which, and it redirects effort from tuning guidance scales toward the only fixes that can work: a better autoencoder, a higher resolution, or a cascaded approach.

Checkpoint

M13

Text-to-image, end to end

≈6 h

This is the first module that assembles the machinery into a system, and the assembly is where the non-obvious decisions live — which text encoder, how the data was captioned, what resolution schedule, how the aesthetic filtering was done. Those choices explain more of the difference between two text-to-image models than the diffusion formulation does.

Concepts

  • The text encoder, which is a larger lever than expected
    • CLIP's text encoder as the original choice, and its limits: trained on short alt-text with a 77-token context, so it handles long or compositional prompts poorly
    • The Imagen finding that a large frozen language model as the text encoder improves image–text alignment more than scaling the diffusion model does — which was surprising and has held up
    • Current practice using multiple text encoders together, and why: CLIP for the visual-semantic alignment, a language model for compositional and long-prompt understanding
  • Data and captions, which is where the quality actually comes from
    • Web image–text pairs, and the fact that alt-text is short, noisy and frequently unrelated to the image
    • Recaptioning with a vision-language model, now standard, and the measured effect: descriptive synthetic captions improve prompt adherence substantially, and training on a mixture of original and synthetic captions preserves the ability to handle terse user prompts
    • Aesthetic filtering, which raises perceived quality and narrows the output distribution toward a recognisable style — a real tradeoff, not a free improvement
    • Resolution and aspect-ratio bucketing so that training does not require square crops, which is why modern models handle non-square outputs well
  • The two system architectures, compared
    • Latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion lineage): compress, diffuse in latent space, decode. Cheap, open, autoencoder-limited
    • Cascaded pixel-space (Imagen lineage): generate at 64×64, then two diffusion super-resolution stages. Higher fidelity on fine structure, more expensive, more moving parts
    • The unCLIP variant: generate a CLIP image embedding from text, then decode it — a different factorisation with different diversity behaviour
  • The failure modes that persist across all of them, and their causes
    • Text rendering, historically bad and improved mainly by character-aware text encoders and by simply training on more images containing text
    • Counting, spatial relations and attribute binding — "a red cube on a blue sphere" — which are compositional failures traceable to how CLIP-style training treats a caption as a bag of concepts
    • Hands and anatomy, which is a data and articulation problem rather than a mysterious one
    • Style and demographic bias inherited from the training distribution and amplified by aesthetic filtering

Work through

  • High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models — reread here as a complete system rather than as an architecture.
  • Photorealistic Text-to-Image Diffusion Models with Deep Language Understanding — Imagen, for the frozen-language-model finding and the cascaded architecture.
  • Hierarchical Text-Conditional Image Generation with CLIP Latents — unCLIP, for the third factorisation and its diversity analysis.
  • Scaling Rectified Flow Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis — the most complete modern system description, including the recaptioning and the multi-encoder setup.
  • LAION-5B: An Open Large-Scale Dataset for Training Next Generation Image-Text Models — the dataset that made open text-to-image possible, and the documentation of what is actually in it.
  • The Illustrated Stable Diffusion — one prompt traced through tokeniser, text encoder, latent noise, U-Net loop and decoder, with a diagram at each hand-off. Read it before the module, as the map.
  • How AI Image Generators Work (Stable Diffusion / Dall-E) — Computerphile — eighteen whiteboard minutes that get the latent-space and conditioning story right. The thing to send a colleague who needs the shape without the twelve modules.

Practice

Build a compositional evaluation set of fifty prompts covering counting, spatial relations, attribute binding, text rendering and negation. Run it across three open models and score adherence by hand. Then recaption a small dataset with a vision-language model, fine-tune one model on original captions and one on synthetic ones, and re-score. You are done when you have a per-category failure table and can show the recaptioning effect on at least one category.

Where people go wrong

Trying to fix compositional failures with prompt engineering. Attribute binding and spatial relations fail because the text encoder's representation does not reliably carry them — the caption is closer to a bag of concepts than a structured description — so rephrasing moves the failure rather than removing it. The interventions that work are architectural or data-level: a better text encoder, layout conditioning from M18, or generating the elements separately and composing them.

Checkpoint

M14

Video generation and world models

≈5 h

Video is images plus a constraint that turns out to dominate everything: temporal consistency. An object must stay the same object across frames, and the naive extension of image diffusion fails at exactly that. This module covers what changes, and it ends at the world-model framing, which is the most interesting current claim about what these systems are learning.

Concepts

  • What makes video hard
    • The dimensionality: a five-second clip at 24 fps is 120 frames, so both memory and compute scale by two orders of magnitude over a single image
    • Temporal consistency as the binding constraint — identity, lighting and geometry must persist, and flicker is the characteristic failure of models that treat frames independently
    • Motion and physics: plausible dynamics, object permanence through occlusion, and the fact that violations are far more noticeable in motion than a comparable error in a still image
    • Data: far less high-quality captioned video than images, and captions that describe content rather than motion
  • Architectures
    • The inflation approach: take an image model, add temporal attention or 3D convolutions, and fine-tune — cheap, and it inherits the image model's quality
    • Spatiotemporal latents: a video autoencoder compressing in time as well as space, then a transformer over spacetime patches. The variable-duration, variable-resolution training this enables is what current systems do
    • Factorised versus joint spatiotemporal attention, and the compute argument for factorising
    • Cascaded generation — keyframes then interpolation, or low resolution then upsampling — as the standard way to reach useful lengths
  • Control, which is what makes video usable rather than a demo — image-to-video conditioning on a first frame, camera-motion control, structure conditioning from depth or pose, and video-to-video restyling
  • The world-model claim, stated carefully
    • The argument: a model that predicts future frames well must have learned something about objects, persistence and dynamics, and could therefore serve as a simulator for planning or for training embodied agents
    • The evidence for: emergent 3D consistency and object permanence not explicitly trained for, and demonstrations of interactive generation conditioned on actions
    • The evidence against: physical violations that a physics engine would never make, no guarantee of consistency over long horizons, and no way to query or constrain the implicit model. Visually plausible is not the same as physically correct, and the gap is exactly what a planner would need
    • The honest position: an open and important question, currently over-claimed in both directions

Work through

  • Video Diffusion Models — the first proper video diffusion paper, with the factorised spatiotemporal architecture and the gradient method for conditional sampling.
  • Structure and Content-Guided Video Synthesis with Diffusion Models — Gen-1, for the structure/content factorisation that underlies most video-to-video control.
  • Video Generation Models as World Simulators — the Sora technical report, for the spacetime-patch formulation and the world-model claim in its authors' own words. Read it critically; it is a technical report, not a paper.
  • Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers — reread here, since the spacetime-patch approach is DiT generalised by one dimension.
  • CVPR 2024 Tutorial on Diffusion-based Video Generative Models — temporal layers, how they are inflated from image models, and a benchmarks-and-attacks section with no image-side equivalent. Slides are split by section, so it works as reference.
  • VBench — run it before you trust any video demo. Look at dynamic degree first — it catches the model that scores well on consistency by generating something that barely moves.

Practice

Take an image diffusion model and generate a sequence by conditioning each frame on the previous one, and measure the drift — identity and colour will degrade visibly within a dozen frames. Then run an actual video model on the same prompts and quantify temporal consistency, for instance by optical-flow warping error between adjacent frames. You are done when you have a consistency number for both and can state how quickly the naive approach fails.

Where people go wrong

Reading emergent 3D consistency as evidence of a learned physics engine. A model that generates plausible video has learned the statistics of video, which includes a great deal about how objects usually behave — and it will also cheerfully generate liquid flowing uphill or an object passing through a wall when the prompt makes that locally plausible. The distinction that matters for the world-model claim is whether the implicit model can be queried and relied on, and currently it cannot.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name three architectural responses to temporal consistency and their compute costs, and state the strongest evidence for and against the world-model interpretation without asserting a conclusion.

M15

Audio, neural codecs and speech

≈6 h

Audio is where the tokenise-then-model recipe from M02 pays off most clearly, and where a neural codec turned an intractable sequence length into a manageable one. It also contains the cleanest example on this page of a modality-specific constraint: human hearing is extremely sensitive to phase and to temporal artefacts that the eye's equivalent would never notice, so audio quality metrics and losses look nothing like image ones.

Concepts

  • Why raw audio is hard — 24,000 samples per second means a three-minute track is over four million time steps, with structure at scales from milliseconds (timbre) to minutes (song form)
  • The representation question
    • Waveform versus spectrogram; the phase problem, and why a spectrogram alone is not invertible without a vocoder or a phase-reconstruction algorithm
    • Mel scales and the perceptual weighting that makes them the standard intermediate representation
  • Neural audio codecs, which reorganised the field
    • Residual vector quantisation: a stack of codebooks each encoding the residual of the last, giving fine-grained rate control and much better reconstruction than a single codebook at the same bitrate
    • SoundStream and EnCodec as the standard designs, with adversarial and multi-scale spectrogram losses because reconstruction loss alone gives muffled audio
    • The consequence: audio becomes a short sequence of discrete tokens, so a transformer can model it exactly as in M02. This is what made high-quality music and audio generation feasible
    • The semantic/acoustic token split — one stream for content, one for acoustic detail — and the hierarchical modelling it enables
  • Speech synthesis
    • The two-stage classical pipeline — text to mel-spectrogram, then a vocoder — and end-to-end alternatives
    • Vocoders: WaveNet's autoregressive quality at unusable speed, then GAN-based vocoders reaching comparable quality in real time, then diffusion vocoders
    • Codec language models for zero-shot voice cloning from a few seconds of audio, and the standard failure modes: prosody errors, unstable speaking rate, and hallucinated or repeated words inherited from autoregressive generation
    • Diffusion and flow-matching TTS as the non-autoregressive alternative with better stability and controllable duration
  • Music generation and its distinct problems — long-range structure over minutes, multiple simultaneous instruments, the near-total absence of licensed training data at scale, and evaluation that essentially requires listening

Work through

  • WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio — reread here for the raw-waveform baseline that everything else improves on.
  • High Fidelity Neural Audio Compression — EnCodec. Residual vector quantisation and the multi-scale discriminator losses, which are the two ideas that transfer.
  • Neural Codec Language Models are Zero-Shot Text to Speech Synthesizers — VALL-E, for the codec-token language-model recipe and its failure modes.
  • Neural Discrete Representation Learning — reread the VQ material here; audio codecs are VQ-VAE with a residual stack and better losses.
  • From White Noise to Symphony: Diffusion Models for Music and Sound — why audio needed its own literature: sample rates three orders of magnitude above an image's pixel grid in time, hence codecs, latents and real-time constraints.
  • AudioCraft — encode and reconstruct audio at several bitrates before generating anything. Hearing what the codec discards is this module's real lesson.

Practice

Encode and decode audio through a neural codec at several bitrates and listen to each, then measure reconstruction with a spectral distance. Compare against an equivalent-bitrate classical codec. Then take the discrete codes and fit a small autoregressive model over them to generate short continuations. You are done when you can state the bitrate at which you personally stop hearing a difference, and when your generated continuations are recognisably in the style of the training audio.

Where people go wrong

Training audio models with a reconstruction loss alone. Mean-squared error on a waveform or a spectrogram produces muffled, phase-incoherent output that scores well and sounds wrong, because the metric does not capture what hearing is sensitive to. Every competitive audio model uses adversarial and multi-resolution spectral losses on top — the same lesson as M03's VAE blur, but far more audible.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why residual vector quantisation beats a single large codebook at the same bitrate, and say why audio generation moved to codec tokens rather than modelling waveforms directly.

M16

3D and neural fields

≈6 h

3D generation has a problem no other modality has: there is very little 3D training data. The field's response — distilling a 2D image model's knowledge into a 3D representation through a differentiable renderer — is one of the more inventive constructions in generative modelling, and score distillation sampling is worth understanding on its own terms as a way of using a generative model as a loss function.

Concepts

  • Representations, and their tradeoffs
    • Explicit: meshes (efficient, hard to optimise topology), point clouds (simple, no surface), voxels (regular, cubic memory)
    • Implicit neural fields: a network mapping coordinates to properties — signed distance functions, occupancy, or radiance. Continuous, compact, and needing a rendering step to become an image
    • 3D Gaussian splatting as the current pragmatic answer: an explicit set of anisotropic Gaussians rasterised directly, giving real-time rendering and much faster fitting than a neural field
  • NeRF and inverse rendering
    • A network mapping position and view direction to colour and density, rendered by volumetric ray marching, optimised so rendered views match photographs. Reconstruction by gradient descent through a renderer
    • Positional encoding as the detail that makes it work at all — without it the network cannot represent high-frequency geometry
    • The costs: hours of optimisation per scene, slow rendering, and a representation specific to one scene. Instant-NGP's hash grids and splatting were the responses
  • Generation without 3D data, which is the module's centrepiece
    • Score distillation sampling: optimise a 3D representation so that its renderings, from random viewpoints, look like plausible samples to a frozen 2D diffusion model. The image model is never fine-tuned; it is used as a critic
    • The mechanism: add noise to a rendering, ask the diffusion model to denoise it, and use the difference as a gradient on the 3D parameters. A generative model repurposed as a differentiable loss
    • The characteristic failures — the Janus problem (multiple faces, because every viewpoint is scored as if it were the front), oversaturation from the high guidance scales SDS needs, and low diversity
    • Variational score distillation and later refinements addressing the saturation and diversity problems
    • The alternative now competitive: multi-view diffusion — generate several consistent views and reconstruct — plus feed-forward models trained on synthetic 3D datasets, which produce a mesh in seconds rather than an hour of optimisation
  • Where this is used — asset creation for games and film, product visualisation, and the reconstruction side (photogrammetry from casual video) which is more mature than the generation side

Work through

  • NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis — the paper that started the field. The positional-encoding ablation is the part that explains why it works.
  • 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering — the explicit representation that displaced neural fields for most reconstruction work, and the differentiable rasteriser behind it.
  • DreamFusion: Text-to-3D Using 2D Diffusion — score distillation sampling. Read the derivation of the SDS gradient; it is the transferable idea.
  • Diffusers — its 3D pipelines, for running the methods rather than only reading them.
  • Neural Fields in Computer Vision (CVPR 2022 tutorial) — the taxonomy — coordinate encoding, architecture, forward map, supervision — with NeRF as one point in it. Then ask why an explicit primitive later beat the implicit one.
  • 3D Gaussian Splatting reference implementation — train it on your own photographs and watch adaptive densification. The heuristic deciding where Gaussians split is where the quality actually comes from.
  • Nerfstudio documentation — the camera conventions and data-format pages, which is where 3D reconstruction breaks first for anyone arriving from 2D generation.

Practice

Fit a Gaussian splatting scene from a set of photographs you take yourself and inspect where it fails — thin structures, reflections, and regions covered by few views. Then run a score-distillation text-to-3D method on five prompts and count how many exhibit the Janus problem. You are done when you can explain each Janus failure in terms of what SDS is scoring at each sampled viewpoint.

Where people go wrong

Treating the Janus problem as a bug to be tuned away. Score distillation scores each rendered viewpoint independently against a 2D prior that has no notion of which side of the object it is looking at, and a prior trained mostly on front-facing photographs will happily judge every view as needing a face. The fixes are structural — view-conditioned priors, multi-view diffusion, or explicit 3D supervision — and no amount of guidance tuning removes it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain how score distillation sampling turns a 2D generative model into a loss function on 3D parameters, and say why the Janus problem is a consequence of that construction rather than an artefact.

M17

Molecules, proteins and scientific generation

≈6 h

This is the application where generative modelling has produced results with consequences outside a screen, and where the constraints are completely different: outputs must be physically valid, symmetries must be respected exactly rather than approximately, and success is measured in a laboratory. It is also where equivariance — a hard architectural constraint — earns its place, which is a useful contrast with the rest of this page's empiricism.

Concepts

  • What changes when the data is physical
    • Validity is binary and checkable: a molecule with impossible valences is not a poor sample, it is not a molecule. Generation has a verifier, which is rare on this page
    • Symmetry as a hard requirement — a molecule's energy is invariant to rotation and translation, so the model must be equivariant or it wastes capacity learning a symmetry it could have been given
    • Evaluation is downstream and expensive: docking scores, then synthesis, then assay. The gap between a good metric and a useful molecule is the field's central difficulty
  • Molecular generation
    • Representations: SMILES strings (sequence models apply directly, validity is not guaranteed), molecular graphs (validity enforceable, permutation invariance required), and 3D point clouds with atom types
    • Equivariant diffusion on 3D coordinates — E(3)-equivariant networks so that rotating the input rotates the output identically, by construction
    • Conditional generation for the task that matters: generate a ligand conditioned on a protein binding pocket
    • The honest caveat: high scores on docking and drug-likeness metrics have repeatedly failed to translate into experimental success, and the metrics are gameable
  • Protein design
    • The structure-prediction context — AlphaFold solving the forward problem well enough that the inverse problem became worth attacking
    • RFdiffusion: diffusion over backbone coordinates, fine-tuned from a structure-prediction network, generating novel folds and binders conditioned on functional-site constraints — with experimental validation, which is what distinguishes this from most of the page
    • Programmable generative models over the whole protein space with conditioning on symmetry, shape and function
    • The inverse-folding step — given a backbone, design a sequence that folds to it — as the necessary second stage
  • Other scientific applications and the shared pattern — crystal structures, materials, and the closed loop where a generative proposal is filtered by a physics simulator or an assay before anything is believed. The lesson generalises: generative models are proposal distributions, and the verifier is what makes them useful

Work through

  • Equivariant Diffusion for Molecule Generation in 3D — E(3)-equivariant diffusion, and the clearest demonstration of why the symmetry constraint pays.
  • De Novo Design of Protein Structure and Function with RFdiffusion — the protein-design result, with experimental validation. Read the conditioning section for how functional constraints enter.
  • Illuminating Protein Space with a Programmable Generative Model — Chroma, for the whole-protein-space formulation and the conditioning interface.
  • Highly Accurate Protein Structure Prediction with AlphaFold — the context. Read it for why the inverse problem became tractable once the forward one was solved.
  • RFdiffusion — generate a backbone, then check it with a structure predictor. That self-consistency loop is how the field decides a design is real, and doing it once explains the evaluation culture.
  • De Novo Protein Design (Nobel lecture) — thirty years of design before diffusion arrived, which is what makes RFdiffusion intelligible — a physics-based pipeline that swapped its sampler.

Practice

Train a small generative model on a molecular dataset — a SMILES-based sequence model is enough — and measure three things: validity, uniqueness and novelty. Then implement a simple equivariance check on any 3D model you use: rotate an input, run the model, and verify the output rotates identically to numerical precision. You are done when the validity rate is a number you can quote and the equivariance check passes exactly rather than approximately.

Where people go wrong

Optimising a docking score. Docking is a fast approximation to binding affinity with well-known systematic errors, and a generative model optimised against it will find its failure modes reliably and enthusiastically — producing molecules that score superbly and do nothing. This is the reward-hacking problem from the reinforcement-learning literature in a domain where the reward model is a physics approximation. Treat any in-silico score as a filter, never as an objective.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why equivariance is imposed architecturally here rather than learned from data, and describe the verification pipeline that has to sit between a generated molecule and any claim about it.

M18

Editing, control and personalisation

≈6 h

Generating a plausible image from a prompt is a demo; changing one thing in an existing image while leaving everything else alone is a product. This module covers the control mechanisms that make these models usable in a workflow, and it depends directly on M08 — inversion is only well defined because the probability-flow ODE is deterministic.

Concepts

  • Inversion, which is the prerequisite for editing a real image
    • Running the deterministic sampler backwards to find the noise that would produce a given image; the accumulated error that makes naive DDIM inversion imperfect
    • Why guidance breaks it: inversion is exact only at guidance scale 1, and editing needs higher scales. Null-text inversion optimises the unconditional embedding to close that gap
    • The alternative that sidesteps it — train the model to edit directly on instruction pairs, which removes inversion from the loop entirely
  • Spatial control
    • Inpainting and outpainting by masking, and the naive approach's seam artefacts versus models fine-tuned for the task
    • Image-to-image: partially noise an input and denoise from there, with the noise strength as a direct control over how much changes
    • ControlNet: a trainable copy of the encoder taking a conditioning image — edges, depth, pose, segmentation — with zero-initialised connections so training starts from the unmodified model. The zero-init is the detail that makes it stable
    • T2I-Adapters as the lighter alternative, and the attention-manipulation methods (prompt-to-prompt) that edit by intervening on cross-attention maps rather than by adding a module
  • Personalisation — teaching a model a specific subject or style
    • Textual inversion: learn a new token embedding only, leaving the model frozen. Tiny, composable, limited in fidelity
    • DreamBooth: fine-tune the model on a few images with a rare identifier token, plus a prior-preservation loss to stop the model forgetting the general class. Higher fidelity, larger artefact, and prone to overfitting on small sets
    • LoRA as the standard compromise, and why the community converged on it: small files, composable, good fidelity
    • Encoder-based personalisation (IP-Adapter and similar) requiring no per-subject training at all, which is what production systems use
  • The consistent thread — every one of these is a way of injecting information the text prompt cannot carry, and the design question is always where in the model to inject it: the embedding, the cross-attention, a parallel branch, or the weights

Work through

  • Adding Conditional Control to Text-to-Image Diffusion Models — ControlNet, and the zero-convolution design that makes it trainable without destabilising the base model.
  • DreamBooth: Fine Tuning Text-to-Image Diffusion Models for Subject-Driven Generation — including the prior-preservation loss and the language-drift failure it prevents.
  • Null-Text Inversion for Editing Real Images Using Guided Diffusion Models — for why inversion under guidance is hard and how it is repaired.
  • Prompt-to-Prompt Image Editing with Cross-Attention Control — editing by manipulating attention maps, which is the most direct evidence of what cross-attention is doing.
  • Diffusers — the ControlNet, inpainting and IP-Adapter pipelines, for running all of this.
  • ComfyUI — the node graph makes the pipeline's separable parts explicit. Swap a scheduler, insert a ControlNet, change the guidance scale, and watch what each one actually controls.
  • r/StableDiffusion — where control and personalisation recipes get tested at scale weeks before anyone writes them up. No evaluation discipline whatsoever — take the techniques, verify the claims.

Practice

Take a real photograph and edit it four ways: DDIM inversion then a modified prompt, image-to-image at three noise strengths, masked inpainting, and ControlNet conditioned on its own extracted depth map. Score each on whether the intended change happened and whether anything unintended did. Then personalise a model on five images of one subject with both textual inversion and LoRA and compare fidelity and file size. You are done when you can state which method to reach for given a specific editing goal.

Where people go wrong

Expecting inversion to round-trip exactly. DDIM inversion accumulates discretisation error and is exact only at guidance scale 1, so encoding and decoding a real image at a useful guidance scale already changes it before any edit is applied — and the drift is then attributed to the edit. Check the round-trip first: invert and regenerate with an unchanged prompt, and whatever moved is your floor.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why ControlNet's zero-initialised connections matter for training stability, and describe what DreamBooth's prior-preservation loss prevents and what happens without it.

M19

Preference tuning for generative models

≈5 h

A model trained by maximum likelihood on web images produces samples that match the training distribution, which is not the same as producing images people want. Post-training against human preference is the same idea as the LLM page's alignment stage, adapted to a multi-step sampling process — and the adaptation is not trivial, because the reward arrives only at the end of a trajectory.

Concepts

  • The objective gap — likelihood training targets the data distribution, and the data distribution contains a great deal nobody wants generated. Aesthetic quality, prompt adherence and safety are all properties of the desired distribution, not the empirical one
  • Reward models for images — aesthetic predictors trained on rating data, human-preference models trained on pairwise comparisons of generations, and CLIP-based alignment scores. Each is a proxy with its own exploitable failure modes
  • The methods, in order of how they handle the trajectory
    • Reward-weighted fine-tuning and rejection sampling: generate many, keep the highest-scoring, fine-tune on those. Simple, stable, and it captures a large fraction of the available gain
    • Direct reward backpropagation through the sampler: differentiate the reward all the way back through the denoising chain. Effective and memory-hungry, requiring gradient checkpointing through the trajectory
    • Policy-gradient methods treating denoising as a multi-step decision process — DDPO — which handles non-differentiable rewards at the cost of RL's instability
    • Diffusion-DPO: the LLM page's direct preference optimisation adapted to diffusion, avoiding an explicit reward model entirely
  • Reward hacking, which is more visually obvious here than anywhere else on this page
    • Aesthetic reward models are reliably exploitable: over-optimisation produces oversaturated, high-contrast, centrally composed images with a characteristic look, because that is what the raters preferred on average
    • Diversity collapse — the model converges toward the reward model's favourite region of image space
    • KL regularisation to the base model as the standard restraint, exactly as in RLHF, and the same over-optimisation curve
  • The safety dimension — filtering training data, unlearning concepts from a trained model, and the finding that concept erasure is frequently superficial and recoverable by fine-tuning or by a paraphrased prompt

Work through

  • Training Diffusion Models with Reinforcement Learning — DDPO, framing denoising as a multi-step decision problem, with the reward-hacking examples shown rather than described.
  • Diffusion Model Alignment Using Direct Preference Optimization — Diffusion-DPO, the adaptation of the LLM method and the simplest thing that works.
  • Human Preference Score v2 — a preference model trained on large-scale human comparisons, and the analysis of what it does and does not capture.
  • Erasing Concepts from Diffusion Models — concept erasure, and the later work showing how superficial it often is.
  • Hugging Face Spaces — compare a preference-tuned model against its base on your own prompts rather than the demo's. The aesthetic gap is real and the prompt-following gap often is not.

Practice

Take an aesthetic reward model and fine-tune a small diffusion model against it by rejection sampling. Track the reward and, separately, a diversity metric and a prompt-adherence metric across training. You are done when you can show the reward rising while at least one other measure falls, and can describe the visual signature of the over-optimised model — because that signature is what an unregularised aesthetic objective always converges to.

Where people go wrong

Optimising an aesthetic score without a diversity check. Aesthetic reward models were trained on human ratings that favour saturated, high-contrast, centrally composed images, and a model pushed hard against one converges on exactly that — every output looks like the same photograph of a different subject. It scores well and is less useful than the model you started with. Always report a diversity measure alongside the reward, and keep the KL restraint.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name three ways to optimise a diffusion model against a reward and the tradeoff each makes, and describe the visual signature of aesthetic reward over-optimisation well enough to recognise it in someone else's samples.

M20

Evaluation, and why it is unsolved

≈6 h

This field cannot reliably measure its own progress, and the metric it has used for a decade is known to be a poor proxy for the thing it claims to measure. That is not a footnote — it means published comparisons are weaker than they appear, and it means you have to build a task-specific evaluation. This module is what is wrong with the standard metrics and what to do instead.

Concepts

  • Why likelihood does not work here — it is unavailable for GANs, a loose bound for VAEs, sensitive to dequantisation choices for flows, and empirically a poor predictor of sample quality even where it is exact. A model can have excellent likelihood and produce bad samples, and the reverse
  • FID, and its documented problems
    • What it is: the Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to Inception-v3 features of real and generated images
    • The Gaussian assumption is wrong — the feature distribution is not Gaussian, so the distance is not measuring what it claims
    • Strong sample-size dependence and bias, so FID computed on 10,000 images is not comparable to FID on 50,000
    • Sensitivity to preprocessing: resizing implementation, JPEG compression and image library have all been shown to change FID by amounts comparable to reported improvements between papers
    • An ImageNet-classifier feature space that captures object identity and misses much of what humans judge; disagreement with human raters on cases that matter
    • It conflates quality and diversity in one number, so two models failing in opposite directions can score identically
  • The alternatives, and what each catches
    • Precision and recall for generative models, separating fidelity from coverage — which is the decomposition FID refuses to give you and is what detects M11's distillation diversity loss
    • CLIPScore for text–image alignment, with the caveat that it inherits every one of CLIP's compositional weaknesses, so it cannot detect the failures M13 catalogues
    • Compositional benchmarks — GenEval, T2I-CompBench — using object detection and VQA models to check counting, spatial relations and attribute binding programmatically
    • Human evaluation: pairwise comparison with enough raters, inter-rater agreement reported, and the arena format. Expensive, and still the ground truth
    • Memorisation and novelty checks, which almost nobody reports and which M21 argues should be standard
  • Modality-specific difficulties — video needs temporal consistency measured separately from per-frame quality, audio essentially requires listening tests, and 3D needs geometric accuracy alongside rendered appearance
  • What to actually do: define the axes you care about, build a prompt set that probes each, measure fidelity and diversity separately, use human comparison for the final judgement, and report the sample size and preprocessing for anything automatic

Work through

  • GANs Trained by a Two Time-Scale Update Rule Converge to a Local Nash Equilibrium — where FID was introduced, so you can see what it was proposed to do.
  • The Role of ImageNet Classes in Fréchet Inception Distance — the analysis showing FID is dominated by ImageNet class structure and can be manipulated without changing perceptual quality.
  • Improved Precision and Recall Metric for Assessing Generative Models — the fidelity/coverage decomposition, which is what you should report alongside or instead of FID.
  • GenEval: An Object-Focused Framework for Evaluating Text-to-Image Alignment — programmatic compositional evaluation using detection models.
  • On Aliased Resizing and Surprising Subtleties in GAN Evaluation — the paper showing that resizing implementation alone changes FID by more than many reported improvements.
  • clean-fid — resizing implementation and JPEG quantisation move FID by more than most papers' claimed improvements. This is the evidence, and it should be your default computation.
  • TorchMetrics: Fréchet Inception Distance — read as documentation of a metric's assumptions: feature extractor, normalisation, sample-count bias. Note where it disagrees with clean-fid, and why that matters.
  • GenEval — object-focused scoring — counting, spatial relations, attribute binding — with a detector instead of a distribution distance. The concrete answer to what FID cannot see.
  • OpenReview (ICLR) — read the review thread of a diffusion paper you know well. The objections about baselines, guidance scales and FID protocols are the ones this module says you should raise.

Practice

Compute FID for one fixed set of generated images under four conditions: two different resize implementations, two sample sizes, and with and without JPEG compression. Record the spread. Then compute precision and recall for two models with deliberately different failure modes — one over-saturated and low-diversity, one diverse and lower quality — and check whether FID distinguishes them. You are done when your FID spread from preprocessing alone is comparable to the difference between the two models, which is the module's argument reproduced on your own data.

Where people go wrong

Comparing FID values across papers. Different sample sizes, resize implementations, feature-extractor weights and reference sets each move the number by amounts comparable to the improvements being claimed, and papers rarely report all four. FID is usable as an internal signal when you control every one of those variables and hold them fixed; as a cross-paper comparison it is close to meaningless, and the field has known this for years.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name three specific things FID does not measure, explain why precision and recall separate what FID conflates, and design an evaluation for a text-to-image system that would catch a diversity collapse.

M21

Memorisation, provenance and misuse

≈5 h

These models are trained on data whose licensing is contested, they can reproduce that data verbatim, and their outputs are now good enough to be used against people. Each of those is a technical problem with a measurable extent as well as a legal and social one, and this module treats the technical side — because "how much does it memorise" and "can this be detected" have empirical answers that the surrounding argument frequently ignores.

Concepts

  • Memorisation, measured
    • Diffusion models reproduce training images near-verbatim under the right prompt, at a rate strongly driven by duplication in the training set — the same relationship as on the language page
    • Extraction attacks: prompt with a caption from the training data, generate many samples, and look for consensus across seeds. Simple and effective
    • The consequences: a privacy exposure, a copyright exposure, and evidence that the model is not always generalising
    • Mitigations with measured costs — deduplication (the highest-value single step), caption diversification, and differentially private training which works and degrades quality substantially
  • Training data and its licensing
    • Web-scraped image–text datasets, opt-out mechanisms and their limited practical effect, and the several jurisdictions where this is actively litigated
    • Style: not protectable in most jurisdictions, and simultaneously the thing artists most object to being reproduced — a genuine gap between the legal and the ethical question rather than a confusion
    • Data-attribution research attempting to trace an output's influence back to training examples, and how far it currently gets
  • Provenance and detection
    • Invisible watermarking of generated content, and its robustness to the standard attacks — crop, compress, regenerate, or pass through another model
    • Content credentials (C2PA): cryptographically signed provenance metadata attached at capture or generation. It authenticates what is signed rather than detecting what is not, which is the right framing and a real limitation since metadata is trivially stripped
    • Post-hoc detection classifiers, and the honest assessment that they generalise poorly to unseen generators and degrade rapidly as generators improve
    • The asymmetry that shapes the whole area: detection is an arms race the detector loses, provenance is a supply-chain problem that requires adoption. Neither is a solution alone
  • Misuse, concretely — non-consensual imagery, which is the dominant real-world harm by volume; fraud and impersonation; and the liar's dividend, where the existence of convincing fakes lets genuine evidence be dismissed. Model-level safety filters, their circumvention, and the limits of what a model provider can control once weights are public

Work through

  • Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models — the memorisation attack, with the duplication relationship measured.
  • Understanding and Mitigating Copying in Diffusion Models — the caption-duplication analysis and the practical mitigations, which is the constructive companion.
  • C2PA Content Credentials specification — the provenance specification; read the threat model, which is clearer than most commentary about what it does not do.
  • LAION-5B: An Open Large-Scale Dataset for Training Next Generation Image-Text Models — reread here for what is actually in an open training set and how it was assembled.
  • LAION-5B — the release page's filtering methodology, read against the memorisation results. The duplication that drives verbatim reproduction is a property of decisions documented here.

Practice

Run a memorisation study on an open model: take captions from its training set, generate many samples per caption, and search for near-duplicates against the training images using a perceptual hash. Report the rate. Then take a watermarked generation and attack the watermark — crop, JPEG at low quality, resize, and pass it through an image-to-image pass — and record which survive. You are done when you have a memorisation rate and a watermark-survival table, both from your own measurements.

Where people go wrong

Treating watermarking as a solution to misuse. Invisible watermarks survive some transformations and not others, are absent from open-weights models that anyone can modify, and are removable by regenerating the image through a second model. They are useful for provenance in a cooperative supply chain and close to useless against a determined adversary. Systems that depend on detecting generated content are building on a foundation that is eroding as generators improve.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the relationship between training-set duplication and memorisation rate, and explain why provenance and detection are different problems with different threat models rather than two approaches to one problem.

M22

The frontier

≈4 h

The last five years of this field have been a sequence of reformulations that each made the previous framing look like a special case. This module is where the next one might come from, and it includes the one place this page and the language-model page converge — discrete diffusion, which would be a genuine architectural change to text generation if it works at scale.

Concepts

  • Discrete diffusion for text
    • The motivation: autoregressive generation is sequential by construction, so a diffusion model over discrete tokens could generate in parallel, revise earlier tokens, and control global structure directly
    • The formulations — absorbing-state masking, and score-entropy approaches that generalise score matching to discrete spaces
    • Current status: closing the gap with autoregressive models on likelihood, with genuine advantages in parallel generation and infilling, and not yet clearly better at scale. This is the live one to watch
    • Why it matters beyond text: it would unify this page's mathematics with the language page's application entirely
  • Unified multimodal generation — single models generating text and images and audio in one autoregressive or diffusion stream, and the open question of whether the modalities should share one objective or be bridged. The tokenise-everything approach from M02 versus diffusion-for-continuous-modalities
  • Where few-step generation goes — whether one-step generation can match multi-step quality without adversarial help, and what the flow-map framing from M11 implies about the limit
  • The data question, which is sharper here than for text — image and video training data is contested legally, licensed corpora are far smaller, and synthetic data raises the model-collapse concern from the deep-learning page in a domain where the tails are exactly the interesting part
  • Controllability as the actual product frontier — precise, compositional, iterative control is what separates a demo from a tool, and it remains the most-requested and least-solved capability
  • Evaluation, which gates all of the above: without a metric anyone trusts, the field cannot tell a real advance from a preprocessing difference, and M20's problems are the bottleneck on knowing whether any of this section's questions have been answered

Work through

  • Discrete Diffusion Modeling by Estimating the Ratios of the Data Distribution — score entropy for discrete spaces, the most principled discrete-diffusion formulation.
  • CVPR 2026 Tutorial on Principles of Diffusion Models — the current unified view of the field, and the best single snapshot of where the frontier sits.
  • arXiv cs.CV — follow specific groups rather than the listing; this literature moves by lab.
  • An Introduction to Flow Matching and Diffusion Models — its closing chapters, which are honest about which parts of the framework are settled and which are conjecture.
  • Diffusion language models — written before discrete diffusion worked, and useful for that: it enumerates why text resists the continuous formulation, which is the checklist any new result must clear.
  • Hugging Face Papers — for discovery with an artefact attached — weights or a demo the same day, so you can check a claim rather than a figure. Popularity-ranked, so never read it as importance.
  • CVPR — watch the tutorial and workshop programme rather than the proceedings. That is where a subfield gets surveyed by the people who moved it, once a year.

Practice

Implement a small discrete diffusion model over a toy sequence task — a masked absorbing-state formulation is enough — and compare it against an autoregressive model of matched size on the same data. Measure likelihood, sample quality, and generation wall-clock at several parallel-step counts. You are done when you can state where the parallel-generation advantage actually appears and where the autoregressive model still wins.

Where people go wrong

Believing a new formulation is better because it is more elegant. Flow matching genuinely improved training and sampling, which is why it was adopted — but several equally elegant reformulations produced no measurable gain and are now footnotes. The question to ask of any new framing is what it lets you do that you could not do before: fewer sampling steps, a simpler objective, a new modality, better scaling. If the answer is only that the derivation is cleaner, wait for the ablation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state what discrete diffusion would buy over autoregressive text generation and what currently stops it, and name the experiment that would settle whether it scales.

Capstone

Two projects. The first builds the machinery; the second is the honest evaluation that most work in this field skips.

Project 1 — a conditional generative model, built from the objective up.

  • Scope: train an autoencoder to compress your data; implement flow matching and DDPM on the same latents and compare them; build a DiT backbone with cross-attention conditioning; implement three samplers (Euler, Heun, a higher-order solver) and plot quality against function evaluations for each; add classifier-free guidance and characterise its artefacts across scales; distil to a four-step model and measure what that costs in diversity; then add one control mechanism from M18 and demonstrate it working on real inputs.
  • Draws on: M03, M07–M12, M18.
  • Done when: you can present a quality-versus-compute curve covering all three samplers and the distilled model, a guidance sweep with the artefact onset marked, and a working conditional control — plus a statement of your autoencoder's reconstruction ceiling measured directly.
  • Stretch: apply the same pipeline to a second modality — audio via a codec, or 3D via a rendering loss — and report what had to change.

Project 2 — an evaluation that would survive review.

  • Scope: take three open text-to-image models and evaluate them properly. Build a compositional prompt set covering counting, spatial relations, attribute binding and text rendering; measure fidelity and coverage separately rather than reporting FID alone; quantify FID's own sensitivity to sample size and preprocessing on your data; run a small human pairwise study with inter-rater agreement reported; and run a memorisation check against the training set where one is available.
  • Draws on: M13, M19, M20, M21.
  • Done when: you can rank the three models on each axis separately and identify at least one case where the automatic metrics and the human judgement disagree — which is the finding that makes the exercise worth doing.
  • Stretch: fine-tune one model against an aesthetic reward and re-run the whole evaluation, showing what improved and what regressed.

Mastery check

  • You can place a generative model in the taxonomy and predict whether it will fail by blurring or by dropping modes.
  • You can derive the ELBO and state what the gap to the true likelihood consists of.
  • You can explain why normalizing flows constrain their architecture, naming the quantity that must stay cheap.
  • You can explain mode collapse as a consequence of the objective rather than an optimisation failure.
  • You can derive denoising score matching and say why the partition function disappears.
  • You can write the DDPM forward process, its closed form and the training loss from memory.
  • You can state what the probability-flow ODE preserves and what determinism buys.
  • You can write the classifier-free guidance equation and say what distribution it samples from.
  • You can state the conditional flow matching theorem and why it makes training tractable.
  • You can explain how a consistency model generates in one step and what that costs.
  • You can compute the compute saving from a latent downsampling factor and explain the autoencoder ceiling.
  • You can name the failure modes of text-to-image models and trace each to a cause.
  • You can explain why temporal consistency is video's binding constraint and name three responses.
  • You can explain why residual vector quantisation beats a single codebook at equal bitrate.
  • You can explain score distillation sampling and why the Janus problem follows from it.
  • You can say why equivariance is architectural rather than learned in molecular generation.
  • You can explain why inversion under guidance is imperfect and how to measure your floor.
  • You can name three things FID does not measure and what to report instead.
  • You can state the relationship between training-set duplication and memorisation.

  • Information Theory — the ELBO is a compression bound, score matching has an information-theoretic reading, and rate–distortion theory is the right frame for M12's autoencoder ceiling. That page develops all three properly.
  • Classical Artificial Intelligence — M17's verification pipeline and M19's reward hacking are both instances of the specification problem that page's decision-theory and alignment modules formalise.
  • Stochastic calculus and numerical methods for SDEs — M08 used Itô calculus and solvers without proving anything. The underlying discipline is where the higher-order samplers and the convergence guarantees come from.
  • Optimal transport — the Wasserstein distance in M05 and the couplings in M10 are the entry points to a deep field, and the Benamou–Brenier dynamic formulation is exactly the object flow matching learns.
  • Computer graphics and inverse rendering — M16 is one module on a discipline. Differentiable rendering, the rendering equation, and physically based materials are what a serious 3D generation system has to sit on top of.

Articles, essays & blogs

An unusual amount of this field's real explanation lives on two or three blogs. Sander Dieleman's in particular functions as a textbook that happens to be published in instalments: each post takes one confusion the papers leave implicit and resolves it, and several of them are the only written account of their subject. They are listed individually rather than as one entry because they map onto different modules.

  1. R-82 What are Diffusion Models? — Lilian Weng, 2021, updated since

    The reference post: every formulation in one notation, with the DDPM bound derived line by line, DDIM, classifier and classifier-free guidance, latent diffusion, and the consistency-model and distillation material added in later revisions. Use it as the notation bridge when two papers disagree about whether the model predicts noise, data or velocity — it states all three and the conversions.

    Core Free Intermediate ~90 min
    Used in M07
  2. R-83 Generative Modeling by Estimating Gradients of the Data Distribution (blog) — Yang Song, 2021

    The post that makes the score picture click, with the figure everyone reproduces: the score field is only well-estimated where data lives, which is exactly why naive Langevin fails and why noise annealing fixes it. Read it before the score-SDE paper in M08; it is the same argument with the measure theory removed.

    Core Free Intermediate ~45 min
    Used in M06 M08
  3. R-84 Guidance: a cheat code for diffusion models — Sander Dieleman, 2022

    Why classifier-free guidance works, why it is not a Bayesian update on anything, and why a technique with no principled justification became mandatory in every deployed text-to-image system. Read alongside M09; the follow-up post on the geometry of guidance is the sequel for anyone who wants the picture in latent space.

    Core Free Intermediate ~40 min
    Used in M09
  4. R-85 Perspectives on diffusion — Sander Dieleman, 2023

    Eight ways to look at the same model — variational autoencoder, score matching, SDE, ODE flow, autoregression in frequency, recurrent denoiser, and more — with the point that fluency means holding several at once, because each paper you read has silently picked one. The single most useful thing to read at the end of M08.

    Core Free Advanced ~50 min
    Used in M08
  5. R-86 The paradox of diffusion distillation — Sander Dieleman, 2024

    Asks the question M11 is built around: if a one-step student can match a fifty-step teacher, what were the other forty-nine steps for? The answer — that iterative sampling is doing work the architecture could in principle do but cannot be trained to do directly — reframes distillation as evidence about optimisation rather than as an engineering trick.

    Core Free Advanced ~40 min
    Used in M11
  6. R-87 Diffusion is spectral autoregression — Sander Dieleman, 2024

    Shows that on natural images the reverse process resolves low spatial frequencies first and high ones last, so diffusion is approximately autoregression over the frequency spectrum — which explains why noise schedules are resolution-dependent and why the same schedule fails when you change image size. The best single explanation of an effect practitioners hit constantly and rarely name.

    Free Advanced ~45 min
    Used in M12
  7. R-88 Generative modelling in latent space — Sander Dieleman, 2025

    Why almost every large generative model now runs in a learned latent space, what the autoencoder is actually buying (perceptual compression, not information compression), and how the choice of latent regularisation propagates into the generator's failure modes. Read with M12, where the two-stage design is introduced.

    Free Advanced ~45 min
    Used in M12
  8. R-89 Diffusion language models — Sander Dieleman, 2023

    Written before discrete diffusion worked, and valuable now for exactly that reason: it enumerates the structural reasons text resists the continuous formulation — no meaningful interpolation between tokens, no perceptual redundancy to destroy gradually — which is the list any modern discrete-diffusion result has to be read against. Pair with M22.

    Free Advanced ~35 min
    Used in M22
  9. R-90 Flow-based Deep Generative Models — Lilian Weng, 2018

    The change-of-variables formula, the determinant problem, and the coupling-layer trick, worked through with the Jacobians written out for NICE, RealNVP and Glow. Read with M04 — flows are the one family on this page where the whole design story is "which invertible map has a cheap determinant", and this is where that becomes obvious.

    Free Intermediate ~50 min
    Used in M04
  10. R-91 From GAN to WGAN — Lilian Weng, 2017

    Derives the original minimax objective, shows where it becomes a Jensen–Shannon divergence, and then shows why that divergence is vacuous when the supports do not overlap — which is the whole motivation for the Wasserstein version. The cleanest written path through the argument M05 turns on.

    Free Intermediate ~50 min
    Used in M05
  11. R-92 The Annotated Diffusion Model — Niels Rogge & Kashif Rasul, Hugging Face, 2022

    DDPM reimplemented in one notebook with the paper's equations interleaved with the code that computes them, including the U-Net with its time embeddings and attention blocks. The fastest route from "I followed the derivation" to "I have trained one", and the reference to check your own implementation against when the samples come out grey.

    Core Free Intermediate ~2 h
    Used in M07
  12. R-93 The Illustrated Stable Diffusion — Jay Alammar, 2022

    Traces one prompt through the whole system — tokeniser, text encoder, latent noise, U-Net loop, decoder — with a diagram at each hand-off, which is the level at which most people's mental model of text-to-image is actually wrong. Read before M13; it is the map the module then fills in.

Communities & staying current

This field's half-life is short enough that a page like this one is out of date in places by the time you read it. These are the places to check what has changed, ordered roughly from most signal to most noise.

  1. R-118 arXiv cs.CV recent listings — arXiv, daily

    Where essentially all of this work appears first, months before any venue. Scanning titles daily for a fortnight is the fastest way to calibrate what a normal contribution looks like versus a significant one; cs.LG and eess.AS are the companion listings for the flow-matching theory and the audio work respectively.

    Public reviews and rebuttals for most of the papers on this page.     The venue where the image, video and 3D generative work lands, and — more usefully — where the tutorials get taught. Open-access proceedings mean every paper is free; the tutorial and workshop programme is where to look each year for a current survey of a subfield rather than an individual result.

    A curated daily selection with author comments and, usually, links to weights and demos — which is the useful property, because it lets you check a claim against an artefact the same day.
