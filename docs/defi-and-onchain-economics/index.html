What this topic is

Decentralised finance is what happens when you rebuild financial primitives — exchange, lending, derivatives, money issuance — as public programs that anyone can call and nobody can stop. The interesting part is not that this is possible but that it forces every mechanism to be stated explicitly. A traditional market maker's inventory policy is private; an automated market maker's is a published equation. A bank's liquidation policy is discretionary; a lending protocol's is a function anyone can call for a bounty. That transparency is what makes the field studyable, and it is also why its failures are so precisely documented.

Three ideas carry the weight. Automated market making replaces the order book with an invariant, which makes liquidity provision permissionless and makes the provider a systematic loser to informed flow. Over-collateralisation replaces credit assessment with a price feed, which converts every lending protocol into an oracle-dependency problem. And composability — any contract can call any other, atomically — makes the whole system a single program with no owner, which is simultaneously the source of the innovation and the transmission mechanism for every failure. This page assumes the machine underneath; that is Blockchain Systems.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes you can read a smart contract, that you know what an account, a transaction and gas are, and that you are comfortable with algebra and basic probability. It does not assume a finance background, and it deliberately teaches the microstructure you need rather than referring you elsewhere for it.

  • No idea what the EVM, an account or gas is — read Blockchain Systems M01–M03 and M09–M12 first; this page does not re-teach them
  • No finance background — M04 covers what you need, and Trading and Exchanges is the reference behind it
  • Cannot read Solidity — patch it with Cyfrin Updraft before M02
  • Want the survey before the detail — read SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) straight through; it is the map this page fills in

Not covered here: trading strategy, price forecasting, portfolio construction or any advice about what to buy — this is a syllabus about mechanisms, and a mechanism you understand is not a reason to take a position. Also excluded: token launch and go-to-market mechanics, tax preparation, and the protocol engineering that lives on the sibling page. Nothing here is financial advice and several modules exist specifically to explain why the returns are worse than they look.

Syllabus

M01

What a crypto asset actually is, and what owning one means

≈5 h

Almost every confused argument in this field starts from an unstated answer to "what is this thing". A token is not equity, not a deposit, and usually not a claim on anything — it is a ledger entry whose value comes from a rule about who may change it. Getting this straight first makes M03's tokenomics and M21's regulation tractable, because both are really arguments about what kind of object is being traded.

Concepts

  • Ownership as key control, not as a claim
    • "Your" token is a balance the protocol will let a signature move. There is no registrar, no chargeback, no recovery. Custody is therefore the product, and the field's largest losses are custody failures rather than protocol failures
    • Self-custody, multisig, MPC and custodial accounts as a spectrum of who can move funds and who can be compelled to. Each is a different legal object as well as a different technical one
  • The taxonomy that actually distinguishes things
    • Native protocol assets that pay for blockspace and secure consensus (ETH, SOL); stablecoins that are claims on an issuer; governance tokens that confer a vote and usually no cash flow; LP and receipt tokens that represent a position; NFTs that represent a specific item. These are genuinely different instruments and the word "crypto" flattens them
    • The uncomfortable question to hold throughout: which of these has a cash flow, and for the ones that do not, what is the value argument? Sometimes there is a real one — blockspace demand, fee accrual — and sometimes there is not
  • Property rights as protocol rules
    • Approvals are the sharp edge: an ERC-20 approval is a standing permission that persists until revoked, and unlimited approvals to compromised contracts are a recurring drain vector. Check your own
    • What "immutable" buys and costs: no counterparty can seize your position, and no counterparty can help you when you send to the wrong address
  • The token as a coordination device
    • The strongest honest case for a token is bootstrapping: it pays early contributors and users with a claim on future network value when there is no revenue yet. The strongest honest criticism is that this is a transfer from later buyers to earlier ones unless the network actually becomes valuable
    • Distinguish the cases where a token is load-bearing (staking collateral, fee payment, credible governance) from where it is a fundraising instrument in mechanism costume. M03 and M15 return to this repeatedly

Work through

  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — sections 1–3 for the taxonomy and the vocabulary the literature uses.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapters 4 and 10, on key management and on what a cryptocurrency is as an economic object.
  • DeFi and the Future of Finance — chapters 1–2, for the framing from people who teach this in a finance faculty.
  • 15.S12 Blockchain and Money — lectures 1–4, for the institutional view of what problem this claims to solve.

Practice

Audit your own or a public address's outstanding ERC-20 approvals: list every spender, the allowance, and when it was last used. Then, for the three largest, find out whether the spender contract is upgradeable and who holds the upgrade key. Done when you can name one approval you would revoke and say precisely what it would cost you if the spender were replaced with malicious code tomorrow.

Where people go wrong

Treating a governance token as equity. Equity is a residual claim on assets and earnings with legal enforcement behind it; a governance token is a vote in a system that usually has no obligation to distribute anything and often cannot be compelled to. Some protocols do route fees to token holders and those are genuinely different — but the default assumption should be "no claim", and the burden of proof is on the token. Getting this wrong makes every valuation exercise in M15 meaningless.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what an ERC-20 approval permits, for how long, and why unlimited approvals are the standard and also a standing risk; and state, for one governance token you have heard of, whether it has any claim on cash flow and how you verified that.

M02

Token standards and the places their abstractions leak

≈7 h

Every protocol from M06 onward is composed out of these interfaces, and every one of them has a well-known deviation that has drained real money. This is the module where "an ERC-20 is a token" becomes "an ERC-20 is whatever the deployer wrote, and the standard is a suggestion" — which is the mental shift the rest of the page depends on.

Concepts

  • ERC-20 and its four famous defects
    • The approve race condition: changing an allowance from a non-zero value is front-runnable, which is why increaseAllowance exists and why the standard's own comment warns about it
    • Tokens that do not return a boolean (USDT is the famous one), which is why SafeERC20 exists and why naive integrations revert or, worse, silently succeed
    • Fee-on-transfer and rebasing tokens: the amount received is not the amount sent, and the balance changes without a transfer. Any protocol that caches balances instead of measuring them breaks
    • Decimals are not 18. Assuming they are is a live bug class
  • Approvals done properly
    • EIP-2612 permit for signature-based approvals, and Permit2 as the universal-approval layer. The security trade: one approval to one audited contract instead of dozens to arbitrary ones
  • ERC-721 and ERC-1155
    • 721 for unique items, 1155 for semi-fungible batches with much cheaper minting. tokenURI is a pointer, and where it points decides whether the asset survives the issuer (M16)
    • safeTransferFrom's receiver callback is a re-entrancy vector, which is how several mint-limit bypasses worked
  • ERC-4626, the tokenised vault standard
    • Shares and assets with a conversion rate; the whole point is that integrators can treat every vault identically. Read the standard's own security section
    • The inflation (or "donation") attack: a first depositor mints one wei of shares, donates a large amount directly to the vault, and subsequent depositors round down to zero shares. The mitigations — virtual shares, dead shares, a seeded deposit — are now standard and were not obvious
    • Rounding direction as a security property: always round in the protocol's favour, and know which way that is for deposit versus withdraw

Work through

  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — ERC-20, ERC-721, ERC-1155, ERC-2612 and ERC-4626, including each one's rationale and security sections.
  • OpenZeppelin Contracts documentation — the ERC-20, ERC-4626 and SafeERC20 implementations, read as source rather than as API docs.
  • Cyfrin Updraft — the token and vault sections, if you have not implemented these before.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks — the sections on token-level and accounting bugs.

Practice

Write a minimal ERC-4626 vault, then execute the inflation attack against it on a local fork: deposit one wei, donate a large amount, and watch the second depositor receive zero shares. Then apply the virtual-shares mitigation and confirm the attack fails. Then integrate a fee-on-transfer token and watch your accounting drift. Done when you can state the rounding direction of every conversion in your vault and justify each one.

Where people go wrong

Writing integrations against the standard instead of against the token. "ERC-20" describes an interface, not behaviour: the token may take a fee, may rebase, may have a blocklist, may be upgradeable, may return nothing, may have six decimals. The correct pattern is to measure balance before and after rather than to trust the amount argument, and to enumerate the specific tokens you support rather than claiming to support all of them. Protocols that accept arbitrary tokens are accepting arbitrary code.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain the ERC-4626 inflation attack and why virtual shares fix it; and say what breaks in a vault that caches token.balanceOf(address(this)) when the token charges a transfer fee.

M03

Tokenomics as mechanism design, and as marketing

≈8 h

Token designs are incentive mechanisms, and mechanism design is a field with results. Most token models are not evaluated against those results — they are evaluated against whether the price went up, which is a measurement of the fundraising, not of the mechanism. This module gives you the tools to separate the two, which M15 then applies to whether any of these protocols are businesses.

Concepts

  • Supply, issuance and the distribution question
    • Fixed versus inflationary supply; emission schedules; cliffs and linear vesting for team and investor allocations. Fully diluted valuation versus circulating market cap, and why the gap between them is the single most-omitted number in token analysis
    • Unlock schedules as predictable supply shocks. This is public information and it is repeatedly ignored
  • What the token is supposed to do, evaluated honestly
    • Four defensible jobs: pay for a scarce protocol resource, serve as slashable collateral, confer governance that actually controls something valuable, and accrue fees. Anything else is a distribution mechanism with a story attached
    • The "value accrual" question: if a protocol earns fees and the token has no claim on them, the token's value rests on the expectation of a future governance decision to grant one. That is a real but weak argument and you should be able to state it as such
  • Vote-escrow and its consequences
    • Lock tokens for time, receive boosted voting power and rewards. Curve's veCRV made governance power a function of commitment, which then became a market: protocols bribe voters to direct emissions to their pools — the "Curve wars"
    • The empirical result is worth knowing before admiring the design: veToken systems concentrate power in a few large lockers and create a secondary market in votes, which is the opposite of the stated intent
  • Emissions, mercenary capital and points
    • Liquidity mining pays users in tokens to supply liquidity. It works, and the liquidity leaves when the emissions stop, because the capital was renting yield rather than using the product. Measure retention after emissions end — this is the number that distinguishes a product from a subsidy
    • Points programmes as unpriced, discretionary future airdrops: they let a protocol capture the incentive effect without committing to a distribution, which shifts all the risk to the farmer. Note the mechanism-design reading — an unenforceable promise is a cheap incentive right up until reputation prices it
    • Sybil farming as the equilibrium response, and why airdrop-eligibility criteria have become an adversarial game

Work through

  • Emergent Outcomes of the veToken Model — the whole paper, for what vote-escrow actually produced as opposed to what it intended.
  • The Infinite Machine — the ICO chapters, where most of the distribution patterns in this module were first established.
  • DeFi and the Future of Finance — the chapters on incentives and governance tokens.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — the incentive and governance sections.
  • Token Terminal — pull revenue, fees and token-incentive figures for three protocols and compare emissions against fees earned.
  • a16z crypto State of Crypto — read critically: it is an industry report from an interested party, which makes it a useful primary source on what the industry believes about itself.

Practice

Pick a protocol with a live token. Build its supply schedule from the public vesting data, plot circulating supply against time for the next 24 months, and mark every cliff. Then compute annualised protocol fees and annualised token emissions at current prices, and answer: is this protocol paying out more than it earns? Done when you have both numbers with sources and can state the ratio.

Where people go wrong

Reading a high APY as a return. An APY paid in a token whose supply is inflating at a comparable rate is a transfer, not a yield — you are being paid in claims that are being diluted at the speed you receive them. The question is always what the yield is denominated in, where the money comes from, and who is on the other side. M14 makes this systematic; the habit starts here, and it is the single most valuable habit on this page.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why fully diluted valuation and market cap diverge and when that gap matters; and say what the Curve wars demonstrated about vote-escrow as a mechanism, in one sentence that a designer would find uncomfortable.

M04

Market microstructure, before it is needed

≈8 h

An automated market maker is a market maker, and a market maker is unreadable without adverse selection, inventory risk and the bid-ask spread. This module is placed before M06 deliberately: every attempt to explain AMMs without microstructure produces the "impermanent loss" folk theory, which is wrong in a way that costs people money. Half a course of classical microstructure buys you the whole of M06 and M07.

Concepts

  • What a market maker does and how it is paid
    • Quote two-sided, earn the spread, carry inventory. The spread compensates for three distinct things: order processing, inventory risk, and adverse selection. The third is the one that matters here
    • Adverse selection stated precisely: your counterparties are not random. Informed traders trade with you exactly when your quote is wrong, so you systematically buy before prices fall and sell before they rise. Glosten–Milgrom is the canonical model and the intuition transfers directly to M07
  • Order books and the mechanics of execution
    • Limit versus market orders, maker/taker fees, price-time priority, depth and its shape. Price impact as a function of size against a given book, and why a "price" is meaningless without a size attached
    • Fragmentation and arbitrage: multiple venues quoting the same asset are kept in line by arbitrageurs who are paid for it. Note that this is the mechanism, not a bug — and that on-chain, the arbitrageur's profit is paid by the liquidity provider (M07)
  • Latency, priority and who gets to trade first
    • In electronic markets, the right to trade first is valuable and is bought — colocation, direct feeds, payment for order flow. Read this now so that M12's MEV supply chain reads as a variant of a known structure rather than as a crypto novelty
  • Liquidity as a measurable thing
    • Depth, spread, resilience, and the difference between them. Volume is not liquidity — a market can have enormous volume and no depth, which is precisely the pattern wash trading produces (M17, M22)

Work through

  • Trading and Exchanges — chapters 11–14 and 19–21. This is the reference for everything above; you do not need the whole book and these chapters repay a careful read. (Oxford University Press returns a 202 to automated link checks; ISBN 9780195144703 confirmed via OpenLibrary.)
  • Decentralized Finance MOOC — the market-structure lectures, which bridge from classical microstructure to the on-chain case.
  • The Adoption of Blockchain-based Decentralized Exchanges — for empirical comparison of on-chain and centralised execution quality.

Practice

Pull an order book snapshot for one asset from a public exchange API. Compute the spread, the depth within 1% of mid, and the price impact of a market order for 0.1%, 1% and 10% of that depth. Then simulate a naive market maker quoting a fixed spread against a price series with occasional jumps, and measure its P&L. Done when your naive maker loses money on the jumps and you can explain that loss as adverse selection rather than as bad luck.

Where people go wrong

Treating market making as a fee-collection business. It is a bet that your quotes are closer to fair value than your counterparties' information, and the fee is the compensation for being wrong sometimes. A market maker who quotes without adjusting for informed flow loses steadily to the informed and never notices, because each individual trade looks like a small win. This is exactly the position an automated market maker's liquidity provider is in, which is why M07 exists as a separate module.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain adverse selection to someone who thinks the spread is just a fee; and say why quoting a price without a size is meaningless, with a worked example from your order-book snapshot.

M05

Custody, exchanges and the off-chain layer everyone actually uses

≈6 h

Most volume and almost all new users pass through centralised venues, and the largest losses in the field's history happened there rather than in any smart contract. This module is here because it is the control experiment: the same assets, the same market, and an entirely different trust model — which is what makes the on-chain properties in M06 onward legible as choices rather than as defaults.

Concepts

  • The custody spectrum and its failure modes
    • Self-custody (you lose it, it is gone), hardware wallets, multisig with a threshold, MPC with distributed key shares, qualified custodians with insurance and regulation. Each moves risk between "I make a mistake" and "someone else fails or is compelled"
    • Operational security as the actual discipline: seed handling, transaction simulation before signing, blind-signing on hardware wallets, and address poisoning. Unglamorous and responsible for most individual losses
  • Centralised exchanges as the dominant venue
    • They are custodians running an internal ledger; your balance is a database row and a claim on the company. Deposits are loans. This is not a criticism, it is the correct description, and it decides what happens in bankruptcy
    • Why they persist: better execution, fiat rails, no gas, customer support, and the fact that most people do not want to be their own bank. Any argument that ignores this is not describing the market
  • FTX as a controlled experiment
    • Customer funds were commingled and lent to an affiliated trading firm; the ledger said otherwise. Nothing about it required a technical failure — it required the absence of the segregation and audit that regulated custody imposes. Read it as evidence about what on-chain settlement does and does not remove
    • Mt Gox, Celsius, BlockFi and Voyager as the same shape with different details. The pattern is a custodian taking undisclosed risk with customer assets
  • Proof of reserves, and what it does not prove
    • A Merkle-tree attestation proves the exchange holds assets and that your balance is in the claimed total. It says nothing about liabilities off the tree, about borrowed assets moved in for the snapshot, or about solvency between snapshots. Proof of reserves without proof of liabilities is half a proof, and the industry mostly ships the half
    • ZK-based solvency proofs (M15 of the sibling page) as the direction that would close the gap, and the reasons it has not been adopted

Work through

  • Number Go Up — the Tether and exchange chapters. Faux is a reporter, not an engineer, and the reporting on what these companies were actually doing is the value.
  • The Cryptopians — for the governance and custody dysfunction in the early Ethereum ecosystem, which set several later patterns.
  • Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies — chapter 4 on wallets and key management, which remains the clearest treatment.
  • Safe documentation — the multisig model and module system, as the standard on-chain custody arrangement.
  • 2026 Crypto Crime Report — the sections on where funds are actually stolen, which is a useful correction to where people expect them to be stolen.
  • Unchained — the FTX and Celsius post-mortem episodes, for what the people involved said at the time and afterwards.

Practice

Set up a 2-of-3 Safe on a testnet with three keys you control separately, execute a transaction through it, and then simulate losing one key and recovering. Separately, take one exchange's published proof of reserves and write down precisely what it proves and what it omits. Done when your omissions list includes liabilities and inter-snapshot behaviour, and when your Safe recovery worked without the lost key.

Where people go wrong

Believing "not your keys, not your coins" is a complete analysis. It is a correct slogan about a real risk and it says nothing about the risk it substitutes: self-custody transfers the failure mode from counterparty insolvency to personal operational error, and the base rate of personal error is not low. The right question is which failure you are better placed to prevent, at what size, with what recovery plan — which for most people at most balances has a different answer than the slogan implies.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what a Merkle-tree proof of reserves proves and name two things it cannot; and describe the FTX failure without using the word "hack".

M06

Automated market makers: pricing from an invariant

≈12 h

This is the load-bearing module of the page. The constant-function market maker is the one genuinely new financial primitive to come out of this field, and M07, M08, M10, M11, M12 and M13 all assume you can reason about a bonding curve — where the price comes from, who trades against it, and who pays. Derive it rather than reading about it; the algebra is short and everything downstream depends on having done it.

Concepts

  • The constant-product invariant, derived
    • x·y = k. Marginal price is y/x; a trade of Δx yields Δy = y − k/(x+Δx). Derive the output formula with a fee, then derive slippage as a function of trade size relative to reserves. You should be able to do this on paper in five minutes
    • Why the curve never runs out of either asset, and why price impact grows without bound — the properties that make it safe to deploy without a liquidation mechanism
    • The AMM as a passive, always-quoting market maker with a fixed, publicly known inventory policy. Every property in M07 follows from "publicly known"
  • The family of invariants and what each one is for
    • Constant sum (zero slippage, fully drainable), constant product (Uniswap v2, robust and capital-inefficient), Curve's StableSwap (an interpolation that is nearly flat near the peg and becomes constant-product away from it — read the amplification coefficient as the interpolation parameter), Balancer's weighted geometric mean generalising to n assets with arbitrary weights
    • Choosing an invariant is choosing where you want liquidity concentrated on the price axis. That reframing makes v3 the obvious next step
  • Uniswap v3 and concentrated liquidity
    • Liquidity providers choose a price range; within it they behave as a constant-product pool with virtual reserves; outside it the position is entirely in one asset. Ticks, the √P parameterisation, and why fees are non-fungible per position — so LP positions became NFTs
    • Capital efficiency and its true cost: a narrow range earns more fees per dollar and goes out of range faster, which converts passive liquidity provision into active management. The empirical work on v3 LP returns is unambiguous that most retail LPs lose to it
  • Uniswap v4 and hooks
    • A singleton contract with flash accounting, and hooks that let a pool run custom logic before or after swaps and liquidity changes — dynamic fees, on-chain limit orders, TWAMM, custom oracles. The design converts the AMM from a fixed product into a platform, and moves a large amount of risk into hook code that the core cannot vet
  • What the arbitrageur is for
    • The AMM has no idea what the market price is. It learns only when someone trades against a stale quote — so the pool's price tracks the market because arbitrageurs are paid to correct it. That payment comes from the LPs, and naming it now is what makes M07 land

Work through

  • Uniswap v2 Core — the whole whitepaper. Short, and the fee and oracle design decisions are stated plainly. (Uniswap's host rate-limits automated checks; the PDF loads normally in a browser.)
  • Uniswap v3 Core — sections 1–6, particularly the tick and virtual-reserve mathematics.
  • Uniswap v4 Core — for the hook architecture and flash accounting.
  • An analysis of Uniswap markets — the formal treatment of why CFMM prices track external markets, and under what conditions.
  • Improved Price Oracles: Constant Function Market Makers — Angeris and Chitra's general CFMM framework; this is the paper that makes the whole family one object.
  • Curve documentation — the StableSwap invariant and the amplification parameter.
  • Uniswap v3 Development Book — build a v3 clone from scratch; this is the fastest route from reading the maths to believing it.
  • CS251: Cryptocurrencies and Blockchain Technologies — the AMM lectures, which treat the invariant with more rigour than the whitepapers do.
  • Devcon Archive — search for AMM design talks; the trade-offs discussed on stage rarely reach the documentation.

Practice

Implement a constant-product AMM in a spreadsheet and in Solidity, with a fee. Verify they agree. Compute the output and slippage for trades of 0.1%, 1% and 10% of reserves. Then implement StableSwap's invariant numerically and plot its price curve against constant product for the same reserves — the flat region is the point. Finally, simulate a v3 position with a ±5% range against a random walk and measure how often it goes out of range. Done when you can state, for your v3 position, the fraction of time it earned nothing.

Where people go wrong

Thinking an AMM "sets" a price. It does not — it quotes a function of its reserves, and its reserves change only when someone trades. The pool's quote follows the market because arbitrageurs profit from correcting it, and that profit is a transfer from liquidity providers. Every AMM is therefore a standing offer to trade at a stale price, priced by an equation that cannot see the market. Once you hold that, concentrated liquidity, dynamic fees, oracle manipulation and LVR all become the same conversation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive the constant-product output formula with fees from scratch; and explain what Curve's amplification coefficient interpolates between, and why a stablecoin pair wants that shape.

M07

LP economics and loss-versus-rebalancing

≈9 h

"Impermanent loss" is the field's most successful piece of bad terminology: it names the right quantity, implies it reverses, and measures against the wrong benchmark. Loss-versus-rebalancing fixes all three and turns liquidity provision into a question with an answer. This module needs M04's adverse selection and M06's invariant, and it is the one that determines whether you can evaluate any yield claim on this page.

Concepts

  • Impermanent loss stated correctly, then discarded as the primary tool
    • Divergence loss is the difference between holding the LP position and holding the initial basket. Derive it for constant product: it depends only on the price ratio, is symmetric, and is not "impermanent" in any useful sense — it is only reversed if the price returns, which is not a plan
    • Why the HODL benchmark is the wrong one: it compares against a portfolio you were not running, and it hides the fact that the loss is paid to a specific counterparty
  • Loss-versus-rebalancing, the right benchmark
    • Compare the LP against a rebalancing strategy that holds the same instantaneous exposure but trades at the market price rather than at the pool's stale quote. The difference is exactly what arbitrageurs extract, it is always non-negative, and it accrues monotonically with volatility and with block time
    • Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden and Zhang give a closed form for constant product: LVR scales with σ²/8 per unit time. The practical consequence is that an LP is short volatility and short latency, whether or not they intended to be
    • The design responses this motivates: dynamic fees, auctioning the right to arbitrage the pool first, oracle-aware AMMs, and shorter block times. All of them are attempts to keep LVR inside the pool
  • The full LP profit-and-loss decomposition
    • Fee income, minus LVR, minus gas, plus incentive emissions. Only fees and LVR are structural; emissions are a subsidy that ends. Do the arithmetic for a real pool before believing any advertised APY
    • The v3-specific empirical finding: a majority of concentrated-liquidity positions studied underperform simply holding, once LVR is accounted for. This is not a fringe claim, and it should reset your prior
  • Active strategies and who actually wins
    • Just-in-time liquidity: add an enormous position in the same block as a large swap, take most of the fee, remove it. Legal, corrosive to passive LPs, and a direct consequence of fees being proportional to liquidity share at the moment of the trade
    • Professional market makers running v3 positions with active rebalancing and external hedges are a different business from a passive LP, and conflating the two is how the retail case gets misjudged

Work through

  • Automated Market Making and Loss-Versus-Rebalancing — the whole paper. This is the central result of the module; work the derivation of the σ²/8 term.
  • Impermanent Loss in Uniswap v3 — for the concentrated-liquidity case worked explicitly.
  • Risks and Returns of Uniswap V3 Liquidity Providers — the empirical study. Read the distribution of outcomes, not the average.
  • An analysis of Uniswap markets — re-read here for the arbitrage-equilibrium argument that LVR quantifies.
  • Dune Analytics — find or fork a dashboard computing LP returns for a specific pool, and reproduce its methodology yourself.
  • Paradigm Research — the LVR-mitigation and auction-design posts, for what the proposed fixes actually change.

Practice

Take one real pool with at least six months of history. Compute realised LVR from price history and reserves, compute fee income over the same period, and produce the net figure against both the HODL benchmark and the rebalancing benchmark. Then answer: at what fee tier would this pool have broken even, and how does that compare to the tier it uses? Done when your two benchmarks give different answers and you can explain exactly why the rebalancing one is the meaningful one.

Where people go wrong

Believing impermanent loss reverses. It reverses only if the price returns to where it started, which is a claim about the future dressed up as a property of the mechanism. Meanwhile the arbitrage profits that caused it have been permanently paid out to third parties. The clearest correction is the LVR framing: you are being paid a fee to provide a standing option to trade at a stale price, and the question — answerable with arithmetic — is whether the fee exceeds the option's value to whoever exercises it.

M08

On-chain lending and the liquidation machine

≈10 h

Lending is the second pillar of DeFi and the clearest example of the field's core substitution: replace credit assessment with over-collateralisation plus a price feed, and every credit question becomes a market-risk question. It needs M06 because liquidations execute against AMMs, and it sets up M09 (CDP stablecoins are lending protocols that mint their own asset) and M10 (the price feed is the whole trust assumption).

Concepts

  • The pooled-lending model
    • Depositors supply to a pool and receive an interest-bearing receipt token; borrowers post collateral and draw against it. No counterparty matching, no credit assessment, no term — everything is variable-rate and perpetual
    • Utilisation-based interest rate curves with a kink: rates rise slowly to a target utilisation and steeply after it, so the curve is a control mechanism for keeping the pool liquid rather than a price of credit. Read a live rate model and identify the kink
    • What this design cannot do: unsecured lending, term structure, and anything requiring identity. Attempts at under-collateralised lending have consistently failed for exactly the reason the design avoided it
  • Collateral, health and the liquidation trigger
    • Loan-to-value limits, liquidation thresholds and the health factor as one number summarising distance to liquidation. Compute the price move that liquidates a given position — it is the number every borrower should know and most do not
    • Liquidation incentives: a bonus paid to whoever repays the debt and seizes collateral. This is an auction for the right to do the protocol's risk management, and it works because it is profitable — which means it stops working exactly when it is not
    • Fixed-bonus versus Dutch-auction liquidation (Maker's flip auctions, Aave's fixed penalty, Euler-style discovery). The trade is between speed and giving away too much collateral
  • Bad debt and how it accumulates
    • If collateral value falls through the debt before liquidation completes, the shortfall is bad debt and someone eats it — a safety module, a reserve, or the depositors. Gas spikes, oracle lag and thin AMM liquidity all delay liquidation exactly when it is most needed
    • Liquidation cascades: forced selling moves the price, which triggers more liquidations. The mechanism is identical to margin spirals in traditional markets, with the addition that everyone can see the liquidation prices in advance
  • The cases worth studying in detail
    • MakerDAO, March 2020 ("Black Thursday"): network congestion plus a zero-bid auction design meant liquidators won collateral for nothing, leaving the protocol undercollateralised. The failure was in the auction parameters, not the price
    • The 2022–23 CRV borrow position: a single borrower's position was large relative to on-chain liquidity for the collateral, so liquidating it would have moved the price through the debt. Concentration risk in a system with no borrower limits
    • Recurring lesson: the liquidation mechanism assumes a liquid market for the collateral, and that assumption is the actual risk
  • Isolated versus pooled risk
    • Shared-pool designs mean one bad collateral asset threatens every depositor; isolated markets and modular designs (Euler, Morpho, Aave's isolation mode) contain it at the cost of fragmenting liquidity. This is the central design argument in lending right now

Work through

  • An Empirical Study of DeFi Liquidations — the whole paper, for how liquidations actually behave rather than how they are specified.
  • Aave documentation — the risk parameters, rate strategy and liquidation sections, with a live market open beside them.
  • Compound documentation — the interest-rate model and the comptroller, as the contrasting implementation that most later protocols forked.
  • DeFi and the Future of Finance — the lending chapters, for the framing against traditional secured lending.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — the lending and liquidation sections of the taxonomy.

Practice

Open a small over-collateralised position on a testnet lending market. Compute in advance the exact price at which you are liquidated, then move the oracle price (or use a fork with a manipulated feed) and watch it happen. Then write a liquidation bot that monitors health factors and executes when profitable, and backtest it on historical data to see how much competition you would have had. Done when your predicted liquidation price matches the actual one to within rounding, and your bot's backtest shows how many liquidations you would have lost to faster bots.

Where people go wrong

Assuming over-collateralisation makes a lending protocol safe. It converts credit risk into market risk plus oracle risk plus liquidity risk, and those three fail together in exactly the conditions that trigger liquidations — volatile prices, congested blocks, thin books. Black Thursday was not a price problem; the collateral was worth more than the debt the whole time. It was an auction mechanism that could not execute under load. Every parameter in a lending protocol is an assumption about market conditions during a crisis.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the liquidation price of a position from its collateral, debt and threshold; and explain why a protocol can hold collateral worth more than its debt and still accrue bad debt.

M09

Stablecoins: three designs, three failure modes

≈10 h

Stablecoins are the field's one unambiguous product-market fit — the settlement asset for almost everything on this page and, increasingly, a payments rail with its own statute. They are also where the most money has been destroyed. This module needs M08 because a CDP stablecoin is a lending protocol that mints its own liability, and it sets up M21, because stablecoins are the part of crypto regulators actually reached agreement on.

Concepts

  • Fiat-backed: a claim on an issuer's reserves
    • USDC and USDT hold cash and short-dated Treasuries and redeem at par to approved parties. The peg holds because arbitrage against redemption holds it, so the mechanism is only as strong as redemption access — which retail does not have
    • The March 2023 USDC depeg: $3.3bn of reserves at Silicon Valley Bank, and USDC traded to $0.88 over a weekend when redemption was closed. The reserves were fine; the redemption channel was not. This is the mechanism to understand, not the headline
    • Attestation versus audit, reserve composition disclosure, and why Tether's history of both is the standing argument in this area
  • Crypto-collateralised: over-collateralised debt positions
    • DAI/USDS: lock volatile collateral, mint the stablecoin as debt, keep the position over-collateralised, liquidate when it is not. The stability fee and the savings rate are the two levers; the peg is maintained by arbitrage against the ability to mint and repay at $1 of debt
    • The centralisation drift worth stating plainly: to hold the peg at scale, the collateral mix moved heavily toward USDC and tokenised Treasuries — so the flagship decentralised stablecoin is substantially backed by centralised assets. That is a real tension, not a gotcha, and the protocol has debated it publicly for years
    • The peg stability module as an explicit swap facility, and what it does to the decentralisation claim
  • Algorithmic: a peg backed by a reflexive promise
    • The seigniorage-share design: a stable asset and a volatile absorber, with mint-and-burn arbitrage between them. It works while the absorber has value, and the absorber's value depends on confidence in the peg. That circularity is the whole story
    • Terra/UST, May 2022: 1 UST was always redeemable for $1 of LUNA. When UST broke, redemption minted enormous LUNA supply, which destroyed the LUNA price, which made the backing worthless, which accelerated the run — roughly $40bn erased in days. Anchor's 20% yield had drawn deposits with a subsidy that could not last. Reconstruct the sequence as a mechanism; it is the single most instructive failure in the field
    • Why "algorithmic stablecoin" is now largely a warning label, and what the surviving partially-collateralised designs changed
  • The current landscape and the regulatory turn
    • Yield-bearing and institutionally issued stablecoins — PYUSD, tokenised Treasury funds — and the delta-neutral "synthetic dollar" designs whose backing is a funding-rate trade rather than a reserve. Read the last of those against M11: their yield is a basis trade and it can invert
    • The GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27, July 2025) imposes 1:1 reserve requirements in cash and short-dated instruments, monthly disclosure, and a federal/state licensing path, and excludes compliant payment stablecoins from securities treatment. MiCA has been fully applicable in the EU since December 2024. The regulatory perimeter is now the binding constraint on design, which was not true two years ago

Work through

  • What Drives the (In)stability of a Stablecoin? — the whole paper, for depeg dynamics treated empirically rather than anecdotally.
  • Sky documentation — the CDP mechanics, stability fee and peg stability module, read as the reference CDP implementation.
  • GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27) — the reserve, disclosure and licensing sections. Read the statute rather than commentary on it; it is more specific than the summaries suggest.
  • Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) — the asset-referenced and e-money token titles, for the contrasting European approach.
  • Number Go Up — the Tether reporting, for how the largest stablecoin's reserves became a public question.
  • DeFi risks and the decentralisation illusion — the BIS view of stablecoins as a run-prone liability, which is the framing regulators brought to the statute.
  • Broken Money — the payment-systems chapters, for why a dollar-denominated settlement rail is worth anything in the first place.

Practice

Model the Terra death spiral: implement the UST/LUNA mint-burn arbitrage, seed it with the approximate pre-collapse supply and price, then introduce a large UST sell and let the mechanism run. Vary the redemption cap and see whether any value stops the spiral. Then, separately, chart USDC's price and SVB news over the March 2023 weekend and mark the exact moment redemption reopened. Done when your Terra model reproduces the reflexive collapse and you can state what parameter, if any, would have prevented it.

Where people go wrong

Treating the peg as the thing to watch. The peg is an output; the mechanism holding it is the thing. UST held its peg for two years while its stabilisation mechanism was structurally reflexive, and USDC broke its peg while fully backed by assets that were never at risk of loss. The questions are always: who can redeem, at what size, on what timescale, and what happens to the backing if everyone tries at once. A stablecoin that has never depegged has not thereby demonstrated anything.

M10

Oracles: the trusted input in a trustless system

≈8 h

A blockchain cannot observe anything outside itself, so every protocol that depends on a price depends on someone reporting it. This is the largest trust assumption in DeFi and the most exploited component after bridges. It sits after M06 and M08 because you now know exactly what the price feeds and what happens downstream when it is wrong.

Concepts

  • The oracle problem stated precisely
    • On-chain consensus can agree on the contents of a ledger; it cannot agree on a fact about the world. Any external fact enters through a party that could lie, so the design question is only how expensive lying is made
    • The consequence people miss: a protocol's security is bounded by its oracle's security. A perfectly audited lending protocol with a manipulable price feed is a manipulable lending protocol
  • The three architectures
    • Push (Chainlink): a committee of node operators aggregates off-chain sources and writes on-chain when a deviation threshold or heartbeat is hit. Costs gas continuously; the update threshold is itself an attack surface, because between updates the on-chain price is stale by up to that threshold
    • Pull (Pyth and similar): publishers sign prices continuously off-chain, and the consumer submits the signed update in the same transaction as their action. Cheaper and fresher; shifts the responsibility for freshness to the caller, who must validate staleness themselves
    • On-chain TWAP: read the AMM itself, time-averaged. No external trust at all, and manipulable by anyone willing to move the pool for the averaging window
  • Manipulation, costed
    • Spot price from a pool is trivially manipulable with a flash loan, because the capital cost is zero within one transaction. This is the single most common DeFi exploit primitive and M13 covers the mechanics
    • A TWAP raises the cost: you must hold the price away from equilibrium for the window, paying arbitrageurs the whole time. Compute that cost for a real pool — it is a concrete number, and comparing it against the profit available downstream is the actual security analysis
    • Low-liquidity assets are the recurring victim: the manipulation cost scales with pool depth, so listing a thin asset as collateral imports an oracle vulnerability regardless of the oracle used
  • Mango Markets, and the question it left open
    • In October 2022 Avraham Eisenberg took a large position, moved the thin MNGO perpetual market to inflate his own collateral value, and borrowed roughly $110m against it. He described it publicly as "a highly profitable trading strategy" using the protocol as designed
    • He was convicted in April 2024; in May 2025 the convictions were vacated on venue grounds and for insufficient evidence of misrepresentation. Whatever your intuition, the legal status of "profitable use of a protocol exactly as written" is genuinely unsettled, and that matters for how protocols are designed and defended
  • Practical hardening
    • Multiple independent sources with deviation checks; circuit breakers on implausible moves; staleness checks on every read; caps on borrowing against thin collateral; and using a redemption-rate oracle rather than a market price for assets that have one (LSTs). Each has a cost and none is free

Work through

  • SoK: Oracles from the Ground Truth to Market Manipulation — the whole paper; it is the systematisation this module follows.
  • Chainlink documentation — the data-feeds architecture, deviation thresholds and heartbeats. Note what the docs say about staleness checks being the integrator's job.
  • Pyth Network documentation — the pull model and the confidence interval, which is a genuinely useful piece of metadata most oracles do not provide.
  • Uniswap v2 Core — the TWAP oracle section specifically, which is where on-chain price oracles came from.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks — the oracle-manipulation attack taxonomy with worked cases.

Practice

Pick a mid-liquidity token pair. Compute the capital required to move its spot price 20%, and then the cost of holding it there for a 30-minute TWAP window, including the arbitrage you would be paying. Compare that number against the borrowing power that 20% move would unlock on a lending market that used it as collateral. Done when you can state whether the manipulation is profitable and by how much — and that number, not a vibe, is what an oracle-risk assessment is.

Where people go wrong

Believing a TWAP is "manipulation-resistant". It is manipulation-expensive, and the cost is finite and computable. Whether it is high enough depends entirely on the depth of the pool and the size of the position it protects — the same TWAP is adequate for one market and trivially breakable for another. The second error is omitting staleness checks: both push and pull oracles can serve an old price, and a contract that reads without checking the timestamp will happily liquidate on data from an hour ago.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the cost of manipulating a TWAP for a given pool and window; and explain what the Mango Markets case did and did not settle about using a protocol as designed.

M11

On-chain derivatives and the perpetual future

≈9 h

Perpetual futures are crypto's second genuinely novel instrument after the AMM, and they carry most of the leverage in the ecosystem. They need M10, because a perp is an oracle with a settlement mechanism attached, and they set up M14, because the largest "real yield" strategies are funding-rate trades on these markets.

Concepts

  • The perpetual, and why the funding rate is the invention
    • A futures contract with no expiry cannot converge to spot by settlement, so it converges continuously: longs pay shorts (or the reverse) a periodic funding payment proportional to the premium of the perp price over the index. The funding rate is a price-anchoring mechanism implemented as a cash flow
    • Derive the equilibrium: persistent positive funding means the market is structurally long and paying to be, which is both a sentiment indicator and a yield opportunity for whoever takes the other side hedged. This is the basis trade of M14
    • Mark price versus index price versus last price, and why liquidations use the mark — using last price makes wick-hunting free
  • The three on-chain design families
    • Order-book (dYdX, Hyperliquid): a real matching engine, either on an app-specific chain or with off-chain matching and on-chain settlement. Best execution, and it reintroduces a sequencer or validator set as the ordering party
    • Pool-as-counterparty (GMX-style): liquidity providers take the other side of every trade as a pool. Zero price impact for the trader, and the LPs are short the traders' aggregate P&L — a very different risk than AMM liquidity provision, and one that has been exploited by traders taking correlated positions
    • Virtual AMM: a bonding curve with no real reserves, pricing positions synthetically. Elegant and has repeatedly struggled with funding imbalance and manipulation
  • Margin, liquidation and the insurance layer
    • Initial and maintenance margin, cross versus isolated, and auto-deleveraging as the mechanism of last resort — where a profitable trader is forcibly closed because the loser's margin is gone. It is the on-chain equivalent of a clearing-house waterfall, made visible
    • Insurance funds, socialised losses, and the fact that in a bankruptcy cascade someone must absorb the shortfall. Knowing who, in a given venue, is the risk analysis
  • Options, and why they keep failing on-chain
    • Options need a pricing model, a volatility surface and active hedging by market makers. On-chain, capital efficiency is poor, gas makes continuous hedging expensive, and liquidity fragments across strikes and expiries. Several serious attempts have wound down; the honest summary is that this remains unsolved

Work through

  • Fundamentals of Perpetual Futures — the whole paper, for the funding mechanism derived properly rather than described.
  • Hyperliquid documentation — the order-book, margin and liquidation mechanics of the highest-volume on-chain perp venue.
  • GMX documentation — the pool-as-counterparty model, read specifically for what risk the liquidity providers are taking.
  • dYdX documentation — the app-chain order-book design, as the third point of comparison.
  • Trading and Exchanges — the chapters on futures and margin, for the traditional-market baseline these depart from.
  • Epicenter — the episodes with perpetual-venue designers, for the funding and liquidation trade-offs they made and why.

Practice

Pull a year of hourly funding rates for one large perp market. Compute the annualised return of a delta-neutral basis trade — long spot, short perp — net of fees, and plot its drawdowns. Mark every period where funding was negative and the trade lost money. Done when you can state the strategy's annualised return, its worst drawdown, and the specific market conditions in which it inverts. That last part is the whole point.

Where people go wrong

Treating the funding rate as free yield. It is compensation for taking the unpopular side of a crowded position, and it goes negative precisely when everyone piles into the same delta-neutral trade. The 2022 unwind and several since showed the pattern: a strategy that pays reliably for months, attracts levered capital, then inverts and liquidates the crowd simultaneously. Any yield that comes from a crowded positioning is a yield that disappears when the crowd is largest.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain how funding keeps a perpetual anchored to spot and what happens when it cannot; and say what risk a GMX-style liquidity provider is taking that a Uniswap liquidity provider is not.

M12

MEV as a market: who extracts, who pays, and what it costs users

≈9 h

Ordering is valuable, and by now you know exactly why: an AMM quotes a stale price (M06), an oracle updates on a threshold (M10), and a liquidation pays a bounty (M08). Every one of those creates a race. This module covers the market — the strategies, the money, who ultimately pays. The plumbing — builders, relays, ePBS — is M16 of Blockchain Systems, and the two are written to be read together.

Concepts

  • The strategy taxonomy, with its economics
    • Arbitrage: between two AMM pools, or between a DEX and a centralised exchange. This is the largest category by value and it is the LVR of M07 viewed from the extractor's side. It is not theft — it is the mechanism that makes pool prices correct — and it is still paid by LPs
    • Liquidations: a competitive race for the bounty in M08. Efficient, and it means the bonus is largely competed away into priority fees rather than kept
    • Sandwiching: buy in front of a user's swap, let their trade move the price, sell behind it. This one is a direct transfer from a specific user, it depends entirely on seeing their transaction before inclusion, and it is the category worth being angry about
    • Long-tail: NFT mint sniping, liquid-staking rate arbitrage, oracle-update backruns
  • Who actually pays, traced
    • Sandwiching is paid by the swapper as worse execution. Arbitrage is paid by liquidity providers as LVR. Liquidation MEV is paid by the liquidated borrower via the penalty. In every case the money comes from someone who did not choose to participate in an auction
    • The transparency point: this extraction exists in every market, and here it is measurable to the wei. That is a genuine contribution of the field, and it should make you suspicious of the equivalent numbers you cannot see elsewhere
  • Order flow as the scarce resource
    • Private RPC endpoints and protected transaction routing keep a user's trade out of the public mempool, which prevents sandwiching. Order-flow auctions go further and sell the right to execute against the flow, returning some proceeds to the user or their wallet
    • The centralisation consequence: exclusive order flow is the main input to builder dominance, so protecting users at the transaction level concentrates power at the block-building level. This is a real trade, not a solved problem, and it is where this module hands over to the sibling page
  • Mitigations at the application layer
    • Slippage limits and deadlines as the minimum defence — and note that a wide default slippage tolerance is what makes a sandwich profitable, so the wallet's default is a security parameter
    • Batch auctions (CoW Protocol's uniform clearing price) remove intra-batch ordering value entirely; RFQ and solver-mediated execution move price competition off-chain; encrypted mempools attack the problem at the base layer

Work through

  • Flash Boys 2.0 — the whole paper. The founding document; read the taxonomy and the consensus-instability argument together.
  • Quantifying Blockchain Extractable Value — for the measurement methodology, which is what lets you check any claim about magnitudes.
  • Flashbots Writings — the order-flow-auction and protected-transaction posts, from the party that built most of the infrastructure.
  • Dune Analytics — find a sandwich-attack dashboard and reproduce one of its queries against raw transaction data.
  • Automated Market Making and Loss-Versus-Rebalancing — re-read here: arbitrage MEV and LVR are the same money seen from two sides.
  • ethresear.ch — the MEV and order-flow-auction threads, where the mitigation proposals are argued before they ship.

Practice

Find a real sandwich attack in a recent block: a pair of transactions from one address bracketing a victim swap on the same pool. Compute the attacker's profit, the priority fee they paid, and the victim's slippage relative to the price they would have received alone. Then re-execute the victim's swap in a simulation with a 0.5% slippage limit instead of the default and show the sandwich becomes unprofitable. Done when you have the three numbers and the counterfactual.

Where people go wrong

Lumping all MEV together as theft. Arbitrage and liquidations are the mechanisms that keep prices correct and lending solvent — a chain without them would be worse, not better. Sandwiching is different in kind: it produces no price correction and transfers value from an identifiable user who did nothing but submit a transaction. Policy conversations that fail to separate these end up proposing to eliminate the price-correction mechanisms, which is why the serious proposals target ordering rights and censorship rather than extraction as such.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain who pays for arbitrage MEV and connect it to M07's LVR; and say why routing transactions privately protects individual users while concentrating power in block builders.

M13

Composability, flash loans and the exploit taxonomy

≈11 h

Everything in M06 through M11 can call everything else, atomically, within one transaction. That is the field's genuine innovation and its systemic risk in the same property. This module needs all the preceding primitives, because an exploit is not a bug in one contract — it is a reachable state across several, and you cannot see it until you know what each one assumes about the others.

Concepts

  • Atomic composability, stated as a capability
    • A transaction either fully succeeds or fully reverts across every contract it touched. That means you can construct a multi-step financial operation with no counterparty risk and no settlement risk — the thing traditional finance spends enormous effort simulating
    • The same property means an attacker can construct a multi-step operation that only executes if it is profitable, which removes all the execution risk from an attack
  • Flash loans, and what they actually changed
    • Borrow any amount with no collateral, provided you repay within the transaction. This is not a lending product; it is a capital-requirement remover. Every economic attack that needed a large balance sheet became available to anyone with gas money
    • The correct reading: flash loans did not create new vulnerabilities, they made existing ones cheap to exploit. A protocol whose security relied on "nobody has $200m" had no security, and now everyone knows it
  • The attack anatomy, as a repeatable method
    • Borrow → manipulate a state variable some other protocol reads (a pool price, a share rate, a vote weight) → interact with the victim while its view is wrong → unwind → repay → keep the difference. Every step is one of the modules above
    • Worked cases to reconstruct from traces: bZx (2020) as the first at scale; Harvest Finance (share-price manipulation via pool imbalance); Beanstalk (2022, a flash-loaned governance majority executing a malicious proposal inside one transaction — the bridge to M19); Euler (2023, a donation-based health-factor bug)
  • Economic exploits versus code bugs
    • A code bug is behaviour the developer did not intend. An economic exploit is behaviour they did intend, at parameters they did not anticipate. The second class is invisible to conventional auditing, and it is where the largest losses now sit
    • Read-only re-entrancy as the crossover case: no state is corrupted in the victim, a view function simply returns a mid-callback value, and a third protocol prices something wrong. Curve-pool integrations were drained this way repeatedly
  • Systemic risk from shared dependencies
    • When many protocols use the same oracle, the same stablecoin, the same LST or the same wrapped asset, a single failure propagates through all of them atomically. Nobody maintains the dependency graph, and the composability that makes integration free makes the blast radius free too
    • Circuit breakers, rate limits, deposit caps and time delays as the mitigations that trade composability for containment — and the reason protocols resist them

Work through

  • Attacking the DeFi Ecosystem with Flash Loans for Fun and Profit — the whole paper, including the optimisation framing of maximising an attack's profit.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks — the full taxonomy with the incident dataset. This is the reference for the module.
  • Damn Vulnerable DeFi — the flash-loan, oracle and governance challenges. Do them before reading the solutions.
  • rekt.news leaderboard — pick four incidents above $50m and reconstruct each from its post-mortem and its on-chain trace.
  • Etherscan — used to read the exploit transactions themselves in the internal-trace and state-diff views.
  • Foundry — write the exploits as forked-mainnet tests; this is how security researchers actually work.

Practice

Reproduce a real flash-loan exploit as a Foundry test against a mainnet fork pinned to the block before the attack. Get it to run and profit. Then write the one-line invariant that, had it been enforced, would have prevented it — and add it as a test that fails on the vulnerable version and passes on the patched one. Done when the exploit runs green and the invariant test correctly discriminates between the two versions.

Where people go wrong

Calling flash loans the vulnerability. The vulnerability is a protocol reading a state variable that can be moved within a single transaction, or one whose security assumed an attacker's capital constraint. Removing flash loans would raise the cost of these attacks and not eliminate them — anyone with a large balance sheet could still run them, and several have. The fix is on the reading side: use manipulation-resistant inputs, and never assume an attacker is poor.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a flash loan makes a previously-uneconomic attack free, using a specific protocol as the example; and describe read-only re-entrancy in terms of who reads what, and when.

M14

Where yield actually comes from

≈8 h

Every mechanism so far generates something advertised as yield, and the advertised number is almost never decomposed. This module is the systematic version of the habit M03 introduced: for any given return, name the source, name who is paying, and name the risk being compensated. If you cannot do all three, you are the source.

Concepts

  • The five actual sources, and nothing else
    • Protocol issuance — staking rewards paid by inflation. Real in nominal terms, diluting in real terms unless the network's value grows. Compare against the issuance rate, always
    • Fees paid by users — trading fees, borrowing interest, protocol revenue. The only fully self-sustaining category
    • Someone else's loss — LP fees offset by LVR (M07), perpetual funding paid by the crowded side (M11), liquidation penalties (M08). Zero-sum, and you should know which side you are on
    • Token emissions — a subsidy. Not a yield. Ends
    • Off-chain interest — tokenised Treasuries and money-market funds. Genuinely exogenous, and it arrives with an issuer, a jurisdiction and a redemption process, which is to say with counterparty risk
  • Staking, liquid staking and restaking, layered
    • The base staking yield is issuance plus priority fees plus MEV. An LST wraps it and takes a cut. A liquid restaking token pledges the same stake to additional services for additional reward and additional slashing exposure — so the yield stack and the risk stack grow together, and only one of them is advertised
    • Depeg risk on the wrapper as a distinct risk from the underlying: stETH traded below ETH during the 2022 unwind while nothing about the underlying stake had changed
  • The basis trade at scale
    • Long spot, short perpetual, collect funding — the strategy behind most "synthetic dollar" products. It is a genuine yield with a genuine risk: funding inverts, the venue fails, or the collateral is liquidated in a gap. M11's exercise is the prerequisite here
  • Real-world assets, and what they import
    • Tokenised Treasury funds bring an actual risk-free rate on-chain, and with it a transfer agent, a custodian, a legal wrapper and eligibility restrictions. The yield is real; the "decentralised" adjective is not. Being precise about that trade is the module's point
  • Risk-adjusting, concretely
    • Smart contract risk, oracle risk, governance risk, custody risk, depeg risk, liquidity risk on exit. A 12% yield with a 3% annual probability of total loss is not a 12% yield. Historical exploit base rates from M13's dataset are the input to that calculation, and almost nobody performs it

Work through

  • DeFi and the Future of Finance — the chapters on yield and risk, for the finance-faculty framing of exactly this question.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — the risk taxonomy sections.
  • DefiLlama — take the top ten yield opportunities and decompose each into the five sources above. Most will not survive it.
  • Token Terminal — protocol revenue versus token incentives, which is the same question at the protocol rather than the position level.
  • DeFi risks and the decentralisation illusion — the BIS analysis of leverage and procyclicality, which is where these yields go in a drawdown.

Practice

Take five advertised yields across different protocols. For each, write one paragraph naming the source, the payer, and the risk. Then compute a risk-adjusted figure using an annual loss probability drawn from the historical exploit rate for that protocol category. Rank the five before and after. Done when the ranking changes, and when at least one of the five turns out to have no identifiable payer other than later entrants.

Where people go wrong

Comparing an on-chain APY to a savings rate. The savings rate is nominal, insured and denominated in the unit you spend. An on-chain yield is variable, uninsured, denominated in an asset that may move, exposed to contract and oracle failure, and frequently paid in a token that is inflating. The comparison is only meaningful after decomposition and risk adjustment, and after that adjustment a lot of headline numbers stop looking anomalous — which is the correct outcome, not a disappointment.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name the payer for any yield you are shown within a minute of seeing it; and explain why a liquid restaking token's yield and its risk grow together while only the first is in the marketing.

M15

Protocol business models and treasuries

≈7 h

Having taken every mechanism apart, the obvious question is whether any of these are businesses. It is a fair question and it has real answers — a handful earn substantial fees, most do not, and the difference is legible from public data. This needs M03 (what the token is for) and M14 (where the money comes from), and it feeds M19, because a treasury is the thing DAO governance is actually fighting over.

Concepts

  • The revenue question, asked precisely
    • Separate total fees (paid by users) from protocol revenue (retained by the protocol) from token-holder revenue (distributed). Most protocols have the first, some have the second, few have the third — and dashboards routinely conflate them
    • The fee switch as the central governance decision: turning on a protocol take reduces supply-side returns and creates a claim for token holders, and it also strengthens the argument that the token is a security (M21). The two considerations are entangled and both are usually stated separately
  • Where the moat is, if anywhere
    • The code is forkable and usually forked within weeks. What is not forkable: liquidity, integrations, brand, and the order flow that routes through a front end. Note that these are the same network effects that make traditional venues durable, which is a less exciting but more accurate conclusion than "protocols have no moats"
    • The vampire-attack pattern (SushiSwap versus Uniswap) as the empirical test, and what it showed about how much of the moat was liquidity versus habit
  • Treasuries and the denomination problem
    • Most DAO treasuries are overwhelmingly denominated in their own governance token, which means the treasury's value is correlated with the protocol's fortunes exactly when it is needed. This is a well-understood corporate-finance error being repeated at scale
    • Diversification is possible and politically difficult: selling the native token to buy stables reads as insiders exiting. Several large DAOs have argued about this for years without resolving it
    • Protocol-owned liquidity as the alternative — own your liquidity rather than renting it with emissions. OlympusDAO's bonding mechanism popularised it and its own token model collapsed; the POL idea survived the vehicle
  • Costs nobody models
    • Audits, bug bounties, security monitoring, legal, grants and core contributor compensation are the real cost base, and they are paid in a volatile asset. A protocol with $50m of annual fees and a treasury in its own token is not obviously safer than one with $5m in stablecoins

Work through

  • Token Terminal — pull fees, revenue, incentives and treasury composition for five protocols and build the comparison yourself.
  • DefiLlama — TVL, revenue and the fee/revenue split, cross-checked against the above. Where the two disagree, find out why.
  • Emergent Outcomes of the veToken Model — re-read here for emissions as a business cost rather than as a governance design.
  • a16z crypto State of Crypto — the industry's own account of where value accrues, useful precisely because it is an interested one.
  • Uniswap Blog — the fee-switch and governance posts, as the best-documented instance of this decision being made in public.

Practice

Build a one-page financial summary for one protocol: annual fees, protocol revenue, token emissions at market value, treasury size and composition, and estimated operating costs from public grant and compensation data. Then answer: at current emissions, how many years of runway does the treasury have, and what happens to that figure if the token halves? Done when both numbers are sourced and you can say whether this protocol is a business or a subsidy.

Where people go wrong

Reading total value locked as a performance metric. TVL is capital present, not value created, and it is trivially inflated by paying people to deposit — a protocol can double TVL overnight with emissions and be worse off. It also double-counts across composed protocols: the same dollar deposited, wrapped, and re-deposited appears three times. Fees paid by users are the metric that cannot be manufactured, and the gap between the two rankings is instructive.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: distinguish fees, protocol revenue and token-holder revenue for a specific protocol with numbers; and explain why a treasury denominated in its own governance token is a risk-management failure rather than an alignment feature.

M16

NFTs: the mechanics, and what is actually on-chain

≈7 h

The NFT is the simplest object on this page and the most misdescribed. It needs only M02, and the reason it deserves its own module is that almost every claim made about NFTs — permanence, ownership, royalties — is a claim about something that is usually not on the chain at all. Getting the mechanics right is what makes M17's market history readable as a consequence rather than a mystery.

Concepts

  • What the token actually contains
    • ERC-721 stores an owner per token id and a tokenURI. That URI usually points to JSON on a server or IPFS, which points to an image somewhere else. The chain holds a pointer to a pointer
    • The permanence ladder: a mutable HTTPS URL (the issuer can change or delete the art), an IPFS CID (content-addressed, so immutable — but only stored while someone pins it), Arweave (paid-once permanent storage), and fully on-chain SVG or generative code stored in contract bytecode. Only the last is unconditionally as durable as the token
    • Generative on-chain art (Art Blocks and its lineage) as the case where the mechanism and the artwork are genuinely the same object — the token id seeds the script that draws the piece
  • Minting mechanics and their failure modes
    • Allowlists, Dutch auctions, reveal delays and commit-reveal. Why an instant reveal lets snipers buy only the rare items — the metadata is public before the transaction confirms — and why randomness on-chain is hard enough that mint rarity has repeatedly been gamed
    • The safeTransferFrom receiver callback as a re-entrancy vector for bypassing per-wallet mint limits
  • Royalties, and the fact that they were never enforceable
    • ERC-2981 declares a royalty; it does not collect one. Enforcement was always voluntary on the marketplace's part, and once one large marketplace made them optional the equilibrium collapsed within months. Attempts to enforce them via transfer blocklists worked technically and fragmented the market
    • This is the cleanest available case study of a "smart contract guarantee" that was actually a social convention, and it is worth generalising: ask, for any on-chain guarantee, which contract enforces it
  • What ownership conveys, legally
    • Owning the token conveys the token. Copyright stays with the creator unless separately assigned; some projects assign broad licences, most do not, and buyers routinely assume otherwise. The token is a receipt whose meaning is set off-chain

Work through

  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — ERC-721, ERC-1155 and ERC-2981, with attention to what each one does not specify.
  • OpenZeppelin Contracts documentation — the ERC-721 implementation and its enumerable and URI-storage extensions.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks — the NFT-specific incidents, for mint and marketplace exploits.
  • Cyfrin Updraft — the NFT sections, if you want to implement the storage variants yourself.

Practice

Deploy the same collection three ways: metadata on an HTTPS URL you control, metadata on IPFS, and the image generated fully on-chain as SVG. Compare deployment gas for each. Then take three well-known collections and determine, from their contracts, where each one's art actually lives and who could change it. Done when your gas table is complete and you can name at least one prominent collection whose art is mutable by its issuer.

Where people go wrong

Believing an NFT proves ownership of an image. It proves an entry in a contract's mapping. Whether that entry means anything depends on where the art is stored, who can change the pointer, what licence was granted, and whether anyone outside the contract recognises the claim. "On-chain" is a spectrum with a large majority of collections near the bottom of it, and the ones at the top made a deliberate and expensive choice to get there.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain the four levels of NFT metadata permanence and place a given collection on that ladder from its contract; and say why ERC-2981 royalties collapsed, in terms of who was enforcing them.

M17

NFT markets: what happened, what was measurement error, and what survived

≈7 h

The NFT market is the best-documented speculative cycle in the field: a public order book, public wallets and public transaction history, which means claims about it are checkable in a way that claims about most bubbles are not. It needs M04 (thin markets, wash trading, what volume does and does not mean) and M16 (what was being traded), and it is the clearest available training set for reading a market you already suspect.

Concepts

  • The market's structure, which explains most of its behaviour
    • Every item is unique, so there is no order book — only a floor price and a set of individual asks. "Floor price" is the lowest ask, not a clearing price, and it moves on a single listing. Treating it as a valuation is the field's most common analytical error
    • Extreme illiquidity: most tokens in a collection never trade. Marking a portfolio at floor is marking to the most impatient seller, and it is why lending against NFTs kept failing
  • Wash trading, measured rather than alleged
    • Self-dealing between wallets inflates volume and creates the appearance of price discovery. It was strongly incentivised during marketplace token-reward programmes — trade with yourself, earn the reward. The measurement literature finds substantial fractions of reported volume are wash trades on some venues
    • The detection heuristics — closed cycles between address sets, round-trip trades, funding from a common source — are the transferable skill, and they generalise to fungible-token markets (M22)
  • The cycle, and what it demonstrated
    • 2021 mania, the 2022 collapse, and the long grind after it: aggregate volumes fell by well over 90% from peak and most collections went illiquid. Two things are true simultaneously — the speculative layer was a bubble by any reasonable definition, and a small number of assets retained real markets
    • The marketplace war: a competitor made royalties optional and took share, the incumbent followed, creator revenue collapsed. A clean demonstration that in a commoditised marketplace the fee that can be competed away will be
  • What survived, and why those cases are different
    • Generative art with genuine on-chain provenance and a collector base that behaves like an art market rather than a token market
    • ENS names — a functional namespace where the token does a job (M20)
    • In-game and platform assets where the NFT is an inventory record with a working application behind it
    • Ticketing and credentials, where transferability and verification are the actual product
    • The common factor: the token is load-bearing for something, rather than being the whole product. That is the same test M03 applied to fungible tokens, and it gives the same answer
  • NFT financialisation, and why it mostly did not work
    • Fractionalisation, peer-to-pool lending against NFT collateral, and floor-price perpetuals. All of them require a reliable price for an illiquid unique asset, which is M10's problem in its hardest form. Liquidation of NFT collateral into a thin market is the failure mode, and it recurred

Work through

  • NFT Wash Trading — the whole paper, for detection methodology and magnitude estimates.
  • Crypto Wash Trading — the fungible-market counterpart; the statistical tests are the part to take away.
  • Dune Analytics — pull a collection's full trade history and compute the fraction of tokens that have never traded.
  • 2026 Crypto Crime Report — the market-manipulation sections, for how this is measured at scale.
  • Attack of the 50 Foot Blockchain — Gerard as the sceptical baseline. He is often unfair and frequently correct, and reading him against the industry sources is the exercise.

Practice

Take one collection with at least 10,000 items. Compute: the fraction of tokens that have ever traded, the median holding period, the distribution of trades per token, and an estimate of wash-traded volume using closed-cycle detection between address sets. Then compare the floor price against the median realised sale price over the same period. Done when you can state how much of the reported volume you believe, and defend the number.

Where people go wrong

Using floor price times supply as a market capitalisation. The floor is one seller's ask on a thin book; multiplying it by total supply asserts that every holder could sell at that price, which is false by an enormous margin in an asset class where most items never trade at all. The same error applied to a low-float token gives the same wrong answer for the same reason — which is why this module is worth doing even if you have no interest in NFTs.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe two wash-trading detection heuristics and their false-positive modes; and explain why lending against NFT collateral kept failing, in terms of M10's oracle problem and M08's liquidation mechanics.

M18

DAO governance: the mechanism as designed

≈8 h

Every protocol in this syllabus has upgrade keys, parameters and a treasury, and someone controls them. DAO governance is the answer the field converged on, and it is a real mechanism with real components worth understanding before you evaluate it. This module builds the machine; M19 takes it apart with the evidence.

Concepts

  • The standard stack, component by component
    • A governance token confers votes; a proposal encodes executable calldata; a quorum and an approval threshold decide passage; a timelock delays execution; an executor holds the privileged roles. Every real protocol's governance is a configuration of these five, and reading a governance contract means reading those five parameters
    • Off-chain signalling (Snapshot: signed messages, no gas, no execution) versus on-chain execution (Governor contracts: expensive, binding). Most protocols use both — a signal vote followed by an on-chain vote — which means the binding step often has far lower participation than the visible one
    • The timelock is the most important component: it is the window in which users can exit if governance does something hostile. A protocol with an instant-execution multisig has no such window, whatever its documentation says
  • Delegation
    • Token-weighted voting with delegation, because most holders will not vote. Delegates accumulate voting power, publish platforms, and become a de-facto legislature. This is a reasonable response to rational apathy and it also means the mechanism's actual electorate is a few dozen addresses
  • The alternative voting mechanisms and what each targets
    • Quadratic voting: cost grows with the square of votes, so intensity is expressed without wealth dominating. Requires Sybil resistance to mean anything, which is M20's problem and is not solved
    • Conviction voting: preference strength accumulates with time held, so a persistent minority can pass proposals without a snapshot majority
    • Optimistic governance: proposals pass by default unless vetoed, which cuts participation cost and concentrates power in whoever is watching
    • Futarchy: vote on values, bet on outcomes. Elegant, rarely deployed, and the few live experiments are worth following
  • Multisigs and councils, which is what most "DAOs" actually run on
    • Security councils with emergency powers, guardian roles that can pause, and multisigs holding upgrade keys. These exist for good reasons — governance is too slow for an active exploit — and they are also the real locus of control. Read the signer set and the threshold before reading the governance forum
    • Legal wrappers: the Wyoming DAO LLC, the Marshall Islands DAO LLC, foundation structures. These exist because an unwrapped DAO risks being treated as a general partnership with unlimited member liability, which is a genuine and under-appreciated exposure

Work through

  • Moving beyond coin voting governance — Buterin's own critique of the standard mechanism, written from inside it.
  • DAOs are not corporations — for the argument that decentralisation in governance should be evaluated as fault tolerance rather than as democracy.
  • OpenZeppelin Contracts documentation — the Governor and TimelockController implementations, which are what most on-chain governance actually runs.
  • Snapshot documentation — the off-chain voting model and its strategy system.
  • Safe documentation — the multisig and module architecture that sits under most governance executors.

Practice

Deploy a Governor with a timelock on a testnet, pass a proposal that changes a parameter on a contract you own, and execute it end to end. Then, for a real protocol, produce its governance map: token supply and top-ten holder concentration, quorum, threshold, timelock duration, the executor's privileged roles, and the emergency multisig's signers and threshold. Done when you can state, in one sentence, who could change this protocol tomorrow and how long you would have to exit.

Where people go wrong

Reading a Snapshot vote as a decision. Snapshot votes are signed messages with no execution power — they signal, and someone must then act. In many protocols that someone is a multisig, which means the "decentralised governance" is an advisory poll followed by a discretionary action by a named group. That is not necessarily bad, and it is not what the word governance implies. Always find the contract that actually holds the privileged role.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why the timelock is the component that most determines a user's actual safety; and describe what quadratic voting is trying to buy and the assumption it requires in order to buy it.

M19

DAO governance: what actually happened

≈8 h

M18 gave you the mechanism; this is the evidence about how it performs, and the evidence is not kind. Token voting concentrates, participation is low, and the attack surface is real and has been exploited. It sits after M13 as well as M18, because the most spectacular governance failure was a flash-loaned proposal executed in a single transaction — governance capture as a composability exploit.

Concepts

  • The concentration finding, stated with numbers
    • Across studied DAOs, voting power is extremely concentrated — a very small fraction of holders control the large majority of votes — and turnout typically sits in the single digits to mid-teens as a percentage. Read the current research rather than a figure quoted here; the direction of the finding has been stable across several years and datasets
    • Why it is structural rather than a startup phase: token-weighted voting is one-token-one-vote, early allocations are concentrated by construction, and rational apathy means small holders do not vote. Delegation concentrates further. Every proposed fix so far has traded the problem for a Sybil-resistance problem
  • Governance attacks, worked
    • Beanstalk (April 2022): an attacker flash-borrowed enough governance tokens to pass their own emergency proposal within a single transaction, draining roughly $180m. The lesson is that voting power computed from a current balance is manipulable, and snapshot-based voting power plus a timelock is the fix
    • The Tornado Cash governance takeover (May 2023): a malicious proposal contained hidden code that granted the attacker all governance tokens after passing. Voters approved something they had not read, which is the ordinary case rather than the exceptional one
    • Vote buying and bribe markets as the persistent, legal version: if voting power is transferable and votes are valuable, a market clears. Curve's bribe ecosystem is the mature example, and it is not an attack — it is the mechanism working as specified
  • The governance research literature, which predicted this
    • Collective action problems, rational ignorance, and the iron law of oligarchy are century-old results about voluntary organisations, and DAOs have reproduced all three. The field's tendency to treat each finding as a novel discovery is itself worth noticing
    • What corporate governance solved with mechanisms DAOs mostly lack: fiduciary duty, disclosure requirements, minority-holder protections, and a court. Whether those can be reproduced on-chain is the open question
  • Where it has worked, honestly
    • Parameter management by informed delegates in mature protocols has a reasonable record — risk parameters on lending markets get adjusted competently and publicly. Treasury allocation and strategic direction have a much worse one. The pattern suggests DAOs work as technocratic parameter committees and struggle as legislatures

Work through

  • On the Centralization of Governance Power in Decentralized Autonomous Organizations — the whole paper; it is the current systematic measurement.
  • Moving beyond coin voting governance — re-read here against the evidence rather than as a design document.
  • rekt.news leaderboard — the Beanstalk entry, then reconstruct the proposal from the transaction.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) Attacks — the governance-attack category.
  • Emergent Outcomes of the veToken Model — for vote markets as the equilibrium of transferable governance power.

Practice

Pick a DAO and compute its real voting concentration: pull the delegate registry and voting history for the last twenty proposals, compute the Nakamoto coefficient (how many addresses are needed to pass a proposal), and the turnout distribution. Then find one proposal that passed with the support of fewer than five addresses. Done when you have the coefficient and can name the addresses.

Where people go wrong

Assuming low participation means holders are satisfied. It means voting costs more than it is worth to a small holder, which is the predicted equilibrium of any token-weighted system and tells you nothing about preferences. The related error runs the other way: treating every governance vote as theatre. Parameter votes on mature lending protocols are consequential, well-argued and genuinely contested — the mechanism performs very differently on technical parameters than on money, and lumping them together loses the only interesting finding.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain the Beanstalk attack and why snapshot-based voting power plus a timelock defeats it; and say why token-weighted voting concentrates structurally rather than incidentally.

M20

Identity, reputation and funding public goods

≈7 h

M19 ended on a problem with no solution inside token voting: you cannot weight by person without knowing who is a person. Sybil resistance is the missing primitive under quadratic voting, fair airdrops, reputation systems and public-goods funding alike — which is why those topics belong in one module rather than four. This is also the part of the field with the most interesting mechanism design and the least product-market fit, and both facts are worth carrying.

Concepts

  • Naming and the account layer
    • ENS as the working case: a name is an NFT with a resolver, renewable, tradeable, and used as a wallet identifier across the ecosystem. It works because the token does a job — the M03 and M17 test again
    • Attestations (EAS and similar): signed, structured claims by one party about another, stored on- or off-chain, composable into reputation. The primitive is sound; what is missing is issuers anyone trusts
  • Sybil resistance and proof of personhood
    • The problem: any mechanism that weights by person is only as good as its person-counting. Approaches — social graph analysis, biometric registration, government-ID attestation, stake-based costs, and web-of-trust — each trade privacy, accessibility and resistance differently, and none is uncontroversially good
    • Biometric approaches (iris registration in particular) buy strong uniqueness at a privacy and consent cost that has drawn regulatory action in several jurisdictions. State the trade rather than the position
    • Non-transferable tokens ("soulbound") as the proposed representation for credentials that should not be sellable. The design problem nobody has solved: revocation, and what happens when a key is lost
  • Quadratic funding, which is a real result
    • Match donations in proportion to the square of the sum of square roots of contributions, so many small donors outweigh one large one. Under stated assumptions it produces the optimal provision of a public good — this is a theorem, not a slogan
    • The assumptions are where it lives or dies: no Sybils and no collusion. Both are violated in practice, and the deployed rounds spend most of their operational effort on Sybil detection and collusion-resistant variants
    • Gitcoin Grants as the largest live experiment, with several years of published round data — including the disqualifications, which is the honest part
  • Retroactive funding as the alternative bet
    • "It is easier to agree on what was useful than on what will be useful": fund after the fact, judged by a badgeholder set. Optimism's retro rounds are the largest deployment. The open question is whether anticipated retro-funding actually changes behaviour ex ante, and the evidence is not yet in

Work through

  • A Flexible Design for Funding Public Goods — Buterin, Hitzig and Weyl's quadratic funding paper. Work the optimality argument and then locate exactly which assumption Sybils break.
  • ENS documentation — the registry, resolver and renewal model, as the case where the mechanism shipped and stuck.
  • Ethereum Attestation Service documentation — the attestation schema model, as the general-purpose reputation primitive.
  • DAOs are not corporations — for why decentralisation should be argued as fault tolerance, which reframes the identity problem usefully.
  • On the Centralization of Governance Power in Decentralized Autonomous Organizations — re-read for why one-person-one-vote is the missing alternative and what it would require.

Practice

Implement quadratic funding over a synthetic donation set: compute the matching allocation for a matching pool split across five projects. Then introduce a Sybil attacker who splits one large donation across 50 addresses, and measure how much matching they capture. Then implement pairwise-bounded matching (the standard collusion-resistant variant) and measure how much the attack is blunted. Done when you can state the attacker's return on investment before and after the mitigation.

Where people go wrong

Treating quadratic funding as robust because it is optimal. Its optimality is conditional on one-person-one-vote and no collusion, and both conditions fail on a public ledger where addresses are free and side payments are unobservable. That does not make it useless — the deployed collusion-resistant variants meaningfully improve on plain matching — but the honest description is "a good mechanism running on a broken identity layer", and every proposal to fix it is really a proposal about M20's first half.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the quadratic funding formula and the two assumptions it needs; and explain why every serious weighted-by-person mechanism in this field reduces to an unsolved identity problem.

M21

Regulation: the perimeter, as it currently stands

≈9 h

Regulation is now the binding constraint on what gets built, which was not true three years ago. It comes after M09 because stablecoins are the object regulators actually legislated, and after M19 because the question "who is liable for a protocol" turns on who controls it. This module states what is settled and what is not, and is deliberately careful about the difference.

Concepts

  • The securities question in the United States
    • The Howey test: an investment of money in a common enterprise with an expectation of profit derived from the efforts of others. Every element has been litigated; "efforts of others" is where a sufficiently decentralised network is argued to fall outside
    • The case law is genuinely mixed. In SEC v. Ripple (SDNY, 2023) institutional sales were held to be securities transactions while programmatic exchange sales were not — a distinction between the transaction and the asset that other courts have not uniformly adopted. SEC v. LBRY went the other way on similar facts
    • The practical upshot: the classification depends on how a token was sold and to whom, not solely on what it is. Anyone telling you the answer is simple is describing their preferred outcome
  • Stablecoins, where there is now a statute
    • The GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27, signed 18 July 2025) creates a federal framework for payment stablecoins: 1:1 reserves in cash and short-dated instruments, monthly reserve disclosure, a licensing path for bank and non-bank issuers, and explicit exclusion of compliant payment stablecoins from securities treatment. Agency rulemaking followed on a statutory deadline and has run behind it
    • The CLARITY Act, addressing market structure and the SEC/CFTC boundary more broadly, passed the House in July 2025 and remains pending in the Senate. Check its status before relying on anything about it
  • Europe, which legislated first
    • MiCA has applied in full since 30 December 2024: authorisation for crypto-asset service providers, reserve and redemption requirements for asset-referenced and e-money tokens, and issuance limits that have pushed non-compliant stablecoins off EU-regulated venues. The practical effect on which stablecoins European institutions can hold has been substantial
  • Sanctions, AML and the limits of code
    • OFAC sanctioned Tornado Cash's smart contract addresses in August 2022 — the first designation of software rather than persons. In Van Loon (Fifth Circuit, November 2024) the court held immutable smart contracts are not "property" of a foreign national under IEEPA and so could not be designated; OFAC delisted the addresses in March 2025. Developer liability is a separate question and remains contested
    • The travel rule applied to virtual asset service providers, and the unresolved question of what it means for a protocol with no operator
  • Liability without an entity
    • If a DAO has no legal wrapper, its members may be a general partnership with joint and several liability — a theory that has been advanced in litigation and is the practical reason legal wrappers proliferated (M18)
    • Front ends, relayers and interface providers as the enforcement surface: the contracts may be unstoppable, and the website, the RPC provider and the developer are not

Work through

  • GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27) — the reserve, disclosure and licensing provisions. Read the statute; the secondary coverage is unreliable about scope.
  • Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) — the ESMA overview and the asset-referenced token requirements.
  • 15.S12 Blockchain and Money — the regulatory lectures, from someone who subsequently chaired the SEC. Dated on specifics, valuable on how the framing was constructed.
  • 2026 Crypto Crime Report — for the illicit-finance data that drives most AML policy in this area.
  • DeFi risks and the decentralisation illusion — the BIS argument that every DeFi protocol has a controlling group and is therefore regulable, which is the analytical basis for much of the current approach.
  • FEDS Notes — the stablecoin and tokenisation notes, for what US regulators are analysing ahead of rulemaking.

Practice

Take one token and apply Howey element by element, in writing, arguing both sides for each element. Then take one DeFi protocol and identify every point at which a regulator could apply pressure without touching the contracts: the front end, the domain, the RPC provider, the fiat on-ramp, the identifiable developers, the foundation. Done when your Howey analysis is genuinely two-sided and your pressure-point list has at least five entries with named parties.

Where people go wrong

Believing "sufficiently decentralised" is a legal test. It is a phrase from a 2018 speech by an SEC official, not a statute or a holding, and no court has adopted it as a safe harbour. It describes a real intuition — that a network with no promoter fits Howey badly — and it is not a defence you can rely on. The mirror error is assuming everything is illegal: stablecoins now have an explicit federal framework, and the perimeter is being drawn rather than closed.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what the Ripple ruling distinguished between and why that distinction is unusual; and state what the GENIUS Act requires of a payment stablecoin issuer, from the statute rather than from memory of a headline.

M22

What actually replicated

≈8 h

This field generates more claims per unit of evidence than almost any other, and the evidence is unusually available — every trade, every position, every exploit is public. The final module is the one that asks what survives contact with data. It is placed last because it needs every mechanism above to be meaningful, and it is the module that determines whether you leave able to evaluate a claim or only to restate one.

Concepts

  • What the empirical literature actually finds
    • Retail traders lose on average, and lose more the more they trade — the same finding as in equities and foreign exchange, at larger magnitudes. Reported volume is materially inflated by wash trading on some venues. Concentrated-liquidity providers mostly underperform holding. None of these are contrarian claims; they are the mainstream findings
    • Where the positive findings are: stablecoins as a payment and settlement rail have real, measurable, growing usage that is not speculative. On-chain lending and AMMs function reliably as market infrastructure and have survived multiple stress events without a central bank. Settlement finality without an intermediary is a real capability that did not previously exist at this scale
  • Reading a claim in this field
    • Ask: what is the denominator, who published it, is the volume real, is the yield net of emissions and impermanent loss, is the "TVL" double-counted across composed protocols, and is the time period cherry-picked to a cycle. Applying these six questions eliminates most of what circulates
    • The specific failure to watch for is survivorship: the protocols and funds still around to be measured are not the population
  • The standing critiques, taken seriously
    • That the returns are largely transfers between participants rather than value creation; that "decentralisation" is a claim the ownership data contradicts (M08 and M16 of the sibling page, M19 here); that composability makes the system fragile in ways nobody is measuring; that the illicit-use share, while small proportionally, is large absolutely
    • Read the strongest sceptics rather than the weakest. Gerard, Molly White's incident record, and the BIS analysis each make arguments that require an answer, and a reader who cannot state them has not understood the field
  • The institutional turn, and what it actually changed
    • Spot ETFs, tokenised Treasury funds, regulated stablecoin issuers and bank custody are real and are changing who holds these assets and under what rules. Note what this does to the original thesis: the asset succeeded partly by being intermediated by exactly the institutions it was designed to route around
  • What is still genuinely open
    • Whether any DeFi protocol is a durable business once emissions stop (M15). Whether on-chain identity is solvable without a trusted issuer (M20). Whether governance can be made capture-resistant (M19). Whether stablecoin regulation makes the sector safer or simply consolidates it into a few licensed issuers. Whether the whole thing has a use beyond finance that has yet shown any traction
    • Hold these as open. A syllabus that resolved them would be lying, and the honest position after twenty-two modules is a well-informed uncertainty rather than a verdict

Practice

Write a two-page assessment answering one question: has DeFi produced anything that is better than the traditional alternative for a specific use case, or only different? Pick one concrete use case — cross-border payments, collateralised borrowing, market making, or fundraising — and argue it with numbers: cost, speed, availability, failure rate. Done when a reader who disagrees with your conclusion would say you stated their side fairly and used the right data.

Where people go wrong

Picking a side and then reading. Both the maximalist and the dismissive positions are available in fully-formed, internally consistent versions, and both require ignoring specific evidence — the maximalists ignore the return distributions and the concentration data, the dismissers ignore stablecoin settlement volumes and the fact that the infrastructure has run for years without central-bank support. The useful position is per-claim rather than per-field, and it is harder to hold because it does not travel well on social media.

Checkpoint

Capstone

Build the machine, then audit the market. Two projects. The first proves you understand the mechanisms; the second proves you can evaluate them, which is the harder and rarer skill.

  • Project 1 — a composable DeFi stack from scratch: a constant-product AMM with fees and a TWAP oracle; an over-collateralised lending market that reads that oracle, with a health factor and a liquidation auction; an ERC-4626 vault that deposits into the lending market; and a flash-loan facility. Then attack your own system: manipulate the TWAP with a flash loan to borrow more than your collateral supports, and drain the vault.
  • Draws on: M02, M06, M08, M10, M13.
  • Done when: the exploit works, and then a second version — with a manipulation-resistant oracle, a borrow cap and an invariant test — defeats it. Show both runs.
  • Stretch: add a perpetual market with a funding rate anchored to your oracle, and a governance module with a timelock. Then execute a Beanstalk-style flash-loaned governance attack against it, and fix that too.

Project 2 — an honest protocol report. Pick one live protocol with over $100m deposited and write the report its users should have. Include: the full mechanism description; where every yield comes from, decomposed into the five sources from M14; the oracle dependencies with manipulation costs computed from live pool depths; the governance map with holder concentration, quorum, timelock and the emergency multisig's signers; the treasury's size and denomination; annual fees against annual emissions; and the historical exploit base rate for its category. Conclude with the cheapest way to steal from it and the most likely way it dies.

  • Draws on: M03, M07, M08, M10, M13, M14, M15, M18, M19.
  • Done when: every number cites a contract address, a transaction or a dataset — and someone who works on that protocol would concede the analysis is fair even where it is unflattering.

  • I can explain loss-versus-rebalancing, say why it is a better benchmark than impermanent loss, and name who receives the money.

  • I can compute the liquidation price of a lending position and explain how a protocol accrues bad debt while over-collateralised.

  • I can explain why USDC depegged in March 2023 despite being fully backed.
  • I can compute the cost of manipulating a TWAP over a given window and compare it to the profit downstream.
  • I can explain how a perpetual's funding rate anchors it to spot and when that anchoring fails.
  • I can identify a sandwich attack in a block and say who paid for it.
  • I can reconstruct a flash-loan exploit and state the invariant it violated.
  • I can name the payer for any advertised yield, and say whether it survives when emissions stop.

Where to go next

  • Blockchain Systems — the layer underneath. Cryptographic primitives, Nakamoto and Byzantine consensus, the EVM, contract security, rollups, data availability and zero-knowledge proofs. Its M16 and this page's M12 are two halves of the MEV subject and are written to be read together; if you found yourself wanting to know why a block contains what it contains, that is the page.
  • Information Theory — further from this material than it looks. The wash-trading and manipulation-detection work in M17 and M22 is statistical inference on a noisy channel, and the entropy and divergence machinery is what makes those tests principled rather than heuristic.
  • Market microstructure, properly — M04 gave you the working subset. The full field — inventory models, information-based models, optimal execution, the empirics of price formation — is the direct upstream of M06, M07 and M12, and it already contains most of the results this field has been rediscovering.
  • Mechanism design and auction theory — token models, order-flow auctions, liquidation auctions and quadratic funding are all mechanism design, and the field has a century of results on incentive compatibility, revenue equivalence and collusion. The gap between what token designers propose and what the theory already settled is large and mostly unexplored.
  • Financial regulation and securities law — M21 is a survey by a non-lawyer, deliberately. The underlying doctrine on what makes something a security, how custody rules work, and why segregation of client assets exists is a genuine discipline, and reading it makes most crypto regulatory commentary look thin.
  • Corporate governance and collective action — M19's findings were predicted decades ago by literature on voluntary organisations, shareholder apathy and oligarchy. Olson, Hirschman and the corporate-governance literature explain the DAO results better than anything written inside this field.
