Economics & finance

DeFi, NFTs and On-Chain Economics

Once a blockchain can run arbitrary code, people build markets on it — and those markets have to work with no broker, no clearing house, no credit officer and no ability to reverse a mistake. This syllabus treats the result as mechanism design under adversarial conditions: why a constant-product curve prices anything at all, why liquidations fail exactly when they are needed, why every algorithmic stablecoin has died the same death, and what the data says about which of it has ever worked.

What this topic is

Decentralised finance is what happens when financial contracts are written as programs that anyone can call and nobody can amend. That constraint removes the intermediary and, with it, every mechanism finance normally uses to handle the hard cases: no credit assessment, so loans must be over-collateralised; no settlement window, so liquidation must be instant and incentivised; no market maker with an inventory desk, so pricing must come from a formula; no reversal, so a bug is a transfer. The interesting content of the field is how each of those substitutions behaves under stress — and the record of stress events is long enough now to study properly.

The same constraint produced the rest of the ecosystem. NFTs are the minimal case of a non-fungible ledger entry, and most of what people believed they bought turned out to live off chain. DAOs are the attempt to govern a protocol with the same tools, and they inherited plutocracy along with transparency. The regulatory layer arrived late and now moves fast. This course sits on top of the protocol machinery — how the chain itself works belongs to Blockchain Systems — and treats the application layer as economics: incentives, market microstructure, and the gap between what a mechanism was designed to do and what it does when someone is paid to break it.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes you are comfortable with basic finance (present value, leverage, an order book, what a market maker does), can read a little algebra without flinching, and can write enough SQL or Python to pull and plot a dataset. You should be able to read Solidity, though you will not write much of it here.

  • No blockchain background — do Blockchain Systems M01–M03 and M07–M08 first. You need the account model, gas and the contract-execution model, and this page will not re-teach them.
  • No market-microstructure background — read DeFi and the Future of Finance chapters 1–3 before M03; it introduces the vocabulary without assuming a finance degree.
  • No SQL — work through Dune documentation's query tutorial. Every practice exercise from M03 onward assumes you can write a query against on-chain tables.

Not covered here: trading strategy, portfolio construction, price prediction, tax advice for your jurisdiction, or how to launch a token. The cryptographic and consensus machinery underneath — signatures, consensus, rollups, zero-knowledge proofs — is the sibling course. This page is also not a defence of the field: several modules end by concluding that a mechanism does not work, because that is what the evidence supports.

How to use this

  • Working knowledge (≈45 h): M01, M03, M04, M05, M07, M12. That covers the mechanisms holding most of the value and the legal frame around them. Skip M11 entirely and skim M02.
  • Full course (≈118 h): every module in order. M03 is the spine — M04, M06, M07 and M08 all assume you can reason about a bonding curve and about who loses money to whom.
  • Coming from traditional finance: start at M03. Your intuitions about market making, collateral and liquidation are mostly right and mostly mis-calibrated; M03 and M04 are where you find out which. DeFi and the Future of Finance is the best translation layer.
  • Coming from smart-contract engineering: start at M03 and go straight through to M08. You know how the code works; this course is about why the code loses money even when it does exactly what it says.
  • Sceptical and want the evidence first: M01, M05, M08, M13, in that order. That is the empirical arc — what these assets are, the largest failure, the failure taxonomy, and what has actually survived.

What you'll be able to do

  • Derive the constant-product pricing formula and compute exact output, price impact and fee for a given trade.
  • Show why impermanent loss understates an LP's real cost, and state loss-versus-rebalancing precisely enough to compute it.
  • Model a lending market's interest-rate curve and find the utilisation at which a position becomes unliquidatable in practice.
  • Trace the Terra/UST collapse step by step through the mint-and-burn arbitrage, and say at which point the mechanism became reflexive.
  • Design an oracle for a new market and defend your choice of source, aggregation and update rule against a manipulation attack you specify.
  • Decompose an MEV supply chain: who searched, who built, who relayed, who proposed, and where the value ended up.
  • Read an NFT contract and state exactly what a buyer owns, where the artwork lives, and what happens when that host stops paying its bills.
  • Evaluate a DAO's governance for capture: token concentration, quorum, timelock, delegate turnout and who actually holds the executing keys.
  • Classify a protocol failure as an implementation bug, an economic design flaw, an oracle failure or a key compromise, from the post-mortem.
  • Say what the GENIUS Act, MiCA and the pending US market-structure bill each do, and which of them binds a given product.
  • Build a Dune query answering a real question about a live protocol, and explain why the obvious version of it is wrong.

Syllabus

M01

What a crypto asset actually is

≈8 h

Every later module prices something. Before you can reason about an AMM's LP returns or a stablecoin's peg you need a defensible account of what the underlying assets are, how they trade, and why the standard valuation frameworks give answers nobody uses. This module is deliberately the least crypto-native in the course: the tools are ordinary asset pricing and market structure.

Concepts

  • Three incompatible framings, all in active legal and economic use — money, commodity, security
    • The Howey test asks about an investment of money in a common enterprise with profits expected from others' efforts. Whether a token passes depends on facts that change over a project's life, which is why "sufficiently decentralised" became a live argument rather than a category
    • A token can be a security when sold and a commodity when traded, and the US regulatory fight in M12 is largely about who supervises which
  • Supply mechanics as monetary policy written in code — Bitcoin's halving schedule; Ethereum's issuance net of the EIP-1559 burn
    • Why stock-to-flow models fail as forecasts: they regress price on a deterministic supply path and therefore encode the conclusion in the independent variable
  • Valuation attempts and why they underdeliver
    • The equation-of-exchange approach (MV = PQ) requires a velocity term nobody can measure and which reacts to price
    • Discounted fee flows work where a protocol has real fee revenue and an enforceable claim on it — which is rare, and the difference between "protocol revenue" and "value accruing to the token" is where most token theses quietly break
    • The honest position: a large fraction of these assets have no cash-flow claim, and their price is a function of adoption expectations and reflexivity. Say so rather than reverse-engineering a model
  • Market structure — where trading actually happens
    • Centralised exchanges still dominate spot and derivative volume; perpetual futures with a funding rate are the field's dominant derivative, and the funding rate is the mechanism that pins the perp to spot
    • The basis trade and its role in ETF-era flows; why a persistent positive basis is a financing rate, not free money
    • Spot ETFs moved a large share of price discovery into regulated venues and changed who the marginal holder is
  • What the empirical literature finds — concentration of holdings, the disconnect between on-chain activity and price, and the repeated finding that retail flows arrive after price moves rather than before

Work through

  • Cryptocurrencies and Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — Makarov and Schoar's survey. The clearest academic account of market structure, concentration and where the returns went.
  • MIT 15.S12 Blockchain and Money — lectures 1–5, for the money-and-payments framing from someone who later ran the SEC.
  • Digital Gold — for how the asset acquired its first users and its first prices; skip if you already know the history.
  • The Crypto Story — Levine's long essay is the single best explanation of what these instruments are for a reader from finance.
  • DefiLlama — pull the current split of value across chains and categories before you read anyone's narrative about it.
  • Dune documentation — the getting-started tutorial; you need it working before M03.

Practice

Pick one token with real protocol fee revenue and build a two-page valuation: annualised fees, the share that reaches token holders and by what mechanism, the supply schedule including unvested allocations, and a discount rate you can defend. Then write the paragraph explaining why your number is probably wrong. Done when you can name the single assumption that moves your answer most.

Where people go wrong

Treating "total value locked" as a size or a revenue measure. TVL counts deposited collateral at current prices, double-counts assets that are deposited, wrapped and re-deposited, and rises purely because prices rose. A protocol can double its TVL while its fee revenue falls. Use fees, active addresses and volume when you want to know whether anything is happening; use TVL only when you want to know how much collateral is sitting somewhere.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain to a fixed-income investor what claim, if any, a governance token gives its holder — and identify a protocol where the answer is "none, despite substantial revenue" and say where the revenue goes instead.

M02

Tokenomics as mechanism design

≈8 h

A token is a set of incentives with a price attached. Most protocol failures that are not code failures are incentive failures: a mechanism that rewards a behaviour nobody wanted, or that funds growth out of dilution and calls it yield. M01 gave you the asset; this gives you the machinery protocols use to distribute it.

Concepts

  • Supply schedules and unlocks — cliffs, linear vesting, and the observable price behaviour around large unlock dates
    • Circulating supply versus fully diluted valuation, and why a low float with a high FDV is a structurally different bet from what the market capitalisation suggests
  • Emissions versus real yield — the distinction the field took three years to learn
    • Liquidity mining pays depositors in newly issued tokens. If the token's only demand is from people selling it, the "yield" is dilution routed through a farm
    • Mercenary capital: 2020–21 measurements repeatedly showed liquidity leaving within days of emissions ending. Retention, not peak TVL, is the metric that matters
    • Real yield — fee revenue distributed to stakers — is rarer and much smaller, and separating the two is the first analytical move on any new protocol
  • Vote-escrow designs — lock the token for up to four years, receive boosted rewards and voting weight
    • Curve's veCRV is the canonical implementation, and it worked as intended: it made governance weight a purchasable asset, which is exactly what created the bribe market in M10
    • Lock-ups reduce sell pressure and concentrate governance; the tradeoff is not avoidable, only chosen
  • Airdrops, points and Sybil resistance
    • Retroactive airdrops rewarded genuine early users and were immediately gamed by farms running thousands of addresses; the arms race produced points programs, whose discretion is precisely the thing token distribution was supposed to eliminate
    • Sybil filtering by heuristics is adversarial classification with the adversary reading your criteria; there is no known clean solution, which is why M11 exists
  • Principal-agent structure — who is paying, who is deciding, and who is exposed. Insiders with cheap cost basis, mercenary depositors and long-horizon users have genuinely opposed interests, and a token design chooses among them whether or not it admits it

Work through

  • DeFi and the Future of Finance — chapters 4 and 5 on protocol tokens and incentives.
  • Token Terminal — compare revenue, expenses (token incentives) and earnings for five protocols. The gap between revenue and earnings is this module in one number.
  • a16z Crypto Startup School — the token-design sessions, read critically: this is the builder's-eye view and worth knowing precisely because it is the one being argued.
  • Web3 is Going Just Great — filter for token launches and follow three from launch to a year later.

Practice

Take a protocol with an active incentive program and compute, from on-chain data, the token value emitted per dollar of fee revenue generated over one quarter. Then chart depositor retention in the four weeks after an emissions change. Done when you can state the customer-acquisition cost in dollars per retained dollar of deposits and say whether it ever pays back.

Where people go wrong

Quoting an APY without asking what it is paid in. A 40% yield paid in a token whose supply is inflating 60% a year is a negative real return, and the quoted figure often already assumes you compound by re-buying the same token. Convert every yield to the asset you actually want to hold before comparing anything, and check whether the denominator includes the impermanent loss the position is accumulating.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: read a new protocol's token distribution and vesting table and predict, with dates, the two periods when insider supply hits the market — and explain what that implies about whose interests the emissions schedule was designed around.

M03

Automated market makers and on-chain microstructure

≈14 h

This is the spine of the course. The AMM is the field's one genuinely new financial primitive — a market maker with no inventory model, no quotes and no ability to cancel — and understanding exactly who loses money to whom is the prerequisite for lending, oracles, MEV and most of the exploit record.

Concepts

  • The constant-product invariant, derived rather than quoted — x · y = k
    • Compute the exact output for an input including the fee, and show that marginal price is y/x while realised price is worse; the difference is price impact, and it is quadratic in size relative to depth
    • Why the curve never runs out of either asset, and why that means it always quotes a price — including a badly wrong one
    • Constant-function market makers as a family: the invariant determines the price curve, and the price curve determines the liquidity profile
  • Concentrated liquidity — Uniswap v3's move from a global curve to per-range positions
    • Ticks, the √P parameterisation, and why a position becomes entirely one asset once price leaves its range
    • Capital efficiency and active management are the same thing: a tight range earns more fees per dollar and needs rebalancing, which is a cost the advertised APY never includes
    • Hooks (v4) let a pool run custom logic at swap and liquidity events — the design space reopened, along with a new class of trusted-code risk in something that used to be a fixed formula
  • Specialised invariants — Curve's StableSwap blends constant-sum and constant-product so that assets expected to trade near parity get very deep liquidity near the peg and graceful degradation away from it. This is the mechanism that makes stablecoin swaps cheap and that makes a depeg expensive for LPs
  • What an LP actually earns, done properly
    • Impermanent loss is the divergence between holding the pool and holding the assets — a real cost, but it measures the wrong thing: it is path-independent and ignores that the arbitrageur trades against you continuously
    • Loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) is the correct benchmark: compare the LP against a rebalancing strategy that trades at the external market price. It isolates exactly the money lost to arbitrageurs on stale quotes, it grows with volatility and with block time, and it is what an LP is actually paid fees to bear
    • The consequence: passive LPs in volatile pairs have frequently earned less than holding, and the fee tier has to exceed LVR for the position to make sense
  • Order flow and its toxicity — informed flow (arbitrage) versus uninformed flow (retail swaps), and why every venue design in this field is an attempt to attract the second while repelling the first
  • The alternatives that grew up around AMMs — intent and RFQ systems where solvers compete to fill a signed order off chain, and fully on-chain order books on high-throughput venues. Both exist because the AMM's stale-quote problem is structural, not an implementation detail

Work through

  • Uniswap v2 Core — short, and the reference for the invariant, the fee mechanics and the built-in TWAP oracle you will meet again in M06.
  • Uniswap v3 Core — sections on ticks and the √P representation. Work the arithmetic; it is the part people skip and then get wrong.
  • StableSwap: Efficient Mechanism for Stablecoin Liquidity — the amplification parameter and what it does to the curve's shape.
  • Automated Market Making and Loss-Versus-Rebalancing — the central paper of this module. Read it slowly.
  • Improved Price Oracles: Constant Function Market Makers — the general theory of CFMMs, and the bridge to M06.
  • Uniswap documentation — the concepts section and the v4 hooks overview.
  • Dune — fork an existing pool-analytics query and modify it until you understand every join.
  • Dune documentation — the DEX tables reference, for what is already decoded for you.

Practice

Implement a constant-product pool in a spreadsheet or a script: given reserves and a fee, compute output, effective price and price impact for a range of trade sizes. Then take one real v2 pool, pull every swap and liquidity event for a 30-day window, and compute the LP's realised return against two benchmarks — holding the assets, and a rebalancing portfolio. Done when you can say whether fees covered LVR for that pool over that window, with the number.

Where people go wrong

Believing impermanent loss "isn't real until you withdraw". It is marked continuously and it is realised by every arbitrage trade against your stale quote; the name is one of the worst in finance. Worse, the standard IL formula understates the problem, because it compares two end states and ignores the path — LVR exists precisely because the loss accrues trade by trade whether or not price returns to where it started.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a pool with higher volatility needs a higher fee tier to break even, derive roughly how LVR scales with block time, and say what that implies for AMMs on a chain with sub-second blocks.

M04

Lending, leverage and liquidation

≈10 h

Lending is the second-largest use of these systems and the one that most obviously lacks its traditional machinery: no credit assessment, no recourse, no workout process. Everything is replaced by collateral and an auction, and the auction is where it breaks. M03 is required because liquidations execute against AMMs.

Concepts

  • Over-collateralisation as a substitute for credit — loan-to-value ratios, liquidation thresholds, health factors, and why the gap between the borrowing limit and the liquidation point is the protocol's only safety buffer
  • Interest rates set by a curve, not a market — utilisation-based models with a kink
    • Below the optimal utilisation the rate rises gently; above it, steeply, to force repayment and protect withdrawals. The kink is a policy parameter that a governance vote sets, so "the market rate" is really a governance decision
    • Rate spikes as a liquidity signal, and the reflexive loop where a spike triggers repayment which triggers liquidation
  • Liquidation mechanics and their failure modes
    • Fixed-bonus liquidation versus Dutch auction: the bonus pays the liquidator's gas and price risk, and if it is too small nobody bids, while too large it needlessly punishes borrowers
    • MakerDAO's Black Thursday, 12 March 2020: network congestion pushed gas costs up and kept competing bidders out, so a keeper won collateral auctions with zero-DAI bids. Roughly 5.4 million DAI of bad debt followed, covered by auctioning MKR. The lesson is that a liquidation system depends on a competitive keeper market and on block space being available exactly when everyone needs it
    • Bad debt as the terminal failure: when collateral value falls below debt faster than liquidation can execute, the shortfall lands on depositors or on the protocol's backstop
  • Thin collateral and governance risk — the recurring pattern where a protocol accepts a low-liquidity token as collateral, someone borrows against a manipulated price, and the liquidation cannot execute because the market to sell into does not exist. Listing decisions are risk decisions taken by voters
  • Isolated versus cross margin — isolation pools, borrow caps and supply caps as the response to contagion between markets; the cost is capital efficiency
  • Undercollateralised lending — why every attempt so far has ended in default or in re-introducing off-chain identity and legal recourse. Pseudonymity and unsecured credit are close to incompatible, and this is the field's most durable negative result

Work through

  • DeFi Protocols for Loanable Funds — the empirical study of rate setting and market efficiency across the major lending markets.
  • The Compound Protocol whitepaper — the original utilisation-curve design, short and clear.
  • Aave documentation — health factor, liquidation bonus, e-mode and caps. Read the risk parameters page and note that every number there is a governance variable.
  • Decentralized Finance (DeFi) specialization — the deep-dive course covering MakerDAO, Compound and Aave side by side.

Practice

Build a liquidation simulator for one real market: take the current collateral, debt, liquidation threshold and bonus, then apply a price shock path and compute at each step whether a rational liquidator profits after gas and slippage against the actual AMM depth. Find the shock size at which liquidation stops being profitable. Done when you can state the price drop, in percent and in minutes, that produces bad debt for that market.

Where people go wrong

Assuming a healthy collateral ratio means the position is safe. Solvency on paper is not the same as liquidatability: what matters is whether a liquidator can sell the collateral, in size, at the moment of stress, while paying the gas price that stress produces. A 200% collateralised position in an illiquid token is more dangerous than a 130% position in a deep one, and every large bad-debt event in this field has been a liquidity failure rather than a solvency miscalculation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why raising the liquidation bonus makes a market safer for depositors and worse for borrowers, and name the market condition under which raising it further stops helping at all.

M05

Stablecoins: the three designs and their failure modes

≈10 h

Stablecoins are the field's clearest product-market fit and the site of its largest single loss. The three designs fail in three completely different ways, and conflating them is the most common analytical error. This needs M04 because the crypto-collateralised design is a lending market wearing a different hat.

Concepts

  • Fiat-backed — a claim on a company holding reserves
    • The reserve composition is the entire risk: cash and short T-bills behave differently from commercial paper, and an attestation by an accounting firm is not an audit. Know which one you are reading
    • USDC's March 2023 depeg to roughly $0.87 came from $3.3 billion of reserves at Silicon Valley Bank — the failure was in the traditional banking layer, transmitted instantly to the token, and it recovered when the deposit guarantee was announced
    • The redemption gate is the real peg mechanism: the price holds because a large holder can redeem at par, so access to redemption — who gets it, at what size, on what days — is what to check
  • Crypto-collateralised — over-collateralised debt positions, as in M04
    • The CDP mechanism, stability fees, the savings rate, and liquidation as the peg's enforcement
    • The peg-stability module lets the system swap one-for-one against a fiat-backed stablecoin. It works, and it means the "decentralised" stablecoin is substantially backed by a centralised one — a tradeoff the system took deliberately and states openly
  • Algorithmic — backed by the protocol's own token, and the design that has never survived
    • Terra/UST, May 2022: one UST was always redeemable for $1 of newly minted LUNA and vice versa. In calm markets the arbitrage held the peg; under redemption pressure it minted LUNA into a falling market, and the reflexive loop destroyed both. The Anchor protocol's roughly 20% yield had drawn in deposits whose only reason to stay was that yield
    • The general argument: the collateral's value is correlated with demand for the stablecoin, so exactly when you need backing most, you have least. Every variant re-derives this
    • Distinguish from over-collateralised designs with algorithmic parameters, which are a different animal and do not share the failure mode
  • Yield-bearing stablecoins and tokenised treasuries — passing T-bill yield through to the holder, and why the GENIUS Act specifically prohibits payment-stablecoin issuers from paying interest, pushing yield into adjacent wrappers
  • The current legal frame, as of mid-2026
    • The GENIUS Act became law on 18 July 2025: full reserve backing in cash and short-term treasuries, monthly reserve disclosure, a federal and state licensing path, and no issuer-paid yield. Implementing rules were due from the agencies by 18 July 2026, taking effect within the following six months
    • MiCA has applied across the EU since 30 December 2024, with its own reserve, redemption and e-money-token rules
    • The consequence: stablecoins are now the most regulated part of this field, and the compliance perimeter is a product feature

Work through

  • Anatomy of a Run: The Terra Luna Crash — the run dynamics with the data, including who exited first and who did not.
  • Sky (MakerDAO) documentation — the CDP, stability fee, savings rate and peg-stability module. Then look up the actual collateral composition.
  • GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27) — the definition of a payment stablecoin, the reserve requirements and the yield prohibition. Read the definitions section at minimum.
  • Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA) — Titles III and IV, on asset-referenced and e-money tokens.
  • DefiLlama — the stablecoins dashboard: chart the supply of each design type over time and watch the algorithmic category vanish.

Practice

Reconstruct the UST depeg hour by hour from on-chain data: the Curve pool imbalance, the redemption volume through the mint-burn mechanism, the LUNA supply expansion, and the price of both assets. Then write the counterfactual — what parameter change, applied when, would have arrested it, and why it was not available. Done when your timeline identifies the point after which no parameter change would have worked.

Where people go wrong

Calling anything with a stable price "a stablecoin" and reasoning about all of them together. A fiat-backed token's risk is bank and issuer risk; a CDP stablecoin's risk is collateral liquidity and liquidation; an algorithmic stablecoin's risk is reflexive collapse. They correlate almost not at all, and the analysis that told you Terra was fine because Tether had survived was a category error, not a bad forecast.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the precise mechanism by which each design breaks its peg, and explain why a fully reserved, audited, redeemable stablecoin can still depeg on a secondary market while its redemption channel is working perfectly.

M06

Oracles and the off-chain data problem

≈8 h

A contract cannot see outside its own chain, so every price, rate and real-world fact arrives through an oracle — and the oracle is therefore the trusted component in an otherwise trust-minimised system. A large fraction of the losses in M08 are oracle failures wearing other names. M03 and M04 are needed because they are what the oracle is feeding.

Concepts

  • The oracle problem stated properly — not "how do we get data on chain" but "how do we get data on chain such that corrupting it costs more than what it secures"
  • Push versus pull architectures
    • Push: a network of node operators aggregates sources off chain and writes an update when a deviation threshold or heartbeat triggers. The consumer reads a stored value, which is cheap and possibly stale
    • Pull: signed prices are published continuously off chain and the consumer submits the latest one with its transaction. Fresher and shifts the update cost to whoever needs it; the freshness itself becomes something to race for
    • Aggregation and the deviation threshold are the design's real parameters — a 0.5% threshold means the on-chain price is legitimately allowed to be 0.5% wrong
  • On-chain price sources and their manipulability
    • Spot price from an AMM reserve ratio is manipulable within a single transaction — with a flash loan, at zero capital cost. Never use it as a price feed; this is the single most repeated mistake in the exploit record
    • Time-weighted average prices raise the cost of manipulation to holding the price away from market for the whole window, which is expensive but not impossible in a thin pool — and the TWAP is stale by construction, which is a cost during real volatility
    • Manipulation cost scales with pool depth, so an oracle's security is a property of the market, not the contract
  • Mango Markets, October 2022, as the case worth studying — a trader took a large perpetual position in a thinly traded token, pushed its price up across the venues the oracle read, and borrowed roughly $110 million against the inflated collateral value. Everything the contracts did was as specified. The criminal case that followed produced convictions in April 2024 that were then vacated in May 2025 on venue grounds and insufficient evidence of misrepresentation — which leaves the legal status of "profitable use of a protocol as designed" genuinely unsettled
  • The latency tradeoff — a faster oracle reduces the window for stale-price arbitrage but increases the value of being first to update, which is an MEV problem (M07). There is no setting that eliminates both
  • Beyond prices — proof of reserves attests to assets at a moment and says nothing about liabilities; oracles for interest rates, sports results and real-world events each have their own dispute-resolution problem, usually solved by a committee with a stake

Work through

  • Improved Price Oracles: Constant Function Market Makers — the formal analysis of when a CFMM's price is a usable oracle and when it is not.
  • Chainlink documentation — the data-feeds architecture, deviation thresholds and heartbeats. Look up the actual parameters for a feed a protocol you know depends on.
  • rekt.news — read every entry tagged as an oracle manipulation and tabulate the price source each victim used.

Practice

Pick a mid-cap token with an on-chain market and compute the cost of moving its price 30% for the duration of a 30-minute TWAP window: capital required, expected slippage in and out, and arbitrageur pushback. Compare that cost against the amount borrowable at that price from a real lending market that lists it. Done when you can say whether the manipulation is profitable, with both numbers.

Where people go wrong

Treating a decentralised oracle network as if decentralisation solved manipulation. Distributing the reporters protects against a corrupt reporter; it does nothing when every honest reporter faithfully reports a manipulated market price. If the underlying market is thin, an honest, decentralised, correctly functioning oracle will report the attacker's price — and the protocol will act on it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: specify an oracle for a newly listed asset — sources, aggregation, update rule, circuit breaker — and then describe the specific attack your design still permits and what it would cost to execute.

M07

MEV as a market

≈10 h

Whoever orders transactions can extract value from them, and on a public chain that right is auctioned. MEV is simultaneously a tax on users, the mechanism keeping AMM prices correct, and a centralising force on block production. M03 is required: almost all MEV is an AMM interaction.

Concepts

  • The taxonomy, by who pays
    • Arbitrage — between two AMMs, or between a centralised exchange and an AMM. The LP pays, and this is the same money LVR measures in M03. CEX-DEX arbitrage is the largest category and it is invisible to the user
    • Sandwiching — buy ahead of a user's swap, sell after. The user pays directly, and this is the category that is unambiguously extractive
    • Liquidations — a competitive race to a liquidation bonus. Necessary work, competed away in fees
    • JIT liquidity — add concentrated liquidity immediately before a large swap and remove it immediately after, capturing the fee without bearing the inventory risk. Better for the swapper's execution, worse for the passive LP
  • The supply chain — searcher, builder, relay, proposer
    • Searchers find opportunities and bid for inclusion; builders assemble full blocks and bid for the right to have one proposed; relays sit between builder and proposer so the proposer can commit to a block without seeing its contents; the proposer signs the highest bid
    • Proposer–builder separation exists because block building became a specialised, capital-intensive activity, and a validator on a home connection cannot compete. The plumbing is in the sibling course; the economics is that most of the extracted value flows to proposers as bids
  • Private order flow and why it centralises
    • A builder with exclusive access to valuable order flow can outbid builders without it, so market share concentrates on order-flow deals rather than on building skill
    • The same mechanism protects users — a transaction sent privately cannot be sandwiched — so the user-protective and the centralising effects are the same effect
  • Timing games — proposers delaying block publication to collect more bids, trading a small reorg risk against a larger payment, and why this pressures the protocol's slot timing
  • Mitigations and what each actually buys — private mempools (protection without decentralisation), order-flow auctions returning some value to users, encrypted mempools (real, expensive, and still vulnerable to the last-look problem), and application-level designs like batch auctions that remove the ordering advantage instead of policing it
  • Reading the data — realised MEV is measurable, and the honest summary is that the extractive share is smaller than commonly claimed while the arbitrage share paid by LPs is much larger

Work through

  • Flash Boys 2.0 — the paper that named the problem and connected transaction ordering to consensus instability.
  • Quantifying Blockchain Extractable Value — the empirical follow-up; take the category breakdown seriously.
  • Flashbots writings — the research posts on order flow auctions and builder centralisation, from the team that built the market.
  • relayscan.io — live relay and builder market share. Check the concentration before and after reading the theory.
  • Paradigm research and writing — the MEV and intents pieces for the current design frontier.
  • Flashbots Collective — read one live thread on order flow auctions or builder policy; the objections in the replies are where the tradeoffs get stated honestly.

Practice

Find a sandwich attack on chain: identify the three transactions, compute the attacker's profit net of gas and the victim's loss versus the price they would have received alone. Then find a CEX-DEX arbitrage in the same block and compute who funded it. Done when you can point at the specific pool state before and after each and reconcile the numbers.

Where people go wrong

Treating all MEV as theft to be eliminated. Arbitrage MEV is what keeps on-chain prices aligned with the wider market, and liquidation MEV is what keeps lending markets solvent — remove both and the system stops working. The extractive category worth targeting is sandwiching and other user-facing front-running, and the mechanisms that suppress it (private flow, batch auctions) have their own centralisation costs. "Eliminate MEV" is not a coherent goal; "change who captures it, and stop the user paying twice" is.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a user who routes through a private relay is safer but the network is more centralised — and identify which participant loses income when that user switches.

M08

Composability, systemic risk and the exploit record

≈10 h

Protocols call each other, so a correct contract can be destroyed by a correct contract it depends on. This module builds the failure taxonomy that lets you read any new incident quickly, and it needs M03 through M07 because every category below is one of those mechanisms breaking under composition.

Concepts

  • Flash loans as a primitive — borrow any amount with no collateral, provided you repay within the same transaction
    • Legitimately, they collapse the capital requirement for arbitrage and refinancing, which improves market efficiency
    • Adversarially, they remove capital as a barrier to any attack whose profit is realised in one transaction: manipulation, governance capture, and complex multi-protocol sequences. They did not create new vulnerabilities; they made every existing economic vulnerability free to exploit
  • The failure taxonomy, which is the module's actual deliverable
    • Implementation bugs — reentrancy, access control, arithmetic. The Curve incident of July 2023 is the sharpest example: the vulnerability was in a specific Vyper compiler version's reentrancy guard, so contracts that were themselves correct were exploitable
    • Economic design flaws — the code does what it says and the mechanism is wrong. Mango (M06) and every algorithmic stablecoin (M05) sit here
    • Oracle failures — covered in M06; usually classified as economic, usually reported as a hack
    • Key and governance compromise — the largest losses overall. Ronin was five of nine validator keys; many "DeFi hacks" are a compromised deployer key
    • Read-only reentrancy — a view function returns inconsistent state during a callback and a downstream protocol prices against it. The victim's code has no reentrant write path at all, which is why guards did not help
  • Dependency graphs and correlated failure — a stablecoin that is collateral in a lending market that backs an LP position that is collateral elsewhere. The same dollar supports several positions, and one depeg propagates through all of them within a block
  • What happens after — the negotiated outcome is now standard: a public bounty offer, an on-chain message, sometimes a return. Euler's March 2023 incident lost roughly $197 million and ended with the funds returned after negotiation. "Code is law" survives as a slogan and not as a practice, and the reason is that the alternative is worse for everyone involved
  • The defensive layer and its limits — bug bounty markets, cover protocols that are themselves capital-constrained, monitoring and pause guardians, and the recurring finding that audited contracts are exploited too, because the audit's scope excluded the economics

Work through

  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — the systematisation of protocol types and attack classes; the best single map of the space.
  • rekt.news — take the fifteen largest entries and classify each by the taxonomy above. Keep the table; it is the deliverable.
  • Immunefi — read live bounty scopes to see what protocols themselves believe their risks are, and note what is excluded.
  • Damn Vulnerable DeFi — the flash-loan and oracle-manipulation challenges specifically. Solve them before reading solutions.

Practice

Pick a live protocol and draw its dependency graph two levels deep: every external contract it calls, every price it reads, every token it assumes is well-behaved. Then pick one node and write the failure propagation — what breaks, in what order, and how much is at risk. Done when your graph includes at least one dependency the protocol's own documentation does not mention.

Where people go wrong

Reading "audited by" as a risk assessment. An audit covers a commit, a scope and a time budget; it usually excludes economic design, oracle assumptions, governance and the behaviour of integrated protocols — which is where most of the money has gone. The useful questions are what the scope excluded, whether the deployed bytecode matches the audited commit, and what changed afterwards. A protocol with three audits and an unaudited upgrade is unaudited.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: read a fresh post-mortem and classify the failure within a paragraph — and say which of the other four categories the same protocol is still exposed to.

M09

NFTs and what digital ownership actually means

≈8 h

NFTs are the simplest thing in this course — a ledger entry with a unique id — and generated the widest gap between what buyers believed and what they held. That gap is the lesson: it is a case study in what a blockchain can and cannot guarantee. M01 is needed for the market framing.

Concepts

  • The standards, exactly — ERC-721 is one owner per token id; ERC-1155 supports fungible and non-fungible ids in one contract with batch transfers, which is why games use it
    • tokenURI returns a string. The standard says nothing about what is at the other end, and that single fact drives most of what follows
  • Where the thing actually lives
    • Metadata and media are usually off chain: an HTTPS URL to a company's server, or an IPFS content hash that is only retrievable while somebody pins it. Content addressing proves the file has not changed; it does not make the file exist
    • Mutable metadata means the seller can change what you bought after you bought it. Fully on-chain art is rare, small, and the only case where the chain guarantees the artefact
  • Royalties as a social norm that was tested and failed
    • ERC-721 has no enforcement mechanism for royalties; marketplaces paid them voluntarily. When zero-royalty venues took share in 2022–2023, the rest followed, and creator royalties on secondary sales became effectively optional
    • Attempted enforcement via transfer allow-lists worked only where the marketplace cooperated, which is where enforcement was not needed
  • The market cycle, with the data — the 2021 boom, the collapse in volume and floor prices through 2022–2023, and the long tail of collections with no bid at all. Any claim about NFT adoption should be checked against volume excluding wash trading, which was a large share of reported activity on incentive-paying venues
  • Where it worked — on-chain generative art, where the token is the artwork and the provenance chain is the point; ticketing and access passes, where the asset is a credential with a short life; and in-game items where the issuer's own economy gives them meaning
  • Inscriptions on Bitcoin — arbitrary data embedded in witness space, no smart-contract layer, and an unresolved argument about whether this is a legitimate use of block space or a spam vector. Worth knowing as the case where the same idea meets a very different protocol culture
  • The legal reality — buying a token conveys no copyright unless a licence says so. Most collections grant a limited personal-use licence, some grant broad commercial rights, most buyers read neither. The token records who holds the token

Work through

  • Ethereum Improvement Proposals — read ERC-721 and ERC-1155 in full. They are short, and the absence of what people assume is present is the point.
  • Web3 is Going Just Great — the NFT entries, as the running record of what went wrong and how often it was the off-chain layer.
  • The Cryptopians — for how the culture that produced this market formed.

Practice

Take three NFTs from different collections and trace each to its artefact: read tokenURI from the contract, follow it, and determine whether the metadata is on chain, on IPFS, or on a web server; whether it can be changed and by whom; and what the licence grants. Done when you can state, for each, exactly what disappears if the project's team stops paying its bills.

Where people go wrong

Believing "it's on IPFS" means permanent. IPFS is content addressing plus a peer-to-peer transport: a CID guarantees that the bytes you fetch are the bytes that were hashed, and guarantees nothing about anyone still hosting them. If the project's pinning service lapses and no one else pinned the file, the CID resolves to nothing — permanently and irreversibly, because nobody can recover data from a hash. Ask who is pinning it and what happens when they stop.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain to a buyer precisely what the chain guarantees about their purchase — and name the three separate parties who must keep cooperating for the image to keep appearing.

M10

DAOs and on-chain governance

≈10 h

Every parameter you met in M04, M05 and M06 is set by a governance process, so governance is where protocol risk ultimately lives. The gap between how DAOs are described and how they operate is large and measurable, and this module is about measuring it. M02 supplies the token distribution and M08 the attack surface.

Concepts

  • The actual governance stack, which is more centralised than the diagrams
    • Off-chain signalling on a snapshot of token balances (free, non-binding), on-chain proposals through a Governor contract (binding, expensive), a timelock between passage and execution, and — in most cases — a multisig that holds the keys and does the executing
    • The timelock is the most user-protective component, because it is the only thing that gives holders time to exit before a malicious upgrade lands
  • Plutocracy and apathy, measured — one token one vote makes governance weight proportional to capital, and participation is routinely in the low single digits of supply. A handful of delegates decide most outcomes, and quorum is often the binding constraint rather than opposition
  • Vote markets — if governance weight is transferable, it has a price
    • The Curve wars: protocols needing emissions directed at their pools acquired and locked governance tokens, then an aggregation layer emerged, then an explicit market for paying voters. It worked exactly as an economist would predict, and it demonstrated that "governance token" and "bribe-collecting instrument" are the same asset
    • The uncomfortable implication: vote buying is not an attack on this system, it is a market clearing
  • Governance attacks, which are cheap when voting power is rentable
    • Beanstalk, April 2022: a flash loan bought a governance supermajority within one transaction and passed a proposal draining roughly $182 million. The vulnerability was that voting power was measured at execution time with no lock-up
    • Tornado Cash, May 2023: a proposal containing hidden malicious code passed and handed control of the DAO to the attacker, who later returned it. Proposal review is a security control, and reading bytecode is not what most voters do
    • The general defences: snapshot voting power at proposal time, require a lock, and put a timelock in front of execution
  • Legal personality — the Ooki DAO case, where the CFTC charged a DAO as an unincorporated association and won a default judgment in 2023 imposing a penalty and ordering the site shut down. The holding that matters: token holders voting on governance can be treated as members of an entity with liability. Legal wrappers exist as a response, and their coverage is jurisdiction-specific
  • Alternatives and their problems — quadratic voting reduces whale dominance and requires Sybil resistance that does not exist (M11); delegation concentrates power in a professional delegate class; futarchy is elegant, rarely used and needs a liquid market on the outcome. "Progressive decentralisation" describes a real path and much more often describes a founding team retaining the keys

Work through

  • Moving beyond coin voting governance — the critique from inside the ecosystem, including why bribery is inherent rather than incidental.
  • DAOs are not corporations — on where decentralised governance is genuinely appropriate and where it is decoration.
  • OpenZeppelin Governor documentation — the reference implementation: proposal thresholds, quorum, voting delay, timelock.
  • Snapshot — pull the vote distribution for three recent proposals in one DAO and compute the concentration.
  • Safe documentation — because the multisig is usually the thing that actually executes; read the module and guard architecture.
  • CFTC action against Ooki DAO — the primary document for how a regulator treated a DAO as a legal person.
  • Uniswap governance forum — follow one contested proposal from post to execution, including the delegate statements.

Practice

Audit one DAO's governance: token concentration by the top ten addresses, quorum requirement, historical turnout, timelock length, whether a multisig can bypass the process, and what that multisig's threshold and signer set are. Then compute the cost of acquiring enough voting power to pass a proposal, using real market depth. Done when you can state that cost in dollars and compare it to the value the DAO controls.

Where people go wrong

Reading "governed by the community" and picturing the token holders. In most DAOs the executing authority is a multisig held by a small, often unnamed group, and the vote authorises rather than performs. Find the address that can actually move the funds or upgrade the contracts, look at its signer threshold, and treat the governance process as the constraint on that address — which is what it is. If there is no timelock, there is no constraint at all.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a DAO with a high quorum requirement can be safer against capture and simultaneously less able to respond to an emergency — and describe the mechanism most protocols use to escape that tradeoff, along with the risk it introduces.

M11

Identity, reputation and funding public goods

≈6 h

Several mechanisms in this course — quadratic voting, fair airdrops, undercollateralised credit, one-person-one-vote anything — are blocked on the same missing primitive: a way to tell that two addresses are different people. This module is short, honest about the lack of a solution, and optional on the working-knowledge path. It follows M10 because governance is where the gap bites hardest.

Concepts

  • Sybil resistance as the general problem — identities are free, so any mechanism that weights by person rather than by capital is attackable by anyone willing to make more addresses
  • Proof of personhood and its approaches — biometric enrolment, social-graph verification, and in-person key ceremonies. Each trades a different thing: biometrics raise surveillance and irrevocability concerns and have drawn regulatory action in several jurisdictions; social graphs are gameable by coordinated groups; ceremonies do not scale
  • Attestations and portable reputation — signed, revocable statements about an address, stored on chain or off, with the reader deciding which issuers to trust. This is the workable direction, and it just relocates the trust question to issuer selection rather than answering it
  • Quadratic funding — matching contributions in proportion to the square of the sum of square roots, so many small donors outweigh one large one
    • It is the theoretically correct mechanism for funding public goods under the stated assumptions, and its assumptions include no collusion and no Sybils
    • In practice both occur, and the running mitigations are pairwise-coordination discounts and identity gating — which reintroduces M11's own problem. Reported rounds have removed substantial fractions of contributions as fraudulent
  • Retroactive public goods funding — pay for work after its value is observable rather than forecasting it in a grant application. It removes the forecasting problem and replaces it with a voter-selection problem, and the empirical record is short but more encouraging than prospective grants
  • On-chain credit — the natural application of reputation and the one that keeps failing, because a pseudonymous borrower's cost of abandoning a reputation is bounded by what it earns them

Work through

  • A Flexible Design for Funding Public Goods — the quadratic funding mechanism and, importantly, its stated assumptions.
  • Moving beyond coin voting governance — the sections on identity and collusion resistance, which set up why this is hard rather than unsolved by accident.
  • Devcon Archive — the public-goods-funding and identity talks; this material moves faster than any written source.

Practice

Take a published quadratic funding round's contribution data and recompute the matching allocation. Then simulate a Sybil attack: split one large contribution across n addresses and recompute. Plot the attacker's matching gain against n and against the round's anti-collusion discount. Done when you can state how many identities it takes to double a project's match under that round's actual rules.

Where people go wrong

Presenting a Sybil-resistance mechanism as solved because it filters the attacks someone already ran. These are adversarial classifiers whose criteria are published, and the farms adapt within one round. The honest framing is a cost curve — how much does it cost an attacker to obtain n accepted identities — and any mechanism whose security rests on that cost should state the number rather than claiming the category is handled.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name three mechanisms in the earlier modules that would work if proof of personhood existed, and say what each currently substitutes in its place and what that substitution costs.

M12

Regulation, compliance and the surveillance stack

≈10 h

The legal perimeter now determines which mechanisms can be offered, to whom, and by whom — and it changed substantially in 2025. Read the primary sources rather than commentary: the statutes and the orders are short, and secondary summaries of this material go stale within months. Depends on M05, M08 and M10 for the products being regulated.

Concepts

  • The securities question and the Howey framework — investment of money, common enterprise, expectation of profit, from the efforts of others. Applying it to a token that is sold by a promoter and then traded among strangers is what produced years of enforcement-by-litigation rather than rulemaking
  • The US framework as it stands in mid-2026
    • The GENIUS Act became law on 18 July 2025 and covers payment stablecoins: full reserves, monthly disclosure, licensing, no issuer-paid yield. Agency rules were due 18 July 2026 with effect within six months of publication
    • Market structure — which tokens are commodities, which are securities, and who supervises exchanges and brokers — remains with the CLARITY Act, which passed the House in July 2025 and is still pending in the Senate. The most contested provision extends the stablecoin yield prohibition to exchanges and affiliates
    • The practical effect of the split: stablecoins have a settled federal regime and everything else does not
  • The EU under MiCA — applicable in full since 30 December 2024, with authorisation requirements for issuers and service providers, reserve and redemption rules for both asset-referenced and e-money tokens, and a passporting regime. It is the most complete framework currently in force anywhere
  • Sanctions and the limits of applying them to code — OFAC designated the Tornado Cash contracts in 2022. The Fifth Circuit held in November 2024 that immutable smart contracts are not "property" of a foreign national under IEEPA, and OFAC delisted them in March 2025. The durable point is that sanctioning an autonomous program is legally different from sanctioning a person, and the law had no ready category for it
  • DAO liability — the Ooki DAO holding from M10, and the resulting question every governance participant now faces about what voting exposes them to
  • AML in practice — the travel rule applied to virtual asset service providers, KYC concentrated at the fiat on-ramps and off-ramps, and blockchain analytics
    • Pseudonymity is not anonymity: clustering heuristics plus one KYC'd touch point deanonymise most activity, and the analytics industry exists because a public ledger is the best transaction dataset that has ever existed
    • The illicit share of activity is measurable and is consistently a low single-digit percentage, which is neither the "almost none" nor the "mostly crime" claim you will hear
  • Where enforcement actually lands — front ends, developers and interfaces, because the contracts cannot be reached. Front-end takedowns and geoblocking are now the standard compliance surface, and they are also the point at which "decentralised" gets tested

Work through

  • GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27) — the definitions and the reserve and yield provisions. It is readable statute; do not take a summary's word for it.
  • Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA) — the recitals for intent, then Titles III–V.
  • CFTC action against Ooki DAO — the theory of liability, stated in the regulator's own words.
  • Van Loon v. Department of the Treasury — the Fifth Circuit opinion. Short, well written, and the reasoning about what a smart contract is legally is worth reading in the original.
  • Number Go Up — the reporting on what enforcement was responding to; read it as a corrective to industry framing rather than as analysis.
  • Unchained — the litigation and policy episodes, which cover rulings faster than anything written.

Practice

Take one live product — a yield-bearing stablecoin, a perpetuals venue, a lending market — and write its regulatory analysis for two jurisdictions: which regime applies, what it requires, which requirement the product currently fails, and what the operator would have to change. Cite the section number for every claim. Done when every assertion traces to a statute or an order rather than to a law firm's client alert.

Where people go wrong

Assuming decentralisation is a legal status. It is a factual matter that regulators assess, and the questions are who profits, who controls the keys, who runs the interface and who markets the product. A protocol with an anonymous team, a front end on a company's domain and a treasury controlled by four signers is not decentralised in any sense a court has accepted. The Ooki holding cuts further: participating in governance can itself be participation in the entity.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a stablecoin issuer now has a clearer compliance path in the US than a lending protocol does, and name the specific piece of legislation whose absence causes that asymmetry.

M13

Institutions, real-world assets, and what has actually worked

≈6 h

The course closes by asking the question the field usually avoids: after fifteen years, which of these mechanisms is doing real work for people who are not speculating on it? The answer is shorter than the syllabus and more interesting than either the promotional or the dismissive version.

Concepts

  • Stablecoin payment rails as the clearest product-market fit — dollar-denominated transfers that settle in seconds across borders, used most heavily where local banking is expensive or capital controls bind. This is the use case with genuine non-speculative demand, and it is a payments product rather than a finance product
  • Tokenised treasuries and money-market funds — real assets, real issuers, real yield, and a legal wrapper doing most of the work. The chain provides settlement and composability; it does not remove the custodian, the transfer agent or the eligibility rules, which is why access remains gated
  • What "real-world assets" means in practice — the on-chain token is a claim on an off-chain entity, so the whole construction is only as good as that entity and the legal system enforcing the claim. The chain does not make an illiquid asset liquid; it makes an illiquid claim transferable, which is a smaller and still useful thing
  • CBDCs versus stablecoins — the same payment function with opposite trust and privacy properties, and the policy question of whether a public alternative crowds out a private one. Several jurisdictions have chosen to regulate private issuance instead of issuing
  • Prediction markets as the surprise — they aggregate information, their forecasts have been competitive with polling and expert aggregation on well-specified questions, and they have a regulatory path in some jurisdictions. The open questions are resolution disputes on ambiguous questions and whether liquidity survives outside election cycles
  • Honest accounting of the rest — decentralised physical infrastructure, on-chain gaming, machine-to-machine payments and agent-driven transactions are all real projects with thin evidence. Say "promising and unproven" and keep the two words together
  • What did not replicate — undercollateralised lending, algorithmic stablecoins, enforced NFT royalties, most token-governed treasuries deploying capital well, and the general claim that removing intermediaries reduces total cost rather than relocating it. Each of these failed for a reason you can now state precisely, which is the actual outcome of this course

Work through

  • DefiLlama — chart stablecoin supply, real-world-asset TVL and DEX volume over three years and see which curves are secular rather than cyclical.
  • Token Terminal — rank protocols by fee revenue rather than by TVL, and note how short the list of genuinely revenue-generating protocols is.
  • SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — revisit the taxonomy now and mark which categories have working production examples.
  • Unchained — the institutional-adoption and tokenisation episodes, for what the buyers themselves say they want.

Practice

Write the honest one-page assessment: for each of the field's five largest categories by value, state the mechanism, the evidence it works, the evidence it does not, and what observation over the next two years would change your mind. Done when at least one category's entry concludes against your prior, with the data that moved you.

Where people go wrong

Treating the two available conclusions — this changes everything, or this is all a scam — as the only options. Both are unfalsifiable as usually stated. The productive version is per-mechanism: stablecoin transfers demonstrably move real value at low cost; algorithmic stablecoins demonstrably do not work; AMMs work and mostly transfer money from passive LPs to arbitrageurs; DAO governance works for parameter changes and not for capital allocation. Hold the verdicts separately, and update them separately.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name one mechanism in this course that clearly works, one that clearly does not, and one where the evidence is genuinely mixed — and defend each with a number rather than an argument from first principles.

Capstone

Project 1 — The LP's real return. Pick one liquidity pool with at least a year of history and answer, from raw on-chain data, whether providing liquidity to it has been worth doing. Reconstruct every swap, mint and burn; compute fees earned; compute the value of the same assets held; compute the value of a rebalancing benchmark; and decompose the difference into fees, impermanent loss and loss-versus-rebalancing.

  • Scope: one pool, one year, daily granularity, an external price series for the rebalancing benchmark.
  • Draws on: M01, M03, M07.
  • Done when: your three benchmarks reconcile to within a stated error, and you can attribute the LP's underperformance or outperformance to arbitrage flow with a number rather than an assertion.
  • Stretch: repeat for a concentrated-liquidity position with a fixed range and quantify the cost of not rebalancing.

Project 2 — The risk memo. Write the memo an institutional allocator would need before putting capital into one live protocol. Cover the mechanism, the collateral and oracle dependencies, the governance and key-control structure, the exploit history of the protocol and its dependencies, the regulatory classification in one jurisdiction, and the specific scenarios that produce total loss.

  • Scope: one protocol, every claim sourced to a contract address, a transaction, a statute or a dataset.
  • Draws on: M04, M05, M06, M08, M10, M12.
  • Done when: a reader who has never heard of the protocol can state, from your memo alone, the three most likely ways they lose their money and roughly how much warning they would get.
  • Stretch: price the risk — what yield would compensate for it, and how does that compare to what the protocol currently pays?

Mastery check

  • I can derive constant-product pricing and compute exact output, price impact and fee for any trade size.
  • I can explain why impermanent loss is the wrong benchmark and state loss-versus-rebalancing precisely.
  • I can find the price shock at which a real lending market produces bad debt, accounting for gas and AMM depth.
  • I can trace the Terra/UST collapse through the mint-burn arbitrage and identify the point of no return.
  • I can name what each of the three stablecoin designs risks, and why those risks barely correlate.
  • I can specify an oracle and then describe the attack my own design still permits and what it costs.
  • I can decompose an MEV supply chain from on-chain data and say who paid.
  • I can classify any protocol failure into implementation bug, economic design flaw, oracle failure or key compromise.
  • I can read an NFT contract and state what the buyer owns and what breaks when a third party stops paying.
  • I can compute the dollar cost of capturing a given DAO and compare it to what that DAO controls.
  • I can say which regime — GENIUS, MiCA, or neither yet — binds a given product, citing a section.
  • I can name one mechanism in this field that works, one that does not, and one that is unresolved, with data for each.

Where to go next

  • Blockchain Systems — the layer underneath: cryptographic primitives, Nakamoto and BFT consensus, the EVM's execution model, rollups and data availability, zero-knowledge proofs. If any module here felt like it was standing on machinery you could not see, that is the page.
  • Market microstructure — adverse selection, inventory risk and the Glosten–Milgrom and Kyle models are the proper theoretical home for M03 and M07. The AMM literature is largely a rediscovery of these results under a new constraint.
  • Mechanism design and auction theory — order-flow auctions, liquidation auctions, quadratic funding and fee markets are all auction designs, and the economics literature on them is deeper than anything crypto-native.
  • Financial regulation — M12 is a snapshot of a fast-moving perimeter. The underlying subject — how securities, commodities, payments and banking law divide the world — changes slowly and explains most of what the snapshot contains.
  • Empirical finance and event studies — most claims in this field are testable and few have been tested. The methods for doing it properly are the highest-leverage thing to learn next.

Papers & primary sources

R-08 to R-17 are the mechanism literature; R-21 to R-24 are the legal primary sources, and reading them beats any summary.

  1. R-08 Uniswap v2 Core — Hayden Adams, Noah Zinsmeister & Dan Robinson, 2020

    Ten pages that define the constant-product AMM as deployed: the invariant, fee accounting, the price accumulator used for TWAP oracles, and flash swaps. Read it before anything else in M03, and note that the oracle design here is the one M06 spends its time qualifying.

    Core Free online Intermediate 10 pp
    Used in M03
  2. R-09 Uniswap v3 Core — Hayden Adams, Noah Zinsmeister, Moody Salem, River Keefer & Dan Robinson, 2021

    Concentrated liquidity, ticks and the √P parameterisation. The mathematics is the point: work sections 6.1–6.3 by hand or you will carry a wrong mental model of what a range position holds. The design change is what turned liquidity provision from passive into an active strategy.

    Core Free online Advanced 10 pp
    Used in M03
  3. R-10 StableSwap: Efficient Mechanism for Stablecoin Liquidity — Michael Egorov, 2019

    The invariant that blends constant-sum and constant-product, with an amplification parameter controlling where the curve switches between them. Short and worth plotting for several values of A — the shape explains both why stablecoin swaps are nearly free and why a depeg is so painful for the pool.

    Free online Intermediate 6 pp
    Used in M03
  4. R-11 Automated Market Making and Loss-Versus-Rebalancing — Jason Milionis, Ciamac C. Moallemi, Tim Roughgarden & Anthony Lee Zhang, 2022

    The most important paper on this page. It replaces impermanent loss with a benchmark that isolates exactly what LPs lose to arbitrageurs, shows it scales with volatility squared and with block time, and gives the condition under which fees compensate for it. Read it slowly; everything in M03 and M07 depends on getting this right.

    Core Free online Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M03
  5. R-12 Improved Price Oracles: Constant Function Market Makers — Guillermo Angeris & Tarun Chitra, 2020

    The general theory of constant-function market makers and the conditions under which their reported price tracks the true one. This is the formal bridge between M03 and M06: it tells you when an AMM is a usable oracle and, more usefully, exactly when it is not.

    Core Free online Advanced ~12 pp
    Used in M03 M06
  6. R-13 SoK: Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — Sam M. Werner, Daniel Perez, Lewis Gudgeon, Ariah Klages-Mundt, Dominik Harz & William J. Knottenbelt, 2021

    The systematisation-of-knowledge paper: protocol categories, their primitives, and a taxonomy of attacks and economic risks. The best single map of the space and the right thing to read before and again after the exploit modules — it is the framework M08's classification exercise uses.

    Core Free online Intermediate ~17 pp
    Used in M08 M13
  7. R-14 DeFi Protocols for Loanable Funds: Interest Rates, Liquidity and Market Efficiency — Lewis Gudgeon, Sam M. Werner, Daniel Perez & William J. Knottenbelt, 2020

    The empirical study of how on-chain lending rates are actually set, how they respond to utilisation, and where the markets are inefficient. Read it alongside R-15 to see the designed mechanism and its measured behaviour next to each other.

    Free online Intermediate ~12 pp
    Used in M04
  8. R-15 Compound: The Money Market Protocol — Robert Leshner & Geoffrey Hayes, 2019

    The original utilisation-curve lending design, and still the clearest statement of it: supply and borrow rates, the reserve factor, and the interest-accrual accounting. Short. Everything in M04 is a variation on this document, so read it before the documentation of any specific protocol.

    Free online Intermediate 9 pp
    Used in M04
  9. R-16 Flash Boys 2.0: Frontrunning, Transaction Reordering, and Consensus Instability in Decentralized Exchanges — Philip Daian, Steven Goldfeder, Tyler Kell, Yunqi Li, Xueyuan Zhao, Iddo Bentov, Lorenz Breidenbach & Ari Juels, 2019

    Named the problem, measured the priority-gas-auction bots, and made the argument that ordering revenue threatens consensus stability itself. Everything written about MEV since is a footnote to this. Read the consensus-instability section carefully — it is the part most summaries omit.

    Core Free online Intermediate ~17 pp
    Used in M07
  10. R-17 Quantifying Blockchain Extractable Value: How Dark is the Forest? — Kaihua Qin, Liyi Zhou & Arthur Gervais, 2021

    The measurement paper: how much MEV there was, split by category, over a long window. Use it to calibrate M07's claim that arbitrage rather than sandwiching dominates the totals, and note the methodology section — the definitional choices move the numbers a lot.

    Free online Advanced ~18 pp
    Used in M07
  11. R-18 Anatomy of a Run: The Terra Luna Crash — Jiageng Liu, Igor Makarov & Antoinette Schoar, NBER working paper, 2023

    The academic reconstruction of the largest failure in the field's history, with the wallet-level data on who exited when. It shows the run had the classic structure — sophisticated holders left first, retail last — and that the mechanism accelerated rather than resisted it. The definitive source for M05.

    Core Free online Intermediate ~50 pp
    Used in M05
  12. R-19 Cryptocurrencies and Decentralized Finance (DeFi) — Igor Makarov & Antoinette Schoar, NBER working paper, 2022

    The best survey of the field's market structure by two finance economists: where trading happens, how concentrated holdings are, how the arbitrage between venues works, and what the returns actually were. Start M01 here rather than with any industry report.

    Core Free online Intermediate ~70 pp
    Used in M01
  13. R-20 A Flexible Design for Funding Public Goods — Vitalik Buterin, Zoë Hitzig & E. Glen Weyl, 2018

    The quadratic funding mechanism, with the optimality result and — read this part — the assumptions it needs: no collusion and no Sybil identities. M11 is essentially the story of what happens when those assumptions fail in the field, so read the theorem and its conditions together.

    Free online Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M11
  14. R-21 GENIUS Act, Public Law 119-27 — 119th Congress, enacted 18 July 2025

    The first US federal framework for payment stablecoins: reserve composition, monthly disclosure, the licensing paths, and the prohibition on issuers paying yield.     Core Free online Intermediate Statute
    Used in M05 M12
  15. R-22 Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 on Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) — European Parliament and Council, in force, fully applicable since 30 December 2024

    The most complete crypto framework currently operating anywhere. Titles III and IV cover asset-referenced and e-money tokens — the stablecoin rules for M05 — and Title V covers service providers. The recitals are unusually useful for understanding intent before you read the articles.

    Free online Advanced Statute
    Used in M05 M12
  16. R-23 CFTC action against bZeroX and the Ooki DAO — US Commodity Futures Trading Commission, September 2022

    The regulator's own statement of the theory that a DAO is an unincorporated association whose token holders are members — and therefore that voting can carry liability.     Free online Intermediate Primary source

  17. R-24 Van Loon v. Department of the Treasury — US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, No. 23-50669, November 2024

    The opinion holding that Tornado Cash's immutable smart contracts are not "property" of a foreign national under IEEPA, and that OFAC therefore exceeded its authority; the designation was withdrawn in March 2025. Short, clearly written, and the best available judicial reasoning on what a smart contract is legally.
