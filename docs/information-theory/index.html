Information Theory

In 1948 Shannon proved that a noisy channel has a number attached to it, and that below that number you can communicate with arbitrarily small error — no slowing down required. This syllabus takes you from that theorem to the machinery it spawned: entropy and divergence, the compressors in your operating system, the error-correcting codes in every phone, rate–distortion theory, and the modern use of the same three quantities as tools in statistics, machine learning and quantum mechanics.

What this topic is

Information theory is the mathematics of what can be communicated, stored and inferred, and at what cost. Its founding move was to define the information content of a message without reference to what the message means — the engineering problem is reproducing a symbol sequence at the far end, and semantics never enters the calculation. From that one decision follow three quantities: entropy H, which measures how many bits a source genuinely needs; relative entropy D, which measures how many extra bits you pay for believing the wrong distribution; and mutual information I, which measures how much one variable says about another. Nearly every theorem in the field is an operational statement about one of these three.

The theory's two pillars are the source coding theorem — you cannot compress below entropy, and you can get arbitrarily close — and the noisy-channel coding theorem, which says every channel has a capacity below which error can be driven to zero without slowing down. Both are asymptotic existence proofs, and the sixty years since have largely been spent building codes that actually attain them and working out what happens when the block is short. The same three quantities have since become standard tools in statistics, where they produce minimax lower bounds, and in machine learning, where cross-entropy loss is a codelength.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes comfort with probability at the level of a solid first course — random variables, expectation, independence, conditional distributions, the weak law of large numbers — plus enough linear algebra to be unbothered by matrix rank and null spaces, and enough calculus to differentiate under a constraint. You should be able to read a proof and write a short program. You do not need a signals background, an algebra background, or any quantum mechanics.

  • Probability, if it has gone rusty — the first three chapters of Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms rebuild exactly what is needed and nothing more.
  • Convexity and Jensen's inequality, which do most of the work in every proof here — appendix and Chapter 2 of Elements of Information Theory.
  • Finite fields, needed only from M11 onward — Chapter 2 of Essential Coding Theory is a self-contained treatment aimed at exactly this gap.
  • Linear algebra over the complex numbers, needed only for M20 — Chapter 2 of Quantum Computation and Quantum Information is the standard on-ramp and assumes no physics.

Not covered here: the signal-processing layer beneath the channel model — modulation, constellations, equalisation, OFDM — which decides how a bit becomes a waveform but is a separate discipline. Coding theory as pure mathematics (algebraic-geometry codes, the MacWilliams identities, list decoding beyond a mention) gets only what a communications engineer needs. Quantum computation — Shor, Grover, circuit synthesis, hardware — is not here either; M20 covers the information-theoretic half of quantum information and stops at the door.

What you'll be able to do

  • Derive the entropy function from its axioms, and explain why any other candidate measure fails one of them.
  • Prove the source coding theorem via typical sets, and say precisely where the proof stops being true for a non-ergodic source.
  • Write an arithmetic coder and an ANS coder from scratch, and explain why both beat Huffman on a skewed binary alphabet by a factor that Huffman cannot close.
  • Compute the capacity of a binary symmetric, binary erasure and additive Gaussian channel by hand, and verify each by simulation.
  • Reconstruct the random-coding argument, and explain what it does and does not tell you about any code you could actually build.
  • Use the finite-blocklength normal approximation to predict achievable rate at n = 128 and say why capacity alone would have misled you.
  • Explain why 5G uses LDPC codes on the data channel and polar codes on the control channel, in terms of blocklength and decoder cost rather than brand.
  • Prove a minimax lower bound with Fano's method, and identify the step where the information-theoretic content actually enters.
  • Audit a machine-learning paper's use of mutual information — say whether the quantity is estimable at the sample size claimed, and what its estimator's bias does.
  • State three classical theorems whose quantum analogue is provably different, and name what breaks in each proof.
  • Read a current paper in IEEE Transactions on Information Theory and identify which of the three quantities it is really about.

Syllabus

M01

The problem Shannon solved, and why nobody expected the answer

≈4 h

The central theorem of this field is counter-intuitive, and if you meet it as a formula you will nod at it without registering how strange it is. Before 1948 every practising engineer believed that pushing error rate toward zero meant pushing rate toward zero — repeat the message, slow the telegraph, spend more power. Shannon proved that belief false. This module installs the wrong intuition first, deliberately, so the theorem in M08 lands as the shock it was.

Concepts

  • The pre-Shannon picture — Nyquist (1924) on signalling speed and Hartley (1928) on "information" as log of the number of distinguishable symbol sequences
    • Hartley had the logarithm and the alphabet-size argument; what he did not have was noise as a probabilistic object, so he could not ask about reliability at all
    • Why the engineering folklore "to halve the error, halve the rate" is exactly the claim the coding theorem refutes
  • Shannon's separation of the problem into source, channel and destination — and the decision that meaning is irrelevant to the engineering question
    • "Frequently the messages have meaning… these semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem" — read this as a scoping decision, not a philosophical claim
    • The consequence: two messages with wildly different importance and identical statistics cost identical bits, which is why information theory says nothing about value
  • The three theorems the 1948 paper actually contains — the source coding theorem, the noisy-channel coding theorem, and the capacity of the band-limited Gaussian channel
    • All three are existence results proved non-constructively; the paper exhibits no usable code, and that gap took forty-five years to close
  • Why the paper was so complete that the field spent decades filling in rather than redirecting — and the counter-case, that Shannon's own later work on rate–distortion (1959) and secrecy (1949) opened two more fields
    • The "bandwagon" problem Shannon himself warned about in 1956: the theory got applied to psychology, linguistics and economics far faster than it earned the right to be

Work through

  • A Mathematical Theory of Communication — Part I and the introduction. Do not attempt the whole thing yet; read it again after M08 and again after M13.
  • Transmission of Information — Hartley's paper, for the shape of the question before noise entered it.
  • The Mathematical Theory of Communication — Weaver's introductory essay, which is where the popular reading of the theory comes from and where most of the misreadings start.
  • The Bit Player — 90 minutes, for the biography and the Bell Labs context.
  • Journey into information theory — the first few videos, before anything else on this page. It is the only account here that assumes nothing at all.
  • Claude Shannon Centennial Conference presentations — Verdú's talk on Shannon's life, for what 1948 looked like from inside Bell Labs rather than from the citation count.

Practice

Design a scheme to send one bit reliably over a channel that flips each transmitted bit independently with probability 0.1, using majority-vote repetition. Compute, by hand or by simulation, the error probability at 3, 5, 11 and 51 repetitions, and plot error against rate. Done when you have a curve that clearly shows error going to zero only as rate goes to zero — and you have written down, in one sentence, what the coding theorem will have to do to beat it.

Where people go wrong

Reading "information" as "meaning", usually via Weaver's essay rather than Shannon's paper. Shannon's information is a property of a probability distribution over messages, not of any single message: a page of Shakespeare and a page of random letters from the same alphabet with the same letter statistics carry the same number of bits, and the random page carries more. This is not a defect to be patched — every operational theorem in the field depends on it. Fields that have tried to bolt semantics on (semantic information, "meaningful information") have produced nothing with the operational force of the coding theorems.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state, without formulas, what a 1947 engineer would have said was impossible about the noisy-channel coding theorem, and why repetition coding is the natural thing to reach for and the wrong thing to reach for.

M02

Entropy: the axioms, the chain rule, and what it actually measures

≈6 h

Everything downstream is bookkeeping on H, so the definition has to stop feeling arbitrary. Two independent arguments pin it down: an axiomatic one, where continuity, monotonicity and a grouping property force the −Σ p log p form up to a constant, and an operational one, where H turns out to be the expected length of the best possible code. The second is the one that matters, and it is the reason this module comes before compression rather than after it.

Concepts

  • H(X) = −Σ p(x) log p(x) as the unique function satisfying Shannon's three axioms — continuity in the probabilities, monotonic increase in n for the uniform distribution, and the grouping (recursivity) property
    • Work the uniqueness proof once. The grouping axiom is the load-bearing one; drop it and Rényi entropies of every order satisfy the rest
    • Base 2 gives bits, base e gives nats, base 256 gives bytes — the choice is a unit, not a modelling decision, and mixing them silently is a common source of factor-of-0.693 bugs
  • Joint entropy, conditional entropy, and the chain rule H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X)
    • Conditioning reduces entropy on average: H(X|Y) ≤ H(X), but H(X|Y=y) can exceed H(X) for a particular y. Learning something can genuinely surprise you; it cannot surprise you in expectation
    • The general chain rule over n variables, which is the workhorse of nearly every converse proof in this syllabus
  • Concavity of H and its consequences — via Jensen, which you will use twenty more times before M21
    • Uniform maximises entropy on a finite alphabet; the maximum-entropy story generalises in M04
  • Entropy rate of a stochastic process — H(𝒳) = lim H(Xn|X1…Xn−1), and why this, not per-symbol entropy, is the right quantity for real sources
    • Shannon's own estimates of English: roughly 4.1 bits/letter at first order, dropping toward 1.0–1.5 bits/letter with enough context — the gap is the entire business case for context modelling in M06
    • The prediction game Shannon used to get those numbers, and why human-in-the-loop estimation gives an upper bound rather than the truth

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 2 entire, then Chapter 4 sections 4.1–4.2 for entropy rate. This is the cleanest statement of the definitions you will find.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapters 2 and 4. MacKay's exercises are graded by difficulty; do every one marked with a filled triangle.
  • MIT 6.441: Information Theory — Chapter 1 of the Polyanskiy–Wu notes, which starts from divergence and derives entropy, the reverse of the usual order and worth seeing.
  • Visual Information Theory — read it in one sitting for the geometric picture of joint and conditional entropy; it is the diagram most people end up carrying in their heads.
  • MacKay's video lectures — lectures 1–3, if you would rather watch the definitions being derived than read them.
  • Silesia compression corpus — use one of its text files for the practice below, so your bits-per-character figures are comparable to published ones.
  • Solving Wordle using information theory — watch before the textbook chapters. It gets you to "entropy is the expected surprise of an observation" in one worked example.

Practice

Take a book-length plain-text file. Compute the zeroth-, first-, second- and third-order empirical entropy per character. Then run the same file through gzip, xz and zstd and convert each output size to bits per character. Done when you can place all seven numbers on one axis and explain the ordering — in particular why your third-order model beats gzip and why xz still beats your third-order model.

Where people go wrong

Treating higher-order empirical entropy as if it were the source's true entropy rate. With a 27-symbol alphabet, a third-order model has ~20,000 parameters, and a book gives you a few hundred thousand characters — the empirical entropy you compute is biased downward, badly, because the model is partly memorising your sample. That bias is not a nuisance to be waved at; it is the entire subject of M18, and it is why the number you just computed is a lower bound on nothing.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the grouping axiom in your own words, produce a function that satisfies the other two axioms but not grouping, and say what goes wrong operationally if you used your function to price a code.

M03

Relative entropy and mutual information: the two quantities that do the work

≈7 h

Entropy gets the publicity; divergence does the labour. Every converse in this syllabus is ultimately a data-processing inequality, and every data-processing inequality is a statement about KL divergence shrinking. Learning the field with entropy as the primitive and divergence as a derived curiosity is the single most common way to end up unable to prove anything — so this module inverts the emphasis.

Concepts

  • Relative entropy D(P‖Q) = Σ p log(p/q) as the expected excess codelength from coding P-distributed data with a code built for Q
    • Non-negativity via Jensen (Gibbs' inequality), with equality iff P = Q — the proof is four lines and you should be able to write it cold
    • Why D is not a metric: asymmetric, no triangle inequality, and unbounded when Q has a zero where P does not. The asymmetry is not a flaw — D(P‖Q) and D(Q‖P) answer different questions, which is exactly the forward-KL / reverse-KL distinction that decides whether a variational fit covers all modes or one
  • Mutual information I(X;Y) = D(PXY‖PXPY) = H(X) − H(X|Y), and the several other identities that all say the same thing
    • Symmetry, non-negativity, and I(X;X) = H(X) — the last of which is why "self-information" and entropy coincide for discrete variables and diverge in M04
    • Conditional mutual information and the chain rule for I; note that I(X;Y|Z) can be larger or smaller than I(X;Y) — conditioning is not monotone, which is why "controlling for a variable" has no general information-theoretic guarantee
  • The data processing inequality — if X → Y → Z is a Markov chain then I(X;Z) ≤ I(X;Y)
    • Post-processing cannot manufacture information. This one sentence is the converse half of nearly every coding theorem in the field
    • Strong data processing inequalities: the contraction coefficient η, which quantifies how much a channel strictly shrinks information, and why it matters for lower bounds in distributed estimation
  • Fano's inequality — H(X|Y) ≤ h(Pe) + Pe log(|𝒳|−1), turning "hard to decode" into "high conditional entropy"
    • This is the hinge of the converse in M08 and of every minimax lower bound in M16. Derive it once and you own both
  • The wider family: f-divergences (total variation, χ², Hellinger, Rényi), Pinsker's inequality relating TV to KL, and when to reach for which
    • Total variation is the one with a direct operational meaning in hypothesis testing; KL is the one that tensorises cleanly over independent copies. Most proofs move between them via Pinsker
  • Sufficient statistics stated information-theoretically — T(X) is sufficient for θ iff I(θ;X) = I(θ;T(X)), i.e. the DPI holds with equality

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 2 sections 2.3–2.9. Prove the DPI yourself before reading their proof.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — Parts I and II. Polyanskiy and Wu build the whole theory on divergence from the start; this is the treatment that makes M16 possible later.
  • MIT 6.441: Information Theory — Chapters 2–4 of the notes, including the strong data-processing material that most first courses omit.
  • Information Theory: Coding Theorems for Discrete Memoryless Systems — Chapter 1 for the f-divergence framing, if you want the sharpest version.
  • Solving Wordle using information theory — rewatch the scoring section once you have the definition of mutual information; the guess score is I(answer; pattern).

Practice

Implement D(P‖Q) and I(X;Y) for discrete distributions. Then build a three-node Markov chain X → Y → Z with a channel of your choice and verify numerically that I(X;Z) ≤ I(X;Y) over a few hundred random parameter draws. Then find a Z, X, Y where I(X;Y|Z) > I(X;Y). Done when you have a concrete example of the second — the standard one is Z = X ⊕ Y with X, Y independent fair bits, where I(X;Y) = 0 and I(X;Y|Z) = 1.

Where people go wrong

Reading mutual information as "nonlinear correlation" and stopping there. It is worse than that in two directions. First, I = 0 does mean full independence, which correlation does not — that part is genuinely stronger. Second, the value of I is on a scale nobody has intuition for: I = 0.3 bits does not mean "30% related", and comparing mutual informations across variable pairs with different alphabet sizes compares nothing at all. For continuous variables it is unbounded and can be infinite for a perfectly ordinary deterministic relationship. If you want a number in [0,1] you have to normalise explicitly and say by what.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive Fano's inequality from scratch, and then explain in one sentence why it is the natural tool for proving that something cannot be done — rather than for proving that something can.

M04

Continuous alphabets: differential entropy and the maximum-entropy principle

≈6 h

Nearly every misuse of information theory in applied science happens at this boundary. Differential entropy looks like entropy, is written like entropy, and behaves like entropy in none of the ways that matter — it can be negative, it changes under a change of units, and it is not the limit of the discrete entropy of a finer and finer quantisation. You need this module before M09's Gaussian capacity and before M18's estimation problems, and you need it as inoculation.

Concepts

  • Differential entropy h(X) = −∫ f log f, and the three ways it fails to be entropy
    • Quantise X to precision Δ and the discrete entropy is ≈ h(X) + log(1/Δ). The divergent log(1/Δ) is exactly what h(X) throws away, which is why h can be negative — a uniform on [0, ½] has h = −1 bit
    • h(aX) = h(X) + log|a|: differential entropy is not invariant under rescaling, so "the differential entropy of this measurement" is unit-dependent and meaningless without stating the unit
    • D(f‖g) and I(X;Y) are invariant and non-negative in the continuous case — the log(1/Δ) cancels in the difference. This is why you should reach for divergence and mutual information here and treat h as an intermediate
  • Maximum-entropy distributions under moment constraints — uniform on a bounded support, exponential given a mean, Gaussian given a variance
    • The Lagrange-multiplier derivation, and the recognition that the answer is always an exponential family with the constraints as sufficient statistics
    • The Gaussian result is what makes M09 work: among all noise distributions with a given power, Gaussian is the worst, which is why the AWGN capacity is a benchmark rather than a special case
  • Jaynes' maximum-entropy principle as an inference rule, and the honest objections to it
    • Its defence: given constraints, MaxEnt is the distribution that assumes nothing further, and it coincides with the Bayesian posterior under a specific prior
    • Its problem: the answer depends on the choice of base measure, which MaxEnt does not supply. In continuous settings "maximum entropy" is under-determined until you say maximum entropy relative to what
  • The entropy power inequality — N(X+Y) ≥ N(X) + N(Y) for independent X, Y — and why it is the deep continuous-alphabet result
    • Shannon stated it in 1948; the first complete proof came later, and it remains the main tool for converses in Gaussian multi-user problems in M14

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 8 entire, plus Chapter 17 section 17.8 for the entropy power inequality.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — the chapters on differential entropy and Gaussian saddle-point, which handle the measure-theoretic care that Cover and Thomas skate over.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapter 22 on maximum entropy, which is unusually honest about the base-measure objection.
  • ITPRNN course page — the maximum-entropy and inference exercises, which are the best available practice for this module.

Practice

Take a Gaussian with σ = 1. Quantise it at Δ = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 and compute the discrete entropy of the quantised variable at each step. Plot HΔ + log Δ against Δ. Done when the curve visibly flattens onto the analytic h(X) = ½log(2πeσ²), and you can state what would have happened if you had instead plotted HΔ alone.

Where people go wrong

Reporting a negative differential entropy as an anomaly, or worse, as evidence of something. It is not an anomaly — it is what the definition does whenever the density exceeds 1 somewhere, and it carries no information about the variable beyond scale. The related and more damaging error is computing "the mutual information between two continuous signals" by histogram-binning them and reporting the number as if it were bin-independent. It is not: refine the bins and the estimate climbs without bound. M18 is about what to do instead.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why h(X) changes if you switch a temperature reading from Celsius to Fahrenheit while I(X;Y) does not, and say what that implies about which of the two belongs in a scientific paper.

M05

The asymptotic equipartition property and typical sets

≈6 h

This is the engine room. Both coding theorems are proved by the same trick — long sequences from a source split into a vanishing-probability junk pile and a "typical set" of about 2nH roughly-equiprobable sequences, and you only ever have to index the typical ones. Once you see that, source coding, channel coding and rate–distortion stop being three theorems and become three applications of one counting argument.

Concepts

  • The AEP: for i.i.d. X1…Xn, −(1/n) log p(X1…Xn) → H(X) in probability
    • It is the weak law of large numbers applied to log p, and nothing more. Seeing that keeps the AEP from looking like magic
    • The Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem extends it to stationary ergodic sources with almost-sure convergence — the version you need for real data in M07
  • The typical set Aε(n) — probability → 1, size between (1−ε)2n(H−ε) and 2n(H+ε), and every member roughly equiprobable
    • The typical set is not the set of most likely sequences. For a biased coin with p = 0.1, the single most likely sequence is all-tails, and it is atypical. This trips up nearly everyone once
    • The counting consequence: nH bits suffice to index it, which is the source coding theorem in one line
  • Jointly typical sequences — pairs (xn, yn) typical for the joint distribution, of which there are about 2nH(X,Y)
    • Given a typical yn, roughly 2nH(X|Y) typical xn are compatible with it. The ratio 2nI(X;Y) is the number of messages you can distinguish, and that is M08's whole argument previewed
  • High-dimensional geometry as intuition — why almost all the mass of a high-dimensional Gaussian sits in a thin shell, and why that is the same fact as the AEP
    • The sphere-hardening picture is what makes the Gaussian channel argument in M09 visual rather than algebraic
  • The method of types as the sharper alternative — counting sequences by empirical distribution gives polynomially many types, each of exponential size, and yields exponential error bounds instead of just vanishing ones
    • Types are the right tool from M10 onward; typicality is the right tool for first proofs. Know both and know why the field mostly teaches typicality first

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 3 entire, then Chapter 11 sections 11.1–11.2 for the method of types.
  • Information Theory: Coding Theorems for Discrete Memoryless Systems — Chapter 2. This book is the method of types; even reading the first twenty pages changes how you see the rest of the field.
  • Stanford EE376A: Information Theory — the AEP lectures, for a second pass at a different pace.
  • EE376A scribed lecture notes — the typicality sections, which work the ε-bookkeeping at the level of detail a lecture uses and a textbook compresses away.
  • Stanford EE376A homework — the typicality problems; do these before the channel-coding sets in M08.
  • Stanford EE274: Data Compression — lecture 5, which derives the AEP on video at the speed of someone writing it out.

Practice

For a Bernoulli(0.3) source, generate 105 sequences of length n = 100 and n = 1000. Empirically measure the fraction landing in the typical set at ε = 0.05, and compare the size of the typical set to 2n. Done when you can show the typical set is a vanishing fraction of all sequences while carrying almost all the probability, and you can state both numbers for n = 1000 without hand-waving.

Where people go wrong

Believing the AEP means "long sequences all look alike". They do not — a typical sequence has an empirical distribution close to p, but there are exponentially many such sequences and they are entirely distinguishable from each other. The AEP says their probabilities are all close to 2−nH, which is a statement about the measure, not about the sequences. The practical consequence: you cannot use the AEP on short blocks. At n = 100 the typical-set argument is quantitatively useless, and pretending otherwise is precisely the error M10 exists to correct.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why the most probable sequence is usually atypical, and why that is not a paradox but the entire point of the compression argument.

M06

Lossless source coding: Kraft, Huffman, arithmetic coding and ANS

≈9 h

M05 proved a bound exists. This module is where you build the things that reach it, and where the theory meets an industry. It also contains the field's cleanest example of a result everyone "knows" being narrower than they think — Huffman is optimal, but only within a class that turns out to be the wrong class, and the codecs shipping in your operating system left that class thirty years ago.

Concepts

  • Prefix (instantaneous) codes and the Kraft–McMillan inequality — Σ 2−li ≤ 1 is necessary and sufficient for a prefix code, and McMillan's extension shows uniquely decodable codes buy you nothing more
    • Kraft is what turns "choose codeword lengths" into "choose a probability distribution", which is the identity the rest of the module runs on: any code is a model, any model is a code
    • The expected-length bound H(X) ≤ L* < H(X) + 1, and why that trailing +1 is negligible for a large alphabet and catastrophic for a binary one
  • Huffman coding — the greedy bottom-up construction, and the exchange-argument proof of optimality among symbol codes
    • The failure mode: with a binary alphabet at p = 0.99, entropy is 0.08 bits/symbol and Huffman must spend 1 bit/symbol, a 12× overhead. Blocking symbols together helps but the table grows exponentially
    • Canonical Huffman codes, which is how real formats (DEFLATE, JPEG) transmit the tree compactly
  • Arithmetic coding — encode the whole message as one number in [0,1), sidestepping the integer-length constraint entirely
    • Rissanen and Pasco independently in 1976; the Witten–Neal–Cleary 1987 paper is the one that made it implementable, with the renormalisation and underflow handling that the idea needs to survive finite precision
    • Redundancy is O(1) bits for the whole message rather than per symbol — this is the structural win, and it is why arithmetic coding cleanly separates modelling from coding
    • The patent thicket around it in the 1990s, which is a large part of why DEFLATE used Huffman and why the field lost a decade
  • Asymmetric numeral systems (ANS) — Duda's 2009–2014 construction encoding information into a single natural number
    • rANS and tANS: the tabled variant becomes a finite-state machine with no multiplication in the inner loop, which is why it is fast
    • Arithmetic-coding compression ratio at Huffman-like speed. This is why zstd, Apple's LZFSE and Google's Draco all adopted it, and why Duda deliberately placed it in the public domain after the arithmetic-coding patent experience
    • The LIFO quirk: ANS decodes in reverse order of encoding, which changes how you structure a codec
  • Dictionary methods — LZ77 (sliding window), LZ78/LZW (explicit dictionary), and how they combine with entropy coding in real formats
    • DEFLATE = LZ77 + Huffman; zstd = LZ77-family + tANS + a trained dictionary option; xz = LZMA with range coding and context modelling
    • The Burrows–Wheeler transform as a third strategy: reversible reordering that makes a simple model effective, which is bzip2's whole trick
  • Context modelling and adaptive coding — PPM, context mixing, and the general principle that the model, not the coder, is where the remaining gains are

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 5 entire. The Kraft inequality proof and the Huffman optimality proof are both here and both worth doing on paper.
  • Arithmetic coding for data compression — the paper that made arithmetic coding practical; the C code in it still reads clearly.
  • Asymmetric numeral systems — Duda's paper. Sections 1–3 are the ones to read; the rest is variants.
  • Finite State Entropy — Yann Collet's implementation notes, which is where ANS goes from paper to production and where the practical gotchas live.
  • A universal algorithm for sequential data compression — LZ77, for the shape of the original argument.
  • A Method for the Construction of Minimum-Redundancy Codes — Huffman's four pages. Read the exchange argument and then note precisely how the optimality claim is scoped.
  • Lossless Compression with Asymmetric Numeral Systems — a slow derivation of rANS with numeric examples, if Duda's paper loses you early.
  • RFC 8478 — the zstd format spec; read the FSE sections to see tANS as a normative bit layout rather than a construction.
  • zstd — the FSE source. Read it against the paper and the RFC; three views of one algorithm is what makes it stick.
  • Introduction to Data Compression — Chapters 3–5 for the engineering treatment, including the details real codecs need and theory books omit.
  • Stanford EE274: Data Compression — lectures 6 and 7, arithmetic coding and ANS, worked to the level of detail you need to implement them.
  • Information Theory collaboration with IEEE — the Lempel–Ziv episode, for why a dictionary method was such a departure from the Huffman tradition.

Practice

Write a static arithmetic coder and decoder for a binary source, in any language, with 32-bit integer arithmetic and correct renormalisation. Test it on a Bernoulli(0.99) stream of 106 bits. Then write a Huffman coder for the same source. Done when the arithmetic coder's output is within 0.01 bits/symbol of the entropy 0.0808, the Huffman coder produces 1.0 bits/symbol, and your decoder reproduces the input exactly — including the last symbol, which is where every first implementation breaks.

Where people go wrong

"Huffman coding is optimal." It is optimal among codes that assign an integer number of bits to each symbol independently. That qualifier is the whole game: arithmetic coding and ANS leave the class and beat it, sometimes by an order of magnitude on skewed alphabets. The second, subtler error is thinking the coder is where compression happens. It is not — for any competent entropy coder the redundancy is a fraction of a bit per message, and every remaining gain comes from the probability model you feed it. That is why "compression research" is mostly modelling research, and why an LLM is a state-of-the-art compressor (M07).

Checkpoint

M07

Universality, minimum description length, and Kolmogorov complexity

≈8 h

M06 assumed you know the source distribution. You never do. This module covers the three escapes: universal codes that match the entropy rate of any stationary ergodic source without being told which, MDL which turns compression into a model-selection criterion, and Kolmogorov complexity which asks what happens if you drop probability altogether. It also establishes the compressor↔predictor equivalence that M17 depends on, and that makes sense of why a language model is a compressor.

Concepts

  • Universal source coding — LZ78 achieves the entropy rate for any stationary ergodic source, with no prior knowledge of it
    • The proof route via the Ziv–Lempel parsing and the number of distinct phrases; this is the result that justifies gzip working on files nobody characterised in advance
    • Convergence is slow. Universality is an asymptotic promise, and on a 10 kB file a source-specific model still wins comfortably
  • Redundancy and the cost of not knowing — the (k/2) log n term for a parametric family with k parameters
    • This is the same (k/2) log n that appears in BIC, and it is not a coincidence: BIC is a two-part code length
  • Minimum description length — Rissanen's two-part codes, and the refinement to normalised maximum likelihood
    • Crude MDL (model + data-given-model) versus modern MDL (the NML universal distribution); the crude version is the one everyone quotes and the one with the arbitrary-encoding problem
    • MDL as a stance on overfitting: a model that cannot compress its training data is not describing structure, whatever its likelihood says
  • Kolmogorov complexity K(x) — the length of the shortest program outputting x on a universal machine
    • The invariance theorem: change the universal machine and K changes by at most an additive constant. This is what makes the definition meaningful and also what makes it useless for short strings
    • K is uncomputable, by a direct Berry-paradox argument; Chaitin's Ω is the concrete uncomputable number that falls out
    • The relationship E[K(Xn)]/n → H(X) — algorithmic and Shannon information agree in expectation for computable sources, which is the bridge between the two theories
  • Solomonoff induction and the universal prior — the theoretical optimum for prediction, uncomputable, and the ancestor of both MDL and AIXI
    • Why "the shortest explanation is the best" gets a formal statement here, and why that statement cannot be executed
  • The compressor↔predictor equivalence, made concrete — any lossless compressor defines a probability model and vice versa, via arithmetic coding
    • Chinchilla 70B compresses ImageNet patches to 43.4% and LibriSpeech to 16.4%, beating PNG (58.5%) and FLAC (30.3%) despite being trained on text. Log-loss is compression rate, so a scaling law is a statement about compression
    • The honest accounting question: the model weights are not counted in that figure, which is the objection the Hutter Prize was designed to make unavoidable

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapters 13 and 14 (universal coding, Kolmogorov complexity).
  • Compression of individual sequences via variable-rate coding — the LZ78 universality result in the original.
  • An Introduction to Kolmogorov Complexity and Its Applications — Chapters 2 and 3; skip the rest on a first pass, it is a reference work.
  • The Minimum Description Length Principle — Chapters 1–5. Grünwald is careful about the crude/modern distinction in a way secondary sources are not.
  • Language Modeling Is Compression — the whole paper; it is short and it is the cleanest modern statement of the equivalence.
  • Data Compression Explained — Mahoney on context mixing and what actually wins benchmarks, written by someone who competes.
  • Large Text Compression Benchmark — study the leaderboard and note that sizes include the decompressor. That rule is the argument of this module in one column.
  • The Canterbury Corpus — small enough to inspect by hand, which is what you want while debugging the practice below rather than benchmarking it.
  • The Hutter Prize — read the rules and the FAQ, which is the most direct statement anywhere of the compression-equals-intelligence thesis and its scoring consequences.
  • Compression for AGI — the modern restatement of the compressor–predictor equivalence. Watch it, then argue with the last ten minutes.

Practice

Take a small language model you can run locally, use its next-token probabilities to drive the arithmetic coder you wrote in M06, and compress a text file with it. Compare the result against gzip, xz and zstd on the same file. Then compute what the file size would be if you had to ship the model weights alongside. Done when you have both numbers and can say which comparison is the honest one for which purpose.

Where people go wrong

Treating Kolmogorov complexity as something you can compute or approximate with a compressor. K is uncomputable, and "we approximated K(x) using gzip" is a claim about gzip, not about K — the normalised compression distance literature is full of results that are perfectly fine as gzip-based similarity measures and are oversold as algorithmic-information measurements. The second error is the invariance theorem's fine print: the additive constant depends on the choice of universal machine and can exceed the length of any string you care about, so K is only meaningful asymptotically.

M08

Channel capacity and the noisy-channel coding theorem

≈10 h

This is the theorem the field is named for, and the reason M01 spent four hours building the wrong intuition. The proof is also the field's most-copied argument: pick codewords at random, decode by joint typicality, and show the average error over the random ensemble vanishes. Everything from M09 to M14, and the quantum version in M20, is a variation on this one page of reasoning — so it is worth reconstructing rather than reading.

Concepts

  • The discrete memoryless channel as a conditional distribution p(y|x), and capacity C = maxp(x) I(X;Y)
    • Worked cases you should be able to produce from memory: BSC with crossover p gives C = 1 − h(p); BEC with erasure probability e gives C = 1 − e; the Z-channel and the noisy typewriter for the asymmetric intuition
    • Capacity is a maximisation over input distributions, which is why it is a property of the channel alone and why computing it is a concave optimisation — solved numerically by Blahut–Arimoto, which reappears in M13
  • The achievability proof by random coding
    • Generate 2nR codewords i.i.d. from the capacity-achieving input distribution; decode x̂ as the unique codeword jointly typical with the received yn
    • The union bound over 2nR − 1 wrong codewords, each jointly typical with probability ≈ 2−nI(X;Y), gives vanishing error exactly when R < I(X;Y)
    • Expurgation: the argument bounds average error over the ensemble, so throwing away the worst half of codewords converts it to a maximal-error guarantee at negligible rate cost
  • The converse — above capacity, error is bounded away from zero
    • The weak converse via Fano's inequality and the DPI: this is where M03 pays off, and the proof is six lines once you have both
    • The strong converse (Wolfowitz): above capacity, error tends to one, not merely away from zero. The distinction matters for M10
  • The source–channel separation theorem — compress to the source's entropy, then code for the channel, and you lose nothing asymptotically
    • This is why compression and error correction are separate boxes in every real system, and it is an asymptotic result that fails at finite blocklength and in networks (M14). Joint source–channel coding exists because the theorem's conditions are often not met
  • What random coding does not give you — no structure, no efficient decoder, exponential storage
    • The theorem proves good codes are abundant while giving no way to recognise one. Closing that gap took from 1948 to 1993, and M11–M12 is that story
  • Feedback does not increase the capacity of a memoryless channel — a genuinely surprising result worth knowing precisely, since feedback does improve error exponents and finite-blocklength performance

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 7 entire. Then close the book and reproduce the achievability argument on paper.
  • A Mathematical Theory of Communication — Part III, now that you have the machinery. Shannon's own argument is terser and more geometric than the modern presentation.
  • MIT 6.441: Information Theory — the channel-coding chapters, which give the information-spectrum framing that generalises to non-ergodic channels.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapters 9–10, for MacKay's unusually physical account of why the theorem is true.
  • Stanford EE376A: Information Theory — the channel-coding lectures as a second pass.
  • MIT 6.441 problem sets — the channel-coding and converse problems. These are the ones that reveal whether you can use Fano or only recognise it.
  • Journey into information theory — the channel-capacity episodes, as a picture to check the algebra against.

Practice

Implement a BSC simulator. Compute C = 1 − h(p) for p = 0.1. Then build a random codebook of 2nR codewords at n = 20 and R below and above capacity, with a nearest-neighbour decoder, and measure block error rate over 105 trials. Done when you can show error dropping as n grows for R < C and refusing to drop for R > C — and when you can explain why at n = 20 the transition looks like a slope rather than the cliff the theorem promises.

Where people go wrong

Hearing "capacity" as a speed limit on a wire, like bandwidth. It is not a rate in bits per second until you attach a symbol rate; it is bits per channel use, and the channel use is whatever your model says it is. The deeper error is treating capacity as an operating point. Capacity is the supremum of rates at which error can be made arbitrarily small as blocklength grows without bound. No finite system operates at capacity, every real system operates at some backoff from it, and the size of that backoff is not something the coding theorem tells you. That is M10's subject, and skipping it is how people end up quoting Shannon limits at packet sizes where the limit is off by 30%.

M09

Gaussian channels, waterfilling and the Shannon limit

≈6 h

Almost every physical channel is modelled as additive Gaussian noise with a power constraint, so this is where the abstract theorem meets radio, copper and fibre. It is also where M04 earns its place: the Gaussian capacity formula falls out of the maximum-entropy property of the Gaussian, and the "worst-case noise" reading of that fact is what makes ½log(1+SNR) a benchmark rather than a special case.

Concepts

  • The AWGN channel Y = X + Z with E[X²] ≤ P and Z ~ 𝒩(0, N), giving C = ½ log₂(1 + P/N) bits per channel use
    • Derive it: I(X;Y) = h(Y) − h(Y|X) = h(Y) − h(Z), maximised when Y is Gaussian, which happens when X is Gaussian. Two lines, given M04
    • The bandwidth form C = W log₂(1 + P/N₀W), and the limit as W → ∞ giving C → P/(N₀ ln 2) — capacity saturates, more bandwidth stops helping
  • The sphere-packing picture — n uses of the channel put the received vector in a shell of radius √(n(P+N)), and each codeword's noise ball has radius √(nN)
    • The ratio of volumes gives 2nC non-overlapping balls, which is the capacity formula recovered geometrically. This is the picture to carry, and it is the AEP's sphere-hardening from M05
  • Parallel Gaussian channels and waterfilling — allocate power to sub-channels by pouring water into a vessel whose floor is the noise level
    • The Lagrangian derivation, and the practical consequence: the noisiest sub-channels get zero power, which is why real systems turn off bad sub-carriers rather than fighting them
    • Where it actually runs: DSL bit-loading, OFDM sub-carrier allocation, MIMO spatial-mode allocation after the singular value decomposition
  • The "Shannon limit" of −1.59 dB — the minimum Eb/N₀ for reliable communication as spectral efficiency tends to zero
    • What it is: a limit in the infinite-bandwidth, vanishing-rate corner. What it is not: a bound applicable at your operating rate, which is always higher
    • Quoting "we are 0.4 dB from Shannon" is meaningful only with the rate, blocklength and error probability attached — and comparing two systems' distances to it without those is meaningless
  • Coloured noise, the water-filling solution in the frequency domain, and channels with memory
    • The Gaussian channel with intersymbol interference reduces to parallel channels after diagonalisation, which is the entire reason OFDM exists

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 9 entire, including the parallel-channel and coloured-noise sections.
  • Information Theory and Reliable Communication — Chapter 8 for Gallager's treatment, which is more careful about the continuous-alphabet limits than most.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapter 11, for the geometric intuition done well.

Practice

Implement waterfilling for a set of ten parallel Gaussian sub-channels with noise levels you choose spanning two orders of magnitude, subject to a total power budget. Plot the allocated power and the resulting per-channel rate as you sweep the budget from very small to very large. Done when you can identify the exact budget at which the worst sub-channel first receives non-zero power, and explain why the allocation is flat-topped rather than proportional to channel quality.

Where people go wrong

Assuming Gaussian noise is an approximation made for tractability, and that real non-Gaussian noise must therefore give lower capacity. The opposite is true: for a fixed noise power, the Gaussian is the worst case — it maximises entropy, so it minimises capacity. Any other noise distribution with the same variance gives a channel with capacity at least as high. That is what makes ½log(1+SNR) a conservative benchmark, and it is why designing against Gaussian noise is a defensible engineering decision rather than a convenient fiction.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why doubling bandwidth eventually stops doubling capacity, and derive the infinite-bandwidth limit that says so.

M10

Where the asymptotics lie: error exponents and finite blocklength

≈7 h

Capacity is a statement about n → ∞, and no system you will ever build has n → ∞. This module supplies the two corrections. Error exponents tell you how fast error decays for a fixed rate below capacity; the finite-blocklength normal approximation tells you what rate is actually achievable at n = 100 or n = 1000. Skipping this is the most common way for someone who has read a first course to be quantitatively wrong about a real system by tens of percent.

Concepts

  • The reliability function E(R) — the exponential decay rate of error probability at fixed rate R < C
    • Gallager's random coding exponent Er(R) as an achievability bound, derived from the ensemble average without invoking typicality
    • The sphere-packing bound as the matching converse, and the fact that the two coincide above the critical rate Rcrit and are still not tight below it — an open problem hiding in an ordinary-looking gap
    • The practical reading: error exponents tell you how much blocklength buys you, which is what you need to size an interleaver or a frame
  • The finite-blocklength regime — Polyanskiy, Poor and Verdú's normal approximation
    • R*(n, ε) ≈ C − √(V/n) · Q−1(ε), where V is the channel dispersion, the variance of the information density
    • The √(1/n) backoff is the dominant correction; at n = 100 and ε = 10−3 on a typical BSC it removes a substantial fraction of capacity, and the approximation is accurate for blocklengths as short as ~100
    • The achievability and converse bounds behind it: the RCU bound, the DT bound and the meta-converse — the meta-converse being the one that unified a scattered literature by recasting coding as binary hypothesis testing
  • Channel dispersion as a second channel parameter — two channels can share a capacity and differ in how fast they approach it
    • The BEC has zero dispersion in a case worth knowing; the BSC does not. This is a real design distinction, not a curiosity
  • Why this became urgent — URLLC in 5G targets packets of tens of bytes at error rates of 10−5, exactly the corner where asymptotic information theory says nothing usable
    • Short-packet design is the reason finite-blocklength theory moved from a specialist interest to a standards input in about a decade
  • The moderate-deviations regime between the two, where both rate and error vary with n — the third asymptotic, and the one that shows exponents and dispersion are two ends of a continuum

Work through

  • Channel Coding Rate in the Finite Blocklength Regime — the central paper. Read sections I–IV closely; the rest is a reference for specific channels.
  • Information Theory and Reliable Communication — Chapter 5 for the random coding exponent in Gallager's own derivation, which is still the clearest.
  • Finite-Blocklength Information Theory — a 2025 survey; use it to see where the field went after 2010 and which questions are still open.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — the finite-blocklength chapters, by one of the authors of the result.

Practice

For a BSC with p = 0.11 (capacity 0.5), plot three curves of rate against blocklength for n from 50 to 2000 at ε = 10−3: the capacity line, the normal approximation C − √(V/n)Q−1(ε), and the rate of a real code family you can find published results for. Done when you can state the percentage of capacity lost to finite blocklength at n = 128, and say how much of the remaining gap is the code's fault rather than the theory's.

Where people go wrong

Comparing a short-packet system's rate to capacity and concluding the code is bad. At n = 128 the normal approximation may already sit 20–30% below capacity — that loss is information-theoretic and no code can recover it. Benchmark against R*(n, ε), not against C. The mirror-image error is treating the normal approximation as a bound; it is an approximation, sitting between the achievability and converse bounds, and for very small ε or very short n you should compute the actual bounds instead of trusting it.

M11

Algebraic coding: linear codes, the bounds, and Reed–Solomon

≈8 h

M08 proved good codes exist and gave you nothing you could build. For forty-five years the answer was algebra: impose enough structure that encoding and decoding become linear algebra over a finite field, and accept a gap from capacity in exchange for a decoder that runs. That tradeoff still ships — Reed–Solomon is in your storage stack right now — and the parity-check machinery here is what M12's LDPC codes and M20's stabiliser codes are both built from.

Concepts

  • Linear codes over 𝔽q — the [n, k, d] parameters, generator matrix G, parity-check matrix H, and syndrome decoding
    • Minimum distance d corrects ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ errors or detects d−1; the syndrome HyT depends only on the error, which is what makes table-driven decoding possible at all
    • Hamming codes as the first construction worth seeing: perfect, single-error-correcting, and the columns of H are literally the binary numerals — the reason the syndrome is the error position
  • The bounds that fence the design space — Singleton (d ≤ n − k + 1), Hamming/sphere-packing, Gilbert–Varshamov, and Plotkin
    • MDS codes meet Singleton with equality; Reed–Solomon is the canonical family that does, which is why it is the default when you want provable erasure recovery
    • Gilbert–Varshamov is an existence bound proved by counting — the same non-constructive move as random coding, in an algebraic costume. Beating it explicitly stayed open for decades and was eventually done by algebraic-geometry codes
  • Reed–Solomon codes as evaluations of low-degree polynomials, and the decoding story
    • The evaluation view makes erasure decoding obvious: k points determine a degree-<k polynomial, so any k of n symbols suffice
    • Berlekamp–Massey for error decoding, and the Berlekamp–Welch reformulation that is easier to understand and slower to run
    • Where it actually runs: CDs and DVDs, QR codes, RAID-6 and erasure-coded object stores, deep-space telemetry, and the data-availability schemes in modern blockchains
  • BCH codes and the cyclic-code machinery — generator polynomials, the BCH bound, and why cyclic structure gives shift-register encoders
    • This is the family that made hardware encoders cheap in the 1960s, and cheapness is why it dominated for so long
  • Erasure versus error correction — a code that corrects e errors corrects 2e erasures, and knowing where the damage is doubles what you can fix
    • Distributed storage exploits this: the network tells you which node is down, so you are always in the erasure regime
  • Concatenation (Forney) — an outer Reed–Solomon code over an inner short code, the first construction achieving positive rate with exponentially small error at polynomial complexity

Work through

  • Essential Coding Theory — Chapters 1–6. Free, rigorous, and it develops the finite-field background it needs.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapters 1 and 13 for the Hamming-code and distance material at a gentler pace.
  • Hamming codes and error correction — watch both parts before reading the algebra; the parity-position construction is much easier to see animated than written.
  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 7 section 7.11, for how the algebraic tradition sits against the coding theorem.
  • Learning to Learn — Hamming's own coding-theory lectures, for the construction as he arrived at it rather than as it is now presented.

Practice

Implement a (7,4) Hamming code with syndrome decoding, then a Reed–Solomon erasure code over GF(256) with k = 4 data and n = 6 total shards, using Lagrange interpolation for reconstruction. Test the RS code by deleting every possible pair of shards. Done when all 15 deletion patterns reconstruct exactly, and you can say what happens on the sixteenth case where three shards are lost — and why the failure is total rather than partial.

Where people go wrong

Believing a larger minimum distance is straightforwardly better. Minimum distance governs worst-case correction and it is the wrong figure of merit for a probabilistic channel — the codes that actually approach capacity in M12 have unremarkable minimum distances and superb average-case behaviour under iterative decoding. The distance-oriented design tradition and the capacity-oriented one genuinely disagree, and the second one won for wireless links. Distance still rules where an adversary, not a noise process, chooses the errors.

M12

Capacity-approaching codes: turbo, LDPC and polar

≈10 h

In 1993 a paper claimed performance within 0.5 dB of the Shannon limit and was widely disbelieved. It was correct, and the following fifteen years closed the gap that M08 opened. This module is where information theory stops being a set of impossibility results and becomes the design brief for hardware in every phone — and the three families here made genuinely different bets, which is why all three are still deployed.

Concepts

  • Turbo codes — Berrou, Glavieux and Thitimajshima, 1993: two convolutional encoders separated by a pseudo-random interleaver, decoded by exchanging soft extrinsic information
    • The interleaver is the invention. It makes the two decoders' errors nearly independent, so each round of message passing genuinely adds information
    • Why the community initially disbelieved it: the code has no impressive minimum distance, and the field's figure of merit was minimum distance (M11's trap, in its historical form)
  • LDPC codes — Gallager's 1962 thesis, forgotten for thirty years, rediscovered by MacKay and Neal in 1996 when computers could finally simulate them
    • Tanner graphs, variable and check nodes, and the sum–product (belief propagation) decoder as message passing on that graph
    • Density evolution and EXIT charts: how you actually design an irregular degree distribution, and how you compute a threshold — the SNR below which decoding fails no matter the blocklength
    • Error floors from trapping sets and near-codewords, the failure mode that only appears at error rates too low to simulate casually, and which has sunk real deployments
    • Spatially coupled LDPC codes and threshold saturation: coupling the graph along a chain pushes the BP threshold up to the MAP threshold, which is one of the prettiest results of the 2010s
  • Polar codes — Arıkan, 2009: the first explicit, low-complexity construction proved to achieve capacity for symmetric binary-input channels
    • Channel polarisation: recursively combine two copies of a channel and split them, and as n grows each synthesised sub-channel becomes either almost noiseless or almost useless. Send data on the good ones, freeze the rest
    • Successive cancellation decoding is O(n log n) but weak at short blocklengths; SC-list decoding with a CRC as a list selector is what made polar codes competitive in practice
    • CRC-aided polar codes were adopted for 5G control channels in 2016 — from paper to standard in seven years
  • The 5G split, and what each family actually decided
    • LDPC on the data channel: excellent at long blocklengths and high throughput, with parallelisable decoders — but its rate flexibility comes from rate-matching a base graph, and its error floors need care
    • Polar on the control channel: short blocks, very low error rates required, and SCL decoding is sequential but cheap at those sizes
    • Elsewhere: DVB-S2 and Wi-Fi use LDPC; turbo codes ran 3G and 4G and were displaced rather than disproved
  • Why belief propagation works at all — it is exact on trees and the graphs here have loops, so the algorithm is provably an approximation that nonetheless performs superbly
    • Girth, and why the design goal is avoiding short cycles rather than eliminating loops
    • The connection to statistical physics: density evolution is a replica-style calculation, and the threshold phenomena are phase transitions in a precise sense

Work through

  • Modern Coding Theory — Chapters 1–4 for the message-passing framework and density evolution. This is the definitive text.
  • Near Shannon limit error-correcting coding and decoding: turbo-codes — the 1993 paper; read it for how modest the claim looks and how large it was.
  • Low-density parity-check codes — Gallager's original, to see how completely the idea was there in 1962.
  • Near Shannon limit performance of low density parity check codes — the rediscovery, two pages.
  • Channel Polarization — Arıkan's paper. Sections I–IV; the polarisation martingale argument is the part to sit with.
  • Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms — Chapters 47–48, by one of the people who rediscovered LDPC codes.
  • 3GPP TS 38.212: Multiplexing and channel coding — sections 5.3.1–5.4. Read enough to see what a standardised code actually specifies, and how much of it is rate-matching rather than coding.
  • AFF3CT — run its LDPC and polar simulations first, so you have a reference BER curve before you trust your own decoder.
  • Polar Codes I–III — Arıkan's three-session tutorial, after the paper. He explains what he was actually trying to do, which was fix sequential decoding.
  • Information Theory collaboration with IEEE — the LDPC episode, for the thirty-year gap between Gallager and the rediscovery, told by people who were there.

Practice

Implement a regular (3,6) LDPC code with a randomly constructed parity-check matrix at n = 1008, and a sum–product decoder over the BSC. Plot bit error rate against crossover probability, sweeping from well below to above the density-evolution threshold. Done when you see a sharp waterfall in the right place, and when you can make an error floor appear by shortening the girth of your graph deliberately.

Where people go wrong

Reading "capacity-achieving" as a property of a code rather than of a code family with a specified decoder as n → ∞. Polar codes achieve capacity under successive-cancellation decoding asymptotically; at n = 512 with SC decoding they are unremarkable, and the practical variant needs list decoding plus a CRC that no capacity proof mentions. The same applies to LDPC thresholds: density evolution assumes a cycle-free graph and infinite blocklength, so a threshold is a promise about a limit, not a prediction for your n = 648 Wi-Fi code.

M13

Rate–distortion theory and lossy compression in practice

≈9 h

Every media format you use throws information away deliberately, and rate–distortion theory is the theory of doing that optimally. Structurally it is M08 run backwards — a minimisation of mutual information instead of a maximisation — and seeing that duality is worth more than either theorem alone. It is also the module that connects to M17, because a variational autoencoder is a rate–distortion optimiser wearing different notation.

Concepts

  • The rate–distortion function R(D) = min I(X;X̂) over conditional distributions p(x̂|x) with E[d(X,X̂)] ≤ D
    • The mirror of capacity: there you maximised I over inputs, here you minimise it over test channels. Same object, opposite direction, and the achievability proof is again a random-codebook plus joint-typicality argument
    • Shannon's 1959 lower bound and the shape of R(D) — convex, decreasing, hitting zero at D = E[d(X, x̂*)] for the best constant reconstruction
  • Worked cases — Gaussian source with squared error gives R(D) = ½ log(σ²/D) for D ≤ σ²; Bernoulli source with Hamming distortion gives R(D) = h(p) − h(D)
    • The Gaussian result says every extra bit buys 6 dB of SNR, which is the rule of thumb behind every quantiser design
    • Reverse waterfilling for a vector Gaussian source: components with variance below the water level get zero rate and are simply replaced by their mean — the lossy analogue of turning off bad sub-carriers in M09
  • Computing R(D) and C numerically — the Blahut–Arimoto alternating-minimisation algorithm, one of the few genuinely constructive algorithms in classical information theory
    • It is alternating minimisation of a Lagrangian, converges because the objective is convex in each argument separately, and it reappears as the information-bottleneck algorithm in M17
  • What real codecs do instead — transform coding, quantisation, entropy coding
    • The DCT and wavelet transforms as approximations to the Karhunen–Loève transform, decorrelating so that scalar quantisation is nearly as good as vector quantisation
    • Rate–distortion optimisation inside a codec: the Lagrangian J = D + λR that every modern video encoder minimises per block, which is rate–distortion theory used literally rather than metaphorically
    • The JPEG → MP3 → H.264 → AV1 lineage as a story of better transforms, better prediction and better entropy coders, not better rate–distortion theory
  • Why mean squared error is a bad distortion measure and is used anyway
    • It is tractable, it has a closed-form R(D), and it correlates poorly with what people see and hear. Perceptual measures (SSIM, and the psychoacoustic masking models in audio codecs) exist because of the gap
    • The rate–distortion–perception tradeoff (Blau and Michaeli, 2019): realism is a third axis, and forcing the output distribution to match the source's costs rate even at fixed distortion. This is why a GAN-based codec can look better and score worse
  • Neural compression — learned transforms with entropy models trained end to end, now competitive with hand-designed codecs and still losing on decode cost

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 10 entire, including the Blahut–Arimoto section.
  • Rethinking Lossy Compression: The Rate-Distortion-Perception Tradeoff — short and it reframes the whole subject; read it after the classical theory, not before.
  • Introduction to Data Compression — Chapters 9–13 on quantisation and transform coding, for the engineering half.
  • CompressAI — read the reference implementations of the learned entropy models; they are the clearest available statement of how a neural codec is actually assembled.
  • Stanford EE274: Data Compression — the lossy half of the course, where the rate–distortion function stops being a theorem and becomes JPEG.
  • Neural Compression: From Information Theory to Applications — Ballé's and Mentzer's keynotes, for what R(D) looks like when the codebook is learned.

Practice

Implement Blahut–Arimoto for the binary source with Hamming distortion and verify it reproduces R(D) = h(p) − h(D). Then take a real image, apply an 8×8 DCT, quantise the coefficients at a range of step sizes, and plot measured rate against measured MSE. Done when your empirical curve sits above the Gaussian R(D) bound computed from the coefficient variances, and you can account for the gap in terms of what your scheme does not do.

Where people go wrong

Treating R(D) as achievable by any per-symbol scheme. It is not: like capacity, it is an asymptotic result requiring long blocks and vector quantisation, and scalar quantisation plus entropy coding loses about 0.25 bits per sample — the "space-filling loss" — which no amount of clever scalar design recovers. The second error is comparing codecs at equal MSE and declaring a winner. Distortion, rate and perceptual realism form a three-way tradeoff, so a codec can be strictly better on the axis you did not measure.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the rate–distortion problem and the channel-capacity problem side by side and identify every place they are duals — and name the one place the duality breaks.

M14

Network information theory, and the problems that stayed open

≈8 h

Everything so far had one sender and one receiver. Real networks do not, and the multi-user versions are not routine extensions — several of the simplest ones have been open since the 1970s. This module teaches the results that are settled, which include two of the most surprising theorems in the field, and it teaches the shape of the failures, which is more instructive than another success.

Concepts

  • Slepian–Wolf distributed source coding — two correlated sources encoded separately, decoded jointly, can achieve the same total rate H(X,Y) as if they had been encoded together
    • The encoders never communicate and still pay no penalty. This is genuinely astonishing and the random-binning proof is short enough to reconstruct
    • The rate region is a pentagon: each source needs at least its conditional entropy, and the sum needs at least the joint entropy
    • Where it shows up: distributed video coding, sensor networks, and — via the same binning idea — the syndrome-based constructions in M15
  • Wyner–Ziv coding — lossy compression with side information available only at the decoder
    • For jointly Gaussian sources and squared error there is no rate loss relative to having the side information at the encoder too; in general there is. Knowing which case you are in matters
  • The multiple access channel — capacity region fully solved, achieved by successive interference cancellation and time sharing
    • The pentagon region again, and the operational meaning of its corner points: decode one user treating the other as noise, subtract, decode the second cleanly
  • The broadcast channel — solved for degraded channels via superposition coding, open in general after fifty years
    • Marton's inner bound and the best known outer bounds, and the fact that nobody has closed the gap even for two receivers with three messages
  • The interference channel — Han–Kobayashi rate splitting is the best known achievable region and has stood since 1981, still not proved optimal
    • Etkin–Tse–Wang's "within one bit" result: the gap to capacity is bounded by a constant, which is a very useful kind of partial answer and a template for how the field now makes progress on intractable regions
  • The relay channel — posed by van der Meulen in 1971, capacity still unknown for the general case
    • Decode-and-forward, compress-and-forward, and the cut-set upper bound; the cut-set bound is loose and the field knows it is loose
  • Network coding — Ahlswede, Cai, Li and Yeung (2000): routing is not optimal, and mixing packets at intermediate nodes achieves multicast capacity
    • The butterfly network in one diagram is the whole argument; linear network codes suffice for multicast, and random linear coding over a large enough field works with high probability
    • Why it changed less in practice than expected: TCP/IP's layering, and the fact that most traffic is unicast, where routing is optimal
  • Where separation fails — source–channel separation (M08) does not hold in networks, so jointly designed schemes can strictly beat any modular one

Work through

  • Network Information Theory — Chapters 4, 10, 11, 14 and 15. This is the reference for the whole subject.
  • Lecture Notes on Network Information Theory — the free precursor to the book; use it if you want the material without the purchase.
  • Noiseless coding of correlated information sources — Slepian–Wolf in the original, four pages.
  • The rate-distortion function for source coding with side information at the decoder — Wyner–Ziv.
  • Network information flow — the network coding paper.
  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 15 for a first pass before El Gamal and Kim.
  • Information Theory collaboration with IEEE — the network-coding episode. Four minutes, and the butterfly network is the whole idea.

Practice

Implement Slepian–Wolf coding for two correlated binary sources where Y is X passed through a BSC(0.1), using an LDPC code's syndrome as the bin index. Compress X to just above H(X|Y) and decode using Y as side information at the receiver. Done when your decoder recovers X at a rate below H(X) — and you can state what the encoder knew about Y, which is nothing.

Where people go wrong

Assuming the open problems here are open because they are unimportant or because nobody serious tried. The general broadcast, interference and relay channels have been attacked continuously for fifty years by the best people in the field. The obstruction is real: single-letter characterisations — capacity expressed as an optimisation over one channel use — provably do not exist for some multi-user problems, so the usual proof template cannot work. This is why the modern literature reports constant-gap and degrees-of-freedom results instead: partial answers that are honest about being partial.

M15

Secrecy, leakage and privacy

≈7 h

Shannon's second great paper defined what it means for a cipher to be secure in a way that does not depend on the adversary's computer, and proved the price: the key must be as long as the message. Everything cryptography does since is an escape from that price into computational assumptions. This module is the information-theoretic side of the boundary — and it is also where the field's most-repeated modelling error lives, which is using mutual information as a leakage measure when it answers a different question than the one being asked.

Concepts

  • Perfect secrecy — I(M;C) = 0 — and Shannon's bound H(K) ≥ H(M)
    • The one-time pad achieves it and the bound proves nothing shorter can. This is why every practical cipher is computationally secure rather than information-theoretically secure, and the distinction is not a technicality
    • Unicity distance: how much ciphertext an unbounded adversary needs before the key is uniquely determined, which is a redundancy calculation and a nice application of M02
  • Wyner's wiretap channel — if the eavesdropper's channel is strictly worse than the legitimate receiver's, a positive rate is achievable with no shared key at all
    • Secrecy capacity as the difference of two mutual informations, achieved by deliberately randomising the codebook — you spend rate to confuse the eavesdropper
    • Strong versus weak secrecy: weak secrecy bounds the leakage rate and lets total leakage grow without bound, which is not what anyone means by secure. Always check which one a paper proves
    • The deployment problem: the model requires knowing the eavesdropper's channel, and you generally do not
  • Secret key agreement from correlated randomness — Maurer and Ahlswede–Csiszár, 1993: two parties observing correlated sources can distil a shared secret over a public channel
    • Information reconciliation (which is Slepian–Wolf from M14) followed by privacy amplification (which is universal hashing); this two-stage structure is exactly what QKD post-processing does in M20
  • Differential privacy and its information-theoretic readings
    • ε-DP as a bound on a max-divergence, and the hypothesis-testing formulation (f-DP, Gaussian DP) that gives sharper composition than the ε, δ bookkeeping
    • Why DP's guarantee is a worst-case divergence bound rather than an average one — and why that is what makes it compose, which mutual-information-based definitions do not
  • Leakage measures beyond mutual information — min-entropy leakage, maximal leakage, g-leakage and the quantitative-information-flow literature
    • The concrete failure: two systems can be ordered one way by mutual information and the opposite way by an adversary's probability of guessing the secret in one try. If the threat model is "guess the password", mutual information is not measuring the threat
    • Maximal leakage as the measure with a clean operational guarantee over all adversary gain functions
  • Where information-theoretic security actually ships — QKD (M20), physical-layer security research, and secret sharing, where Shamir's scheme is Reed–Solomon from M11 read as a secrecy construction

Work through

  • Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems — Shannon's 1949 paper. Sections on perfect secrecy and unicity distance; the rest is of historical interest.
  • The Wire-Tap Channel — Wyner, 1975. Short, and the randomised-codebook idea is clearer here than in retellings.
  • Information Theory: Coding Theorems for Discrete Memoryless Systems — the second edition's chapter on information-theoretic security, added specifically because the subject grew.
  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 2 section 2.10 and the cryptography exercises, as a warm-up.
  • Success Stories of Differential Privacy — Dwork on what the definition bought in practice, and what composition costs.

Practice

Build a toy system where a secret S ∈ {1…1000} is observed through two different channels: channel A reveals the secret exactly with probability 0.01 and nothing otherwise; channel B reveals the low-order bit always. Compute I(S;output) for both, and compute the adversary's probability of guessing S in one try for both. Done when the two orderings disagree and you can explain to a security engineer which number they should have asked for.

Where people go wrong

Reporting "the system leaks 0.3 bits" as if bits of leakage were an interpretable safety budget. Mutual information is an average over the secret's distribution, and averages hide exactly the tail an attacker exploits — a channel that leaks everything one per cent of the time has small mutual information and catastrophic worst-case behaviour. Pick the measure that matches the threat model: min-entropy leakage for one-guess attacks, maximal leakage for guarantees across unknown adversary goals, differential privacy when you need composition.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: prove that perfect secrecy forces H(K) ≥ H(M), and then explain why the wiretap channel appears to evade that bound without contradicting it.

M16

Information theory as a tool in statistics

≈9 h

Outside communications, this is where information theory has been most useful and least publicised. The technique is uniform: to prove that no estimator can do better than some rate, reduce estimation to a hypothesis test, then bound the test's error with Fano or Le Cam. Every minimax lower bound in modern high-dimensional statistics has this shape, and M03 gave you the whole toolkit without saying what it was for.

Concepts

  • The method of types and large deviations — Sanov's theorem giving the exponential rate at which empirical distributions deviate, with the rate being a KL divergence
    • Cramér's theorem as the scalar special case, and the recognition that the rate function is a Legendre transform — the same convex-duality structure as everywhere else in this syllabus
    • Conditional limit theorem: conditioned on a rare event, the empirical distribution converges to the I-projection, which is a genuinely useful modelling fact
  • Hypothesis testing — Stein's lemma giving error exponent D(P‖Q) for the asymmetric case, and Chernoff information for the symmetric case
    • The Neyman–Pearson lemma as the operational bridge: likelihood-ratio thresholds are optimal, and their error exponents are divergences
  • Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound, and how they relate to the quantities in M03
    • Fisher information is the local curvature of KL divergence: D(Pθ‖Pθ+dθ) ≈ ½ IF(θ) dθ². KL is the global object, Fisher the infinitesimal one, and this identity is why both appear in the same proofs
    • The Cramér–Rao / entropy-power connection, via the de Bruijn identity linking Fisher information to the derivative of entropy along a heat flow
  • Minimax lower bounds — the three standard reductions
    • Le Cam's two-point method: reduce to distinguishing two hypotheses, bound by total variation. Simple, and often loose by a factor of the dimension
    • Fano's method: reduce to an M-ary test over a packing set, apply Fano from M03. This is the workhorse for nonparametric and high-dimensional rates
    • Assouad's lemma: reduce to d simultaneous binary tests, which handles coordinate-wise loss cleanly
    • The recipe is always the same: construct a well-separated packing in the parameter space, upper-bound the mutual information between index and data, conclude that no estimator can be reliable
  • Strong data processing and distributed estimation — when data is split across machines with a communication budget, contraction coefficients give lower bounds that ordinary DPI cannot
    • This is where information theory earns its keep in modern statistics: the bounds on federated and communication-constrained estimation are information-theoretic and have no other derivation
  • Information-theoretic model selection — AIC as an estimate of expected KL divergence to the truth, BIC as a two-part codelength from M07, and why they disagree systematically rather than randomly

Work through

  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 11 entire (types, Sanov, Stein, Chernoff), plus Chapter 17 for the Fisher information connections.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — Part VI. This is the best single treatment of the statistics crossover in print, by people who work on both sides.
  • Information-theoretic Methods for High-dimensional Statistics — Wu's lecture notes; the minimax chapters are worked in more detail than the book.
  • High-Dimensional Statistics: A Non-Asymptotic Viewpoint — Chapter 15, which is the statistician's-eye view of the same machinery and worth reading against the others.
  • UIUC ECE 563: Information Theory — Raginsky's notes, which sit deliberately at this intersection.
  • Distribution Estimation and Applications I–III — Orlitsky's boot-camp sessions, for the sample-complexity view of the same minimax questions.

Practice

Prove a minimax lower bound of order √(d/n) for estimating the mean of a d-dimensional Gaussian in Euclidean loss, using Fano's method: construct a packing of the unit sphere, bound the mutual information between packing index and sample, apply Fano. Done when your bound matches the known rate up to constants, and you can point at the single step where the information-theoretic content enters — and say what would change if the packing were smaller.

Where people go wrong

Treating a minimax lower bound as a statement about the problem you actually have. Minimax is a worst case over a parameter class, and the adversarial parameter is often nothing like your data — a bound saying "no estimator achieves better than rate r uniformly" is entirely compatible with your estimator doing far better on your instance. The bound tells you no method is uniformly better, not that yours cannot be improved. Reading minimax rates as performance predictions is a habitual error in applied papers.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: write down the three-step Fano recipe from memory and apply it to a problem you have not seen worked — and explain why the packing has to be well-separated in the loss and close together in KL.

M17

Information theory in machine learning

≈9 h

Machine learning uses information-theoretic language constantly, sometimes rigorously and sometimes decoratively, and telling the two apart is a skill worth having. Cross-entropy loss is literally a codelength; the ELBO is literally a rate–distortion Lagrangian; generalisation bounds in terms of mutual information are real theorems. The information bottleneck story is the cautionary counter-example, and this module takes it seriously in both directions.

Concepts

  • Cross-entropy, log-loss and codelength are the same object
    • Minimising cross-entropy is minimising expected codelength, which is minimising KL to the truth plus a constant. Every classifier trained with log-loss is being trained as a compressor, and M07's equivalence makes that literal
    • Why perplexity is 2cross-entropy and therefore an effective alphabet size, which makes it comparable across models and not across tokenisers
  • Variational inference as an information problem — the ELBO decomposes into a reconstruction term and a KL term
    • The β-VAE objective is a rate–distortion Lagrangian with β = λ from M13: rate is the KL to the prior, distortion is the reconstruction error, and β traces the R(D) curve
    • Bits-back coding as the operational meaning of the KL term, and why it explains posterior collapse — if the decoder can model the data alone, the optimal rate is zero
  • Estimating and bounding mutual information for training objectives
    • MINE, InfoNCE and the variational lower-bound family; InfoNCE is what contrastive learning optimises, and it is a lower bound on MI
    • The hard ceiling: any distribution-free lower bound on MI computed from n samples cannot exceed log n with high confidence (McAllester–Stratos). InfoNCE with batch size 256 cannot certify more than 8 bits, whatever it reports
    • The consequence: contrastive methods work, but not because they are measuring mutual information accurately — the objective's success and its information-theoretic interpretation have come apart, and the literature says so
  • The information bottleneck, and the case against it
    • The original 1999 formulation: compress X to T while preserving I(T;Y), solved by a Blahut–Arimoto-style iteration. As a clustering and representation-learning objective this is sound and useful
    • The 2017 deep-learning claims: a two-phase fitting-then-compression dynamic, causally responsible for generalisation, driven by SGD noise
    • Saxe et al.'s rebuttal: none of the three claims holds generally. The compression phase is an artefact of saturating nonlinearities and the binning used to make I(T;X) finite in a deterministic network; it appears with tanh and not with ReLU, it appears under full-batch gradient descent so it is not SGD noise, and networks that do not compress still generalise
    • The deeper problem: for a deterministic network, I(T;X) is either infinite or equal to H(X), so any finite plot is measuring the estimator's binning, not the network. Keep the IB as an objective; discard the empirical narrative
  • Information-theoretic generalisation bounds — the line that did survive
    • Xu and Raginsky: generalisation gap bounded by √(I(S;W)/2n), where S is the training sample and W the learned weights. An algorithm that memorises has high I(S;W) and no guarantee
    • The conditional mutual information (CMI) framework, which fixes the bound's vacuity for deterministic algorithms and is now the standard formulation
    • The unification with PAC-Bayes: the same bounds, derived twice, and the 2025 monograph that finally treats them as one subject
  • Where else it shows up honestly — mutual-information feature selection and its redundancy problem, decision-tree information gain, and channel-capacity framings of model capacity

Work through

  • The information bottleneck method — the 1999 original, for what the objective actually is.
  • Opening the Black Box of Deep Neural Networks via Information — the claim, in full. Read it first and let it convince you.
  • On the information bottleneck theory of deep learning — the rebuttal, immediately afterwards. This pairing is the best available lesson in how an appealing empirical claim fails.
  • New Theory Cracks Open the Black Box of Deep Learning — the Quanta article that carried the claim to a wide audience in 2017; read it last, as a case study in how fast this travelled.
  • Information-theoretic analysis of generalization capability of learning algorithms — Xu and Raginsky, the bound that held up.
  • Generalization Bounds: Perspectives from Information Theory and PAC-Bayes — the current reference. Chapters 1–4 for the framework, then the CMI chapter.
  • On Variational Bounds of Mutual Information — organises MINE, InfoNCE and the rest into one family with a shared bias–variance tradeoff.
  • Formal Limitations on the Measurement of Mutual Information — read straight after: it says what none of those estimators can do.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — the learning chapters, which is where the book's title comes from.
  • Information Theory of Deep Learning — Tishby arguing it in person in 2018. Listen for which claims are theorems and which are observations about tanh networks.

Practice

Train a small classifier twice, once with tanh activations and once with ReLU, and reproduce the information-plane plot by binning the hidden activations. Then re-plot with a different number of bins. Done when you have produced both the "compression phase" and its disappearance from the same network by changing only the activation and the binning — and can state, in one sentence, what that demonstrates about the original claim.

Where people go wrong

Reporting mutual information between a neural network's internal representation and its input as if it were a measured property of the network. For a deterministic network with continuous activations that quantity is degenerate — infinite, or equal to H(X) — and every finite value in the literature comes from a discretisation choice that is doing the work. If you must report it, add noise to make the map stochastic and say how much, or report a variational bound and state the log-batch ceiling that limits it. Silently binning and plotting is not a measurement.

M18

Estimating information from finite data

≈6 h

Every applied use of information theory ends at the same place: you have samples, not distributions, and you need a number. Estimating entropy and mutual information is much harder than it looks, the naive estimator is biased in a direction that manufactures spurious structure, and the theory of how to do it well was only settled in the 2010s. Without this module, M17 is a vocabulary; with it, M17 is a method.

Concepts

  • The plug-in estimator and its bias — Ĥ from empirical frequencies underestimates H by roughly (k−1)/(2n ln 2) bits for alphabet size k and n samples
    • The bias is systematic and downward, which for mutual information means upward bias in Î — unseen symbols look like structure. This is why naive MI estimates find dependence in independent data
    • Miller–Madow as the first-order correction, and why it stops helping when k is comparable to n
  • The sample-complexity result that reframed the problem — estimating entropy to fixed accuracy needs Θ(k / log k) samples, not Θ(k)
    • Achieved by polynomial-approximation estimators (Jiao–Venkat–Han–Weissman; Wu–Yang), which use best polynomial approximation in the small-count regime and the corrected plug-in elsewhere
    • The practical reading: you can estimate the entropy of a 10,000-symbol alphabet from far fewer than 10,000 samples, which the naive analysis says is hopeless. Use an estimator that knows this
    • Bayesian alternatives: NSB, which puts a prior over the entropy itself rather than over the distribution, and works well when the alphabet is large and unknown
  • Continuous variables — the KSG estimator (Kraskov, Stögbauer, Grassberger) using k-nearest-neighbour distances
    • Why it is the default: no binning, adapts to local density, and the bias partially cancels between the joint and marginal terms by construction
    • Its failure modes: strong dependence (where it underestimates badly), heavy tails, high dimension, and ties in discrete-valued data that break the nearest-neighbour geometry silently
    • Choosing k: small k is low bias and high variance, and the estimate should be reported across a range of k rather than at one
  • Binning as the method to avoid, and what to do if you must
    • Estimates increase monotonically with bin count until they saturate at log n, so "we chose 20 bins" is a free parameter that sets the answer
    • Equal-occupancy binning is less bad than equal-width; neither is defensible without a bias correction
  • Validation discipline — permutation tests for a null distribution, and always estimating on data where the true value is known before trusting a pipeline
    • The single most useful habit in this module: shuffle one variable, re-estimate, and see what your estimator reports for data you know is independent. If it does not report ≈0, your number means nothing

Work through

  • Estimating mutual information — the KSG paper. Read the derivation of the estimator, not just the recipe; the bias cancellation is the idea.
  • Minimax Estimation of Functionals of Discrete Distributions — the k/log k result. Sections I–III for the statement and the intuition.
  • NPEET — read the source; it is short enough to read entirely and it is the reference implementation of KSG.
  • dit — for the discrete side, including the estimators most packages omit.
  • Information Theory: From Coding to Learning — the functional-estimation chapters, which give the minimax framing from M16.
  • Distribution Estimation and Applications I–III — the sessions on estimating entropy and support size, which is where the k/log k result comes from.

Practice

Generate 500 samples from a bivariate Gaussian with known correlation ρ = 0.6, where the true mutual information is −½log(1−ρ²). Estimate it four ways: histogram binning at 10 and 50 bins, KSG at k = 3 and k = 10. Then repeat with ρ = 0 and 200 permutation shuffles. Done when you can state each estimator's bias and its false-positive rate under the null, and say which one you would defend in a paper.

Where people go wrong

Reporting an estimated mutual information without a null. The plug-in estimator applied to independent variables returns a positive number that grows with alphabet size and shrinks with sample size — so "we found I = 0.15 bits between these variables" is not evidence of anything until you have shown what the estimator returns on shuffled data. This single omission accounts for a large fraction of the spurious information-theoretic results in neuroscience and genomics, and the fix costs twenty lines of code.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why entropy's plug-in bias is downward while mutual information's is upward, and derive the sign of each from the same argument.

M19

Physics, biology, betting: the field's reach beyond communication

≈6 h

This module is the one elective on the page — nothing after it depends on it, and you can go straight to M20. It is here because information theory has made genuine, non-metaphorical contributions in three other fields, and because separating those from the large volume of loose analogising is a skill in itself. Landauer's principle in particular is a physical law with an experimentally measured constant, not a simile.

Concepts

  • Maxwell's demon and its resolution — the 150-year argument that ended in an information-theoretic accounting
    • Szilard's single-molecule engine (1929) reduced the paradox to one bit and extracted kT ln 2 of work from it, which is the first appearance of the bit as a physical quantity
    • Landauer's principle (1961): erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat. Measurement can be reversible; forgetting cannot
    • Bennett's resolution (1982): the demon's memory fills up, and resetting it pays back exactly the entropy the demon extracted. The books balance because of erasure, not because of observation
    • Bérut et al. measured it in 2012 with a single colloidal particle in an optical trap, confirming the bound experimentally
  • Thermodynamic entropy and Shannon entropy — where the identification is exact and where it is an analogy
    • Gibbs entropy over microstates is Shannon entropy of the microstate distribution times kB, which is an identity, not a resemblance
    • What does not transfer: "entropy = disorder", and the use of the second law to make claims about information in systems with no well-defined state space. The pop-science version has caused decades of confusion in adjacent fields
  • Biology — information in neural codes and gene regulation
    • Measuring the information rate of a spike train, and why the answer depends entirely on the stimulus ensemble you chose — a fact that makes cross-study comparison mostly meaningless
    • Information transmission in developmental gene networks, where the measured capacities are around 1–2 bits and that is enough to specify position in an embryo
    • The estimation problem from M18 is the binding constraint on this whole literature, not the theory
  • Betting and portfolios — Kelly's 1956 paper, which is information theory applied to capital growth
    • The doubling rate of an optimal gambler equals the mutual information between the side information and the outcome; better information is worth exactly its bits in growth rate
    • Log-optimal portfolios and the duality with universal source coding, which is why universal portfolio algorithms look exactly like universal compressors
    • Why practitioners use fractional Kelly: full Kelly is growth-optimal and has drawdowns that no real institution tolerates, and the objective is not what anyone actually maximises
  • Language — the uniform information density hypothesis, that speakers spread information evenly across an utterance, and the evidence for and against it

Work through

  • Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process — Landauer, 1961.
  • Experimental verification of Landauer's principle — Bérut et al., 2012, for the measurement.
  • A New Interpretation of Information Rate — Kelly, 1956. Six pages, and the connection to channel capacity is explicit in the original.
  • Elements of Information Theory — Chapter 6 (gambling) and Chapter 16 (portfolios), which are the parts of that book nobody assigns and which are excellent.
  • Christoph Adami on How Information Makes Sense of Biology — an eighty-minute worked example of naming the random variables before claiming a bit has been transmitted.

Practice

Simulate a repeated bet with known odds and a side-information channel of your choosing that predicts the outcome with some accuracy. Compute the optimal Kelly fraction, simulate 10,000 rounds, and measure the realised doubling rate. Done when your measured growth rate matches the mutual information between side information and outcome to within simulation noise — and when you have also plotted the maximum drawdown, so you can see why nobody bets full Kelly.

Where people go wrong

Sliding between the exact identifications and the loose analogies without noticing which one is in play. Gibbs entropy is Shannon entropy of the microstate distribution; Landauer's bound is a physical limit with a measured value. "The organism reduces its entropy by taking in information", by contrast, is a sentence with no operational content until someone names the random variables and the channel. The test is simple and unforgiving: if you cannot say what the alphabet is and what the distribution is over, no theorem in this syllabus applies.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why Maxwell's demon fails, naming erasure rather than measurement as the step that pays — and state the numerical cost of forgetting one bit at room temperature.

M20

Quantum information: where the classical picture breaks

≈10 h

This module sits last in the technical arc rather than beside M08 because it only teaches by contrast: every result here is the deliberate mirror of a classical theorem you have already proved, and the interesting content is in what fails to carry over. Some of it carries over almost verbatim — Schumacher compression is the source coding theorem with a typical subspace. Some of it does not carry over at all: quantum capacity is not additive, and two useless channels can combine into a useful one, which has no classical analogue whatsoever. This is not classical information theory with new symbols.

Concepts

  • The state space changes — density matrices ρ instead of distributions, and von Neumann entropy S(ρ) = −Tr ρ log ρ
    • S(ρ) is the Shannon entropy of ρ's eigenvalues, so for commuting states everything reduces to M02. Non-commutativity is the entire source of novelty
    • There is no joint distribution over X and Y to condition on, only a joint state ρAB. Conditional entropy S(A|B) = S(AB) − S(B) is defined by that subtraction and can be negative — for a maximally entangled pair, S(A|B) = −1
    • What the negativity buys: in state merging, negative conditional entropy means the transfer costs no communication and generates entanglement. The strange sign has an operational cash value
    • Quantum relative entropy and its monotonicity under channels — the DPI from M03, surviving intact and doing the same job
    • Strong subadditivity (Lieb–Ruskai) as the hard theorem that classical entropy gets for free from the chain rule
  • Schumacher compression — the quantum source coding theorem, and the qubit as the unit of quantum information
    • The typical subspace replaces M05's typical set: project onto the span of the high-probability eigenvectors, of dimension ≈ 2nS(ρ)
    • The proof structure is the classical one; the extra work is showing the projection barely disturbs the state, which has no classical counterpart because classical measurement is free
  • The Holevo bound — accessible information about a classical variable encoded in quantum states is at most χ, and χ ≤ log d
    • Consequence: n qubits carry at most n classical bits, despite a qubit having continuously many parameters. This is the module's single most misused result and the one to state precisely
    • Superdense coding does not violate it: two bits in one qubit requires a pre-shared entangled pair, so two qubits were used in total
  • Channel capacities, plural — where the classical single-number story fragments
    • HSW theorem: the classical capacity of a quantum channel, given by a regularised Holevo quantity. Regularisation means the formula is not computable in general, and Hastings showed in 2009 that the Holevo quantity is genuinely non-additive, so the regularisation cannot be dropped
    • Quantum capacity Q, given by the regularised coherent information, and its non-additivity is worse
    • Superactivation (Smith and Yard, 2008): two channels each with Q = 0 can have positive quantum capacity when used together. There is no classical analogue — classically, zero capacity plus zero capacity is zero capacity — and it means a channel's quantum capacity is not a property you can evaluate in isolation
    • Entanglement-assisted classical capacity (Bennett, Shor, Smolin, Thapliyal) is the one capacity with a clean single-letter formula, given by the quantum mutual information. Free entanglement makes the theory behave classically, which is itself the lesson
  • Entanglement as a currency, and the resource identities
    • No-cloning as the structural constraint behind most of the differences — you cannot repeat a quantum message, so M11's repetition intuition is not merely inefficient but forbidden
    • Teleportation (1 ebit + 2 classical bits → 1 qubit) and superdense coding (1 ebit + 1 qubit → 2 classical bits) as exact conversion rates between resources
  • Quantum error correction — the machinery of M11, lifted
    • Shor's nine-qubit code: the first demonstration that error correction is possible despite no-cloning and despite errors being continuous, by discretising them through measurement
    • Stabiliser codes as the direct analogue of M11's parity-check formalism, and CSS codes (Calderbank–Shor–Steane) as the construction that takes two classical linear codes and produces a quantum one
    • Surface codes: local check operators on a 2D lattice, a threshold around 1% error per gate, and the enormous qubit overhead that follows — this is what every fault-tolerance roadmap is costed against
    • The threshold theorem: below a constant physical error rate, arbitrary-length computation is possible with polylogarithmic overhead
  • Quantum key distribution — BB84, and the connection back to M15
    • Security rests on no-cloning and measurement disturbance rather than on computational hardness, so it is information-theoretic security in M15's exact sense
    • Post-processing is M15's two-stage recipe verbatim: information reconciliation (Slepian–Wolf) then privacy amplification (universal hashing)
    • What the security proof does not cover: the devices. Photon-number-splitting, detector blinding and side channels have broken deployed systems whose protocol was provably secure, which is why device-independent QKD exists

Practice

In QuTiP or equivalent, construct a two-qubit Bell state and compute S(A), S(B), S(AB) and S(A|B). Then build the [[9,1,3]] Shor code, apply each of X, Y and Z errors to each physical qubit in turn, measure the stabiliser syndrome, and recover. Done when S(A|B) = −1 for the Bell state and you can explain that number in words, and when all 27 single-qubit error patterns are corrected with the right syndrome each time.

Where people go wrong

"Quantum channels carry more information." For classical payload they do not — that is exactly what the Holevo bound says, and n qubits deliver at most n classical bits. What changes is which tasks are possible (key distribution with unconditional security, teleportation, transmitting quantum states at all) and how capacities compose. The second habitual error is transporting classical intuitions about capacity: superactivation means the quantum capacity of a channel is not a fixed property you can measure once and then add up, and there is no classical situation that prepares you for that.

M21

The frontier: open problems and live disagreements

≈5 h

A syllabus that ends at settled results leaves you thinking the field is finished. It is not, and knowing which questions are open is what lets you read a current paper and understand why it exists. This module is deliberately a map rather than a treatment: each item is something you could start reading about tomorrow, and several have been open longer than most people reading this have been alive.

Concepts

  • Zero-error information theory — Shannon's 1956 question of what rate is achievable with exactly zero error, which is a combinatorics problem in disguise
    • The Shannon capacity of a graph, still unknown for the 7-cycle. Lovász's theta function settled the 5-cycle in 1979 and the general problem is wide open
    • This is where information theory and extremal combinatorics meet, and the second edition of Csiszár–Körner added a chapter on it for that reason
  • The multi-user gaps from M14, restated as a research programme — general broadcast, interference and relay capacities, and the structural result that single-letter characterisations do not always exist
    • What "progress" now looks like: constant-gap results, degrees-of-freedom characterisations, and approximate capacity regions
  • Feedback capacity — known not to help for memoryless channels, and largely unknown for channels with memory
    • Recent dynamic-programming and reinforcement-learning approaches to computing feedback capacity are an active and genuinely new line
  • Finite blocklength beyond the point-to-point case — dispersion for multi-user channels is mostly unknown, and this is the gap between M10 and M14
  • Information theory of learning systems
    • Scaling laws read as rate–distortion curves, and whether the compression view predicts anything the empirical curves do not
    • Tokenisation as a source-coding decision with downstream consequences nobody can currently price
    • Watermarking of generated text as a covert-channel problem, with the detectability/quality tradeoff being an information-theoretic one
    • Communication-constrained distributed learning, where the lower bounds are the strong-DPI results from M16 and the achievability side is wide open
  • Semantic and goal-oriented communication — the 6G-era proposal to reintroduce the meaning Shannon deliberately excluded in M01
    • Take it seriously and sceptically at once: there is a real question (if the receiver only needs to act, why transmit the whole message?) and a large amount of writing that has not yet produced an operational theorem
    • The honest version is closer to rate–distortion with a task-specific distortion measure, which is a reframing rather than a new theory — and reframings sometimes turn out to matter
  • The quantum open problems from M20 — additivity, the exact quantum capacity of the depolarising channel, and whether superactivation happens for classical capacity too
  • How to keep up — the arXiv cs.IT feed, ISIT and ITA as the two meetings, and the observation that the field's centre of gravity has moved toward statistics and learning over the last fifteen years

Practice

Pick one open problem from this module. Find the three most-cited papers on it from the last five years, read their introductions, and write a one-page account of what is known, what the best current bounds are, and what the obstruction is. Done when your page names a specific technical barrier — not "it is hard" but "the standard converse gives a regularised expression that provably cannot be single-letterised".

Where people go wrong

Assuming that because the two coding theorems are settled, the field is a closed subject that only gets applied. Half the results in this syllabus postdate 1990 — polar codes are from 2009, finite-blocklength from 2010, superactivation from 2008, the minimax entropy estimators from 2015, and the CMI generalisation framework from the last few years. The other error is the opposite one: treating every new "information-theoretic" framing in an adjacent field as progress. The test is the one that has applied since M01 — does it produce an operational theorem, a statement that some rate is or is not achievable? If not, it is vocabulary.

Capstone

Two projects. The first is the one everybody should do, because it forces the compression half of the syllabus to become concrete; the second is for readers who came for channels rather than sources.

  • Project A — a compressor you can defend. Build a lossless compressor for a fixed text corpus, from the model outward: an adaptive context model of your choosing feeding an arithmetic or ANS coder you wrote yourself. Then measure it honestly — against the empirical entropy rate at several orders, against gzip, xz and zstd, and against the file's compressed size under a small neural language model driving the same coder.
  • Scope: the coder must be yours, the model may be anything, and the report must state bits per character for every configuration plus the model's own size in bits.
  • Draws on: M02, M05, M06, M07, M18
  • Done when: your compressor beats gzip on the corpus, you can explain every gap between your result and xz in terms of modelling rather than coding, and you can state which of your comparisons are honest and which quietly omit the cost of the model.
  • Stretch: enter the corpus into the Hutter Prize framing — compressed size including the decompressor — and report where you land relative to the current record.
  • Project B — a link budget from first principles. Take a real short-packet scenario: 64-byte payloads, a BSC or AWGN channel at a stated SNR, and a target block error rate of 10−5. Compute the capacity, then the finite-blocklength achievable rate, then implement an actual code — an LDPC code with sum–product decoding, or a CRC-aided polar code with SC-list decoding — and measure where it lands.
  • Scope: one channel, one code family, three numbers (capacity, R*(n, ε), measured rate) and an accounting of the gaps between them.
  • Draws on: M03, M08, M09, M10, M12
  • Done when: you can attribute the total gap from capacity to two separate causes — the finite-blocklength penalty, which no code can avoid, and your decoder's suboptimality, which is yours — and give a number for each.
  • Stretch: repeat with the other code family and explain the crossover, which is the M12 checkpoint made quantitative.

Mastery check

  • You can derive H from Shannon's axioms and exhibit a function that satisfies two of the three but not the grouping property.
  • You can prove Fano's inequality and then use it twice — once for a channel converse, once for a minimax statistical lower bound — and say why it is the same argument both times.
  • You can explain why differential entropy is not the continuous limit of entropy, and why mutual information is unaffected by the same problem.
  • You can reconstruct the random-coding achievability proof from memory and name the step that fails for a channel with memory.
  • Given a channel and a blocklength, you can say roughly how far below capacity a real system should be expected to operate, and why capacity alone would have misled you.
  • You have written an arithmetic coder that hits within 0.01 bits/symbol of entropy on a skewed binary source, and you can say why Huffman cannot.
  • You can state what "capacity-achieving" means for a code family, including the decoder and the limit it is taken in, and name a code that is capacity-achieving in theory and unremarkable at n = 512.
  • You can set up a rate–distortion problem and its dual channel-capacity problem side by side and identify the correspondence term by term.
  • You can name two multi-user capacity regions that have been open for over forty years and say what specifically obstructs a solution.
  • Given a paper reporting a mutual information estimate, you can identify the estimator, predict the sign of its bias, and say whether the sample size supports the claim.
  • You can explain the Saxe et al. objection to the information bottleneck story precisely enough to say which of the three original claims fails for which reason.
  • You can state three classical theorems whose quantum analogue differs, and identify the failing proof step in each — with superactivation as one of them.

Where to go next

  • Erasure coding in distributed storage and data availability — the Reed–Solomon material in M11 is the load-bearing mechanism behind erasure-coded object stores and behind data-availability sampling in permissionless systems, where the same construction is read as a cryptographic commitment rather than as a storage code. The Blockchain Systems page develops that reading directly, and the data-availability-sampling papers it cites are the continuation.
  • Quantum computation — M20 covered the information-theoretic half and deliberately stopped. Shor's and Grover's algorithms, circuit synthesis, and the engineering of fault tolerance answer a different question: not what can be communicated, but what can be computed. Nielsen and Chuang is the same book for both halves.
  • Applied cryptography — M15 established what information-theoretic security costs and why almost nothing uses it. Everything that ships is computational instead, and the security definitions, reductions and proof techniques there are a separate discipline that begins where this module ends.
  • Statistical learning theory — M16 and M17 give the information-theoretic route to generalisation bounds. VC dimension, Rademacher complexity and the uniform-convergence tradition arrive at overlapping results by a different road, and reading them against each other is the fastest way to see what each framework can and cannot express. The Classical Machine Learning page develops that second route in full, along with the model-selection machinery M17's bounds are supposed to justify.
  • Deep learning, where these quantities are used and misused — M17 treats the information bottleneck as a cautionary case; the Deep Learning page is where the models it was proposed to explain actually live, including the generalisation puzzle that makes every information-theoretic explanation of deep networks contested. Its scaling-law material is the empirical counterpart to M07's compression-is-prediction equivalence.
  • Generative models as rate–distortion machines — M17 shows the β-VAE objective is a rate–distortion Lagrangian with β playing the role of M13's λ. The generative-modelling page follows that identification forward through latent diffusion, where the autoencoder's rate budget determines what the generator can ever produce, and through the evaluation problem, which is a distortion measure nobody can write down.
  • Digital communications and signal processing — the layer this syllabus sits on top of. Modulation, synchronisation, equalisation and OFDM decide how a bit becomes a waveform, which is the part M09 abstracts into a single Gaussian.
  • Stochastic thermodynamics — M19's Landauer material is the entry point to a live field: fluctuation theorems, information engines, and the thermodynamic cost of computation and of inference.

This is where the paper's construction becomes a table and a loop, and where the practical constraints — state size, table construction, symbol ordering — get discussed. Follow the blog's subsequent ANS posts in order.

  3. R-85 Data Compression Explained — Matt Mahoney, updated 2013

    A book-length free text on compression written by someone who competes on benchmarks, which makes it unusually honest about what actually wins: context mixing, not clever coders. Reference & documentation

Things to consult rather than read. R-89 and R-90 are the two places where this syllabus's theory appears as a normative specification.

  1. R-88 IEEE Transactions on Information Theory — IEEE Information Theory Society, monthly since 1953

    The field's journal of record; almost every paper in this list appeared here or in its predecessor. Use it as an exercise rather than a subscription — take one recent table of contents and classify every paper by which of entropy, divergence or mutual information it is really about, which is a fast way to see the field's current shape.

    Paid Advanced Reference
    Used in M21
  2. R-89 3GPP TS 38.212: NR — Multiplexing and channel coding — 3rd Generation Partnership Project, ongoing releases

    The 5G channel-coding specification: LDPC base graphs for the data channel, CRC-aided polar coding for control, and the rate-matching machinery around both. Read sections 5.3.1–5.4 to see how little of a deployed coding scheme is the code itself. This is M12's theory as a normative document.

    Free online Advanced Reference
    Used in M12
  3. R-90 RFC 8478: Zstandard Compression and the application/zstd Media Type — Yann Collet & Murray Kucherawy, IETF, 2018

    The zstd format specification, including the FSE (tANS) entropy-coding stage in normative detail — the bit-level layout, the table-construction procedure, and the Huffman fallback. This is the shortest path from R-34's mathematics to a byte format you can implement against.

    Free online Intermediate Reference
    Used in M06
  4. R-91 arXiv cs.IT / math.IT: Information Theory — arXiv, daily

    Where the field publishes first. Skim the new-submissions listing weekly for a month and you will learn the current distribution of topics faster than from any survey — and you will notice how much of it is now statistics and learning rather than channels.

Tools, datasets & playgrounds

R-93 and R-97/R-98 are the ones you will actually run. The corpora exist so that your compression numbers are comparable to somebody else's.

  1. R-93 zstd — Yann Collet and contributors, Meta, ongoing

    Production source for a modern compressor, and the reference tANS implementation in the lib/common/fse* and lib/compress/ files. Read the FSE code against R-84 and R-90 — three views of the same algorithm at three levels of abstraction is the fastest way to actually understand it.

    Free online Intermediate Hands-on
    Used in M06
  2. R-94 Large Text Compression Benchmark — Matt Mahoney, ongoing since 2006

    Around 200 compressors ranked on the first 109 bytes of English Wikipedia, with sizes reported including the decompressor. That last detail is the whole point: it is the only widely used benchmark that closes the loophole M07 warns about, where model size is quietly excluded.

    Free online Intermediate Live data
    Used in M07
  3. R-95 Silesia compression corpus — Sebastian Deorowicz, Silesian University of Technology, 2003

    The modern standard corpus: twelve files of realistic size and variety (databases, medical images, XML, executables) chosen because the older corpora had become too small and too easy. Use it so your bits-per-character numbers in M02 mean the same thing as everyone else's.

    Free online Beginner ~200 MB
    Used in M02
  4. R-96 The Canterbury Corpus — Ross Arnold & Tim Bell, University of Canterbury, 1997

    The classic small benchmark, and still the one most historical results are quoted against — which is why it is here despite being superseded by R-95 for serious measurement. Its files are small enough to inspect by hand, which makes it the right corpus for debugging a coder rather than benchmarking one.

    Free online Beginner ~3 MB
    Used in M07
  5. R-97 dit: discrete information theory — Ryan G. James and contributors, ongoing

    A Python package covering far more discrete measures than the usual entropy-and-MI pair — common informations, partial information decomposition, and the multivariate generalisations that other libraries omit. Use it for the discrete side of M18, and read its documentation on why there are several inequivalent multivariate mutual informations.

    Free online Intermediate Hands-on
    Used in M18
  6. R-98 NPEET: Non-parametric Entropy Estimation Toolbox — Greg Ver Steeg, ongoing

    The reference KSG implementation for continuous and mixed variables, in a few hundred lines of NumPy and SciPy — short enough to read entirely, which you should, because M18's failure modes are visible in the code. It also ships the permutation-based significance test that M18's trap says you must run.

    Free online Intermediate Hands-on
    Used in M18
  7. R-99 AFF3CT: A Fast Forward Error Correction Toolbox — IMS Laboratory, University of Bordeaux, ongoing

    Simulation and implementation of LDPC, polar, turbo and RS codes with real decoders, fast enough to produce publication-quality BER curves. Use it to check the M12 practice against a reference implementation before concluding your own decoder is broken — which, the first time, it will be.

    Free online Advanced Hands-on
    Used in M12
  8. R-100 CompressAI — InterDigital, ongoing

    PyTorch implementations of the main learned-compression architectures with pretrained models and rate–distortion evaluation built in.     Density matrices, partial traces, von Neumann entropy and channel simulation with a sane API, which is all M20's practice needs. Being able to type out a Bell state and read off S(A|B) = −1 makes the negativity concrete in a way no amount of reading does.

Practice: problem sets & project ideas

Information theory is a proof subject, and reading proofs is not the same as producing them. Do R-102 or R-104 in full — one graded problem set beats three re-reads.

  1. R-102 MIT 6.441 problem sets — Yury Polyanskiy, MIT OpenCourseWare, 2016

    Graduate problem sets with solutions, matched to R-17's notes. The channel-coding and converse problems are the ones that reveal whether you can actually use Fano rather than recite it. Attempt each before opening the solution; the gap between recognising a proof and constructing it is the whole point.

    Free online Advanced Hands-on
    Used in M08
  2. R-103 Stanford EE376A homework — Tsachy Weissman, Stanford University

    Gentler than R-102 and better as a first set — more computation, fewer proofs, and several problems that ask you to simulate rather than derive. The typicality problems are the right accompaniment to M05.

    Free online Beginner → Intermediate Hands-on
    Used in M05
  3. R-104 Information Theory, Pattern Recognition and Neural Networks: course page — David J. C. MacKay, University of Cambridge

    The course built around R-02, with past exam papers, worked examples and the exercise recommendations that tell you which of the book's several hundred problems are the load-bearing ones. The maximum-entropy and inference exercises are the best available practice for M04.

    Free online Intermediate Hands-on
    Used in M04
  4. R-105 The Hutter Prize for Lossless Compression of Human Knowledge — Marcus Hutter, ongoing since 2006

    A standing prize for compressing 1 GB of Wikipedia, scored on the total size of the compressed file plus the decompressor. Its stated premise — that compression and intelligence are the same problem — is exactly M07's thesis, and its scoring rule is the honest accounting that most language-model compression claims omit. A capstone-scale project.
