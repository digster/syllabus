Generative AI: Large Language Models

A language model predicts the next token. Everything else — instruction following, reasoning, tool use, the entire industry — is what happens when you scale that objective, then spend a second training budget teaching the result to be useful. This syllabus covers the full pipeline: tokenisation through pretraining data and distributed training, then evaluation, post-training from RLHF to GRPO, reasoning with verifiable rewards, and the inference stack that decides whether any of it is affordable. It is deliberately explicit about which parts are settled and which changed in the last eighteen months.

What this topic is

A large language model is a transformer trained to predict the next token in a very large corpus, and then trained again — on a much smaller budget and with entirely different machinery — to behave the way a user wants. Those two phases are so different that treating them as one subject is the main obstacle to understanding the field. Pretraining is a systems problem: thousands of accelerators, a data pipeline whose curation decisions matter more than most architectural ones, and a loss curve you can predict in advance from scaling laws. Post-training is an optimisation problem against a metric you cannot measure reliably, which is why it has churned through four dominant algorithms in five years and why evaluation, not modelling, is the field's hardest open problem.

The third phase is inference, and it is where the economics live. A model's parameter count determines its serving cost per token, so the deployment question — which is almost never the same as the benchmark question — is decided by KV cache size, batching behaviour and quantisation rather than by loss. This syllabus follows that structure: what the objective is, how the model is built, how it is trained, how it is judged, how it is made useful, and how it is served. It is written to remain useful as specific models are superseded, so it names mechanisms and results rather than the current leaderboard.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes the transformer. Specifically, it assumes you can write multi-head causal attention from memory, know what the residual stream is, understand backpropagation as reverse-mode autodiff, and have seen distributed training — all of which are on this library's Deep Learning page, modules M03 through M12. It also assumes fluent Python and enough probability to be comfortable with cross-entropy and sampling.

  • The transformer architecture — if RoPE, pre-norm and grouped-query attention are unfamiliar, do Deep Learning M09 first; this page treats them as vocabulary. Neural Networks: Zero to Hero is the fastest route if you want one resource.
  • Distributed training and the memory arithmetic — M06 here assumes it. Deep Learning M11 covers it properly.
  • Reinforcement learning basics, needed from M09 — policy gradients and PPO specifically. Spinning Up in Deep RL is enough, and the MDP framing is on the Classical Artificial Intelligence page.
  • Honest evaluation practice — M07's problems are the classical page's leakage problems at a scale where the test set may be inside the training corpus. Classical Machine Learning M06 is the grounding.

Not covered here: the transformer architecture itself, which is taught on the deep-learning page rather than repeated — M03 covers the decisions that differentiate modern LLMs and assumes the mechanism. Diffusion, GANs, VAEs and generative modelling of images, audio and video are on Generative AI Beyond Language; the one exception is discrete diffusion for text, which M22 names as a live alternative to autoregression and which that page develops. Multimodal models appear only where they change the language stack — this page does not teach vision encoders. Application-layer frameworks and orchestration libraries are deliberately absent: they change every six months and the mechanisms underneath them do not.

M01

What a language model is, and why next-token prediction was enough

≈4 h

The objective is one line — maximise the log probability of the next token — and everything in this syllabus is downstream of it. The interesting question is why an objective that simple produces the behaviours it does, and the answer connects to compression in a way that is worth installing early because it recurs in M10's reasoning material and M19's hallucination material.

Concepts

  • The objective and its measurement
    • Autoregressive factorisation of a sequence's joint probability; cross-entropy loss, and perplexity as its exponential — a per-token branching factor, comparable only across models with the same tokeniser, which is a caveat routinely ignored in comparisons
    • Bits per byte as the tokeniser-independent alternative, and why serious comparisons use it
    • Teacher forcing during training versus free-running generation at inference, and the exposure bias that creates
  • The lineage, briefly, because it explains the vocabulary
    • n-gram models with smoothing, and the sparsity wall that motivated neural language models
    • Word embeddings and the distributional hypothesis; the shift from static to contextual representations
    • Encoder-only (BERT) versus decoder-only (GPT) and why decoder-only won for general-purpose systems — one objective, one architecture, and generation for free
  • Prediction and compression as the same thing
    • A model assigning probability p to a token can encode it in −log₂ p bits; a better predictor is literally a better compressor, and cross-entropy loss is a codelength
    • Why this matters here: it makes "the model is just predicting text" a statement about mechanism rather than a dismissal, since compressing a corpus well requires modelling whatever regularities produced it
    • The honest limit of the argument — it says nothing about whether the model's internal representations resemble understanding, which is M17's question
  • What scale changed, stated carefully — the shift from task-specific fine-tuning to a single model prompted at inference time, and the observation that few-shot capability emerged from scale without being trained for

Work through

  • Language Models are Few-Shot Learners — the GPT-3 paper. Read sections 1–3 for the in-context-learning framing that reorganised the field.
  • Speech and Language Processing — Chapters 3 and 7, for n-gram models and the neural lineage in a proper textbook treatment.
  • CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — lecture 1, for the course's framing of what building one of these actually involves.
  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — the bigram and MLP language-model videos, which build the objective from nothing.
  • Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT — Karpathy's three-hour overview of this whole syllabus; watch it before starting, not after.
  • Understanding Deep Learning — Chapter 12, if the transformer prerequisite needs refreshing before M03.

Practice

Train a character-level bigram model, an n-gram model with backoff, and a small transformer on the same corpus. Report perplexity and bits-per-byte for each, and then use each as the model in an arithmetic coder to actually compress the corpus. You are done when your compressed sizes rank in the same order as your losses and you can state the exact relationship between the two numbers.

Where people go wrong

Comparing perplexity across models with different tokenisers. Perplexity is per token, and a model with a larger vocabulary uses fewer tokens for the same text, so its perplexity is not comparable to a model with a smaller one — the difference can easily exceed the difference the comparison was meant to detect. Bits per byte normalises this away, and any comparison that does not use it or explicitly share a tokeniser is not measuring what it claims.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the relationship between cross-entropy loss and compressed file size precisely, and explain why two models' perplexities can be misleading to compare while their bits-per-byte figures are not.

M02

Tokenisation, and the failures it causes

≈6 h

Tokenisation is the least glamorous component and the source of a disproportionate share of a model's observable defects — arithmetic errors, character-counting failures, and multilingual cost asymmetries all originate here rather than in the model. It comes second because every later module measures things per token, and you cannot reason about context cost or serving economics without knowing what a token is.

Concepts

  • Why subword units at all — the tradeoff between characters (long sequences, no out-of-vocabulary problem) and words (short sequences, unbounded vocabulary, poor morphology)
    • Byte-pair encoding: start from bytes, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, stop at the target vocabulary size. The merge list is the tokeniser
    • Byte-level BPE, which guarantees no unknown tokens by operating on bytes rather than Unicode characters — and the consequence that a rare script costs several tokens per character
    • WordPiece and Unigram/SentencePiece as the alternatives, and where each is still used
    • Pre-tokenisation regexes, which decide what can ever be merged — this is where digit grouping and whitespace handling get fixed
  • The pathologies, each traced to a specific decision
    • Arithmetic: inconsistent digit grouping means 1234 may be one token and 1235 three, so the model cannot see place value. Right-to-left digit chunking and single-digit tokenisation are the fixes now common in models that are good at arithmetic
    • Character-level tasks: counting letters in a word is hard because the model never sees the letters, only an opaque id
    • Multilingual inequity: identical meaning costs several times more tokens in some languages than in English, which is a direct cost and context-length penalty on those users
    • Code: indentation and common identifiers depend heavily on whether the training mixture included code
    • Glitch tokens — strings present in the tokeniser's training data but effectively absent from the model's, producing bizarre behaviour when they appear
    • Prompt-boundary effects, where a trailing space changes the tokenisation of the next word and degrades the completion
  • Practical consequences that recur later — vocabulary size trading embedding parameters against sequence length, tokeniser choice being effectively frozen after pretraining, and vocabulary extension for a new domain requiring embedding surgery
  • The tokeniser-free direction — byte-level models with learned patching, and why they remain a research direction rather than a default

Work through

  • Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units — the BPE paper, where subword tokenisation enters NLP.
  • Neural Networks: Zero to Hero — the "Let's build the GPT tokenizer" video, which implements BPE from scratch and demonstrates each pathology live.
  • CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — the tokenisation lecture and its assignment, which is the most thorough treatment available.
  • tiktokenizer — paste real text and watch the boundaries; ten minutes here is worth an hour of reading.
  • tiktoken — the library, for measuring token counts across languages and for the encoding definitions.
  • Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) — Chapter 2, which implements the tokeniser and data loader in PyTorch alongside the explanation.
  • StatQuest — the tokenisation and embedding videos, for a slower visual pass.

Practice

Implement BPE training and encoding from scratch and train it on a mixed corpus of English, code and one non-Latin-script language. Then measure: tokens per character for each language, how the numbers 0–2000 tokenise, and how many tokens the same paragraph costs in each language. You are done when you can point at a specific merge rule that causes an arithmetic failure and predict which numbers will be affected.

Where people go wrong

Attributing tokenisation failures to reasoning. "How many r's in strawberry" and unreliable multi-digit arithmetic are usually not reasoning failures — the model cannot see the characters, and its digit groupings are inconsistent across numbers of the same magnitude. The diagnostic is to re-run the task with the input spaced out into individual characters or digits; if performance jumps, it was the tokeniser. Conflating the two leads to entirely wasted prompt engineering.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: predict from a tokeniser's merge rules which arithmetic operations it will make hard, and explain why a user writing in a non-Latin script pays several times more per message and gets less usable context.

M03

The architecture decisions in a modern LLM

≈7 h

The deep-learning page taught the transformer as published in 2017. Almost nothing in a current open-weights model matches that paper, and every difference is a decision someone made for a stated reason. This module is that list — each change, the alternative it replaced, and the evidence. It is the module that lets you read a new model's technical report and know which choices are conventional and which are the actual contribution.

Concepts

  • Position: absolute learned → RoPE
    • Rotary embeddings apply a position-dependent rotation to queries and keys so the attention score depends on relative position by construction, with no added parameters
    • Why it matters beyond elegance: it is what makes context extension possible at all, since the rotation frequencies can be rescaled after training — M12's material
    • ALiBi and NoPE as the alternatives, and where each is still used
  • Normalisation: LayerNorm → RMSNorm, post-norm → pre-norm
    • RMSNorm drops the mean subtraction and the bias; measurably cheaper, empirically no worse, and now near-universal
    • Pre-norm for training stability at depth; QK-norm as the more recent addition that stabilises attention logits in very large runs
  • The feed-forward block: ReLU → GELU → SwiGLU
    • Gated linear units with a Swish activation, using three matrices instead of two, with the hidden dimension reduced to keep the parameter count constant
    • The honest note that the original paper attributed the gain to "divine benevolence" — this is an empirical result, not a derived one
  • Attention: multi-head → multi-query → grouped-query
    • The KV cache is the reason: sharing key and value heads across query heads shrinks it by the sharing factor, which directly determines how many concurrent requests fit on a device
    • MQA's quality cost and GQA as the compromise that recovers most of it; multi-head latent attention as the more aggressive compression
    • This is the clearest case on the page of an inference constraint dictating a training-time architecture choice
  • Sparsity: dense → mixture of experts
    • Total parameters versus active parameters per token, and why the two numbers must both be quoted for a model description to be meaningful
    • Routing, load balancing, expert capacity, and shared always-on experts; the memory cost of holding all experts regardless of activation
  • The remaining details that matter — bias terms removed almost everywhere, weight tying between input and output embeddings, and depth-versus-width choices at fixed parameter count

Work through

  • RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding — RoPE in the original; the rotation construction is the part to follow.
  • GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models from Multi-Head Checkpoints — grouped-query attention, including the uptraining recipe for converting an existing model.
  • GLU Variants Improve Transformer — Shazeer's SwiGLU note, which is two pages and refreshingly honest about the absence of a derivation.
  • CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — the architecture lecture, which surveys exactly this list with the evidence for each.
  • The Llama 3 Herd of Models — a full technical report; read the architecture and data sections as a worked example of what these decisions look like in one system.
  • The Illustrated Transformer — re-read it here with the modern variants in mind; the diagrams still map onto the current block.
  • CS25: Transformers United — talks by the authors of several of these decisions, explaining why they were made.
  • Hugging Face Transformers documentation — open a modern model's modelling source and identify each decision in the code.

Practice

Take your transformer from the deep-learning page and modernise it one change at a time: learned positions to RoPE, LayerNorm to RMSNorm, post-norm to pre-norm, ReLU to SwiGLU, multi-head to grouped-query. Train to a fixed token budget after each change and record loss, throughput and KV cache size. You are done when you have a five-row table and can say which changes bought quality, which bought speed, and which bought neither at your scale.

Where people go wrong

Assuming every deviation from the 2017 paper improves quality. Several of the most universal changes — grouped-query attention especially — are quality-neutral-to-slightly-negative and were adopted because they shrink the KV cache and therefore the serving cost. Reading them as accuracy improvements makes the design space incoherent. Ask of every architectural choice which of three things it optimises: loss at fixed compute, training throughput, or inference cost.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: open an open-weights model's config file, name every architectural decision it encodes, and say for each whether it was made for quality, for training speed or for serving cost.

M04

Pretraining data

≈6 h

Data quality is the single largest lever on a pretrained model's capability, and it is the part of the pipeline that published work describes least. This module covers the pipeline as it is actually built, because the difference between a good model and a mediocre one at the same scale is mostly here — and because M07's evaluation problems all originate in decisions made at this stage.

Concepts

  • The pipeline, stage by stage, with the attrition at each
    • Acquisition: Common Crawl and its structure, plus curated sources — code repositories, books, papers, reference works
    • Text extraction from HTML, which is a larger quality lever than it sounds; boilerplate removal
    • Language identification and filtering, with the accuracy problems on short and code-switched text
    • Quality filtering: heuristic rules (length, symbol ratios, repetition) versus classifier-based selection trained against a reference corpus, and the bias that second approach bakes in
    • Deduplication — exact, then fuzzy via MinHash/LSH — which removes a large fraction of the corpus and measurably improves models while reducing memorisation
    • Decontamination against known benchmarks, and why it is never complete
    • Toxicity and PII filtering, and the documented tradeoff where aggressive filtering disproportionately removes text about and by marginalised groups
  • Mixture, which is where judgement enters
    • Domain weights as a hyperparameter with real effects — code in the mixture improves reasoning benchmarks that contain no code, a robust and still-not-fully-explained finding
    • Curriculum and annealing: raising the proportion of high-quality data late in training, now standard
    • Multilingual proportions, and the transfer-versus-capacity tradeoff
  • Memorisation and contamination as one problem
    • Extraction attacks recovering verbatim training data; the relationship between duplication count and memorisation probability
    • Benchmark contamination as the reason M07 is hard: if the test set is in the corpus, the score measures retrieval
    • Legal and licensing questions as an active constraint on what can be used, with litigation ongoing in several jurisdictions
  • The data wall — high-quality text approaching exhaustion, repeated epochs and their measured decay, and synthetic data with the model-collapse caveat from the deep-learning page

Work through

  • The Pile: An 800GB Dataset of Diverse Text for Language Modeling — the first well-documented large pretraining corpus, with per-source analysis.
  • FineWeb: decanting the web for the finest text data at scale — the most transparent modern pipeline write-up, with ablations for every filtering decision.
  • Deduplicating Training Data Makes Language Models Better — the measured effect of dedup on both quality and memorisation.
  • Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models — the memorisation attack, which is what makes dedup a privacy measure as well as a quality one.
  • The Llama 3 Herd of Models — the data section, for how a frontier lab describes its pipeline.

Practice

Build a miniature pipeline on a Common Crawl sample: extract text, filter by language, apply three heuristic quality rules, then deduplicate with MinHash. Record the fraction removed at each stage. Then train identical small models on the raw and the processed corpora at equal token budgets and compare loss and downstream accuracy. You are done when you can state which single stage bought the most quality per token removed.

Where people go wrong

Treating deduplication as a storage optimisation. Its effects are on model behaviour: duplicated sequences are memorised at a rate that rises sharply with duplication count, so a document appearing a thousand times is likely to be reproducible verbatim — which is simultaneously a privacy exposure, a copyright exposure, and a waste of capacity. It also silently contaminates evaluation, because benchmark items circulate on the web in many copies.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe the pipeline end to end with rough attrition at each stage, and explain why aggressive toxicity filtering can reduce a model's usefulness for the groups it was meant to protect.

M05

Budgeting a training run

≈5 h

The deep-learning page established that scaling laws exist. This module is how they are used: converting a compute budget into a specific model size, token count, batch size and schedule, and knowing which of those decisions are determined and which are judgement. It is short, arithmetic, and the difference between a run that is planned and a run that is attempted.

Concepts

  • The arithmetic
    • C ≈ 6ND for a dense transformer — six FLOPs per parameter per token, two-thirds of which are the backward pass. Derive it rather than quoting it
    • The Chinchilla allocation, roughly 20 tokens per parameter at compute-optimal, and the three independent estimation methods that agree on it
    • Model FLOPs utilisation, and converting a FLOP budget into wall-clock time on real hardware — typically 30–50% of peak for a well-tuned large run
  • Why nobody trains compute-optimal any more
    • Inference cost scales with parameters and is paid on every request forever; training cost is paid once. For a model that will serve a lot of traffic, a smaller model trained far past compute-optimal is the correct economic choice
    • Overtraining ratios in current open models are often ten to fifty times the Chinchilla token count, and the loss penalty is small while the serving saving is large
    • The full-lifecycle framing: total cost is training plus expected inference, and the optimum depends on projected traffic — a decision that has nothing to do with the loss curve
  • The hyperparameters that must be set with the size
    • Batch size and the critical-batch-size notion; learning rate and its scaling with model width
    • Maximal-update parameterisation (μP) as the principled approach: tune on a small model and transfer the hyperparameters to a large one, which turns an unaffordable search into an affordable one
    • Schedule choices — warmup length, decay shape, and the increasingly common warmup–stable–decay schedule that permits mid-run branching
  • Fitting your own laws on a ladder of small models, which is the standard sanity check before committing a large budget, and the failure modes it catches

Work through

  • Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models — Chinchilla. Read all three estimation approaches; their agreement is the argument.
  • Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal: Accounting for Inference in Language Model Scaling Laws — the correction that includes serving cost, and the formalisation of why overtraining is rational.
  • Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer — μP, and the result that makes hyperparameter search affordable at scale.
  • CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — the scaling-laws lecture and its assignment, which has you fit a law on a real ladder.
  • CS336 assignments — the scaling-laws assignment, which has you fit a law on a real ladder rather than read about one.

Practice

Write a budgeting calculator: given a FLOP budget, produce compute-optimal N and D, then produce the alternative allocation that minimises total cost given a projected inference volume and a cost-per-FLOP for each phase. Plot the optimal parameter count against projected traffic. You are done when you can show the crossover point where overtraining a smaller model becomes the cheaper lifetime choice, and state it in tokens served.

Where people go wrong

Treating Chinchilla-optimal as the target. It minimises loss for a fixed training budget and says nothing about deployment — and for any model that will serve significant traffic, that is the wrong objective. Nearly every widely deployed open-weights model is deliberately overtrained relative to Chinchilla, and reading that as a mistake means misunderstanding what its makers were optimising.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive C ≈ 6ND from the forward and backward pass costs, compute compute-optimal N and D for a stated budget, and then argue for a different allocation using a projected serving volume.

M06

Pretraining systems, and what goes wrong at scale

≈6 h

The deep-learning page covered parallelism strategies in general. This module is what a specific multi-week language-model run involves — the configuration, the failure modes, and the operational discipline — because at this scale the difference between a finished run and an abandoned one is almost entirely operational. It is also the shortest path to understanding why frontier training is concentrated in a small number of organisations.

Concepts

  • Composing the parallelism for a language model specifically
    • The standard 3D arrangement — tensor parallel within a node where the interconnect is fast, pipeline parallel across nodes, data parallel with FSDP outermost — and the arithmetic that decides each degree
    • Sequence and context parallelism once contexts are long, since activation memory scales with sequence length
    • Expert parallelism for MoE, and the all-to-all communication that makes it the hardest configuration to run well
    • Why the ordering matters: the fastest-communicating dimension must carry the most communication
  • The data pipeline as a systems component — sharding, sequence packing to avoid wasted padding, document boundaries and attention masking, deterministic ordering for reproducibility, and resumability at exactly the right batch after a restart
  • Instability, which dominates the operational experience
    • Loss spikes: their correlation with specific data batches, with attention-logit growth, and with the optimiser's second-moment estimates; QK-norm and z-loss as the architectural mitigations now common
    • The standard playbook — skip the batch, lower the learning rate, or roll back to a checkpoint and reshuffle — and the fact that this is documented lore rather than theory
    • Divergence, silent NaNs, and the numerical-precision decisions behind them
  • Failure as an expected event
    • At thousands of accelerators for weeks, hardware failures happen regularly; mean time between failures becomes a design input
    • Checkpoint frequency as a tradeoff between storage/throughput cost and lost work; asynchronous checkpointing
    • Straggler detection and silent data corruption, which is rare per device and common per cluster-week
  • The instrumentation that makes a run debuggable — per-layer gradient norms, attention-logit maxima, activation statistics, throughput and MFU, and a held-out evaluation running continuously rather than at the end

Work through

  • The Ultra-Scale Playbook — the practical guide to composing these strategies, with measured throughput for each configuration.
  • The Llama 3 Herd of Models — the infrastructure section, which reports real failure rates and interruption causes over a production run. Unusually candid.
  • OPT: Open Pre-trained Transformer Language Models — read the accompanying logbook rather than the paper: a day-by-day record of a large run going wrong and being recovered. Nothing else is this honest.
  • How to Scale Your Model — for the roofline reasoning behind the configuration choices.
  • CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch — the systems lectures and the distributed-training assignment.
  • nanoGPT — its training script is the smallest complete example of the loop, checkpointing and resumption discussed here.

Practice

Train a small model with a deliberately induced loss spike — inject a batch of pathological data mid-run — and implement the three standard recoveries: skip the batch, reduce the learning rate, roll back and reshuffle. Measure the token cost of each. Separately, kill the job at a random step and verify that your restart resumes at exactly the right batch with the same data order. You are done when the restart is bit-identical to an uninterrupted run.

Where people go wrong

Building a data pipeline that cannot resume deterministically. It works fine until the first crash, at which point you either restart from the beginning, or resume with a different data order and lose the ability to reproduce or reason about anything that follows. At the scale where crashes are weekly, this is the difference between a run you can debug and a run you can only observe. Design resumability before the first large job, not after the first failure.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: specify a full parallelism configuration for a stated model size and cluster topology with a reason for each degree, and name three distinct causes of a loss spike with the mitigation for each.

M07

Evaluation, and why it is the hardest problem here

≈8 h

This module is placed before all of post-training deliberately. Every module from M08 onward optimises against a measurement, and a reader who has not first seen how comprehensively LLM measurement fails will read RLHF, DPO and RLVR as more reliable than they are. It is also the module with the most direct professional value: most published and internal claims about model quality are weaker than they appear, and the reasons are learnable.

Concepts

  • Why the classical setup does not transfer
    • Held-out data assumes the test set was not in training. At web scale that assumption is unverifiable and usually false — benchmark items, their solutions and their discussions are all on the web
    • Detecting contamination: n-gram overlap, canary strings, perplexity gaps between a benchmark and its paraphrase, and the finding that models often reproduce benchmark items verbatim when prompted with a prefix
    • Benchmark rot: a benchmark's usefulness decays from publication, and saturated benchmarks stop discriminating long before anyone stops quoting them
  • Static benchmarks and their pathologies
    • Multiple choice measured by comparing option likelihoods, and its sensitivity to option ordering, to letter labelling, and to prompt format — differences of several points from formatting alone
    • Exact-match scoring on generative tasks, which is what produces the discontinuous "emergence" curves from the deep-learning page
    • Holistic frameworks that report many metrics under standardised conditions, as the response to cherry-picking
  • LLM-as-judge, now the default for open-ended tasks
    • The known biases, each measurable: position bias (order of the two responses), verbosity bias (longer answers preferred), self-preference (a model rating its own outputs higher), and formatting bias
    • Mitigations: randomising position, calibrating against human ratings, using a judge from a different model family, and reporting agreement rates with humans rather than assuming them
    • The circularity risk when a judge is used both to train and to evaluate — which is exactly what M09's pipelines do
  • Human evaluation and arenas
    • Pairwise comparison with Elo or Bradley–Terry aggregation; the sample sizes needed for the differences being claimed
    • What arenas measure: preference under a particular user distribution, which rewards style and confidence and is not the same as correctness
    • Style control as the correction, and the finding that a substantial part of ranking movement was formatting
  • Building an evaluation that is actually informative for your task — start from failure cases rather than from benchmarks, keep a private held-out set, use pairwise comparison against the current production model, and measure inter-rater agreement before trusting any of it

Work through

  • Holistic Evaluation of Language Models — HELM, for the argument that a single number is never enough and for the standardised-conditions methodology.
  • Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena — the paper that established the practice and quantified its biases in the same document.
  • LMArena — the live leaderboard; read its methodology notes on style control rather than the rankings.
  • HELM — the running results, useful as an index of which benchmarks are still discriminating.
  • lm-evaluation-harness — the standard harness; read its documentation on prompt formatting, because that is where reproducibility is won or lost.
  • AI Engineering — the evaluation chapters, which are the most practical treatment in print of this module's problems.
  • ACL Anthology — where evaluation methodology gets the peer review the preprint firehose does not provide.

Practice

Measure the biases yourself. Take a benchmark and evaluate one model under six prompt formats — different option labels, different orderings, with and without a system prompt — and report the spread. Then build an LLM judge for an open-ended task and measure its position bias by presenting each pair in both orders, and its verbosity bias by padding responses without adding content. You are done when you can state the size of each bias in points, because that number is what tells you whether a reported improvement is real.

Where people go wrong

Trusting a benchmark improvement that is smaller than the format sensitivity. Reordering multiple-choice options or changing the prompt template moves scores by several points on many benchmarks, so a two-point gain measured under one fixed format establishes very little. Report the variation across formats, or use a benchmark whose harness fixes them — and be equally suspicious of your own results, since this is the most common way a real-looking internal improvement evaporates.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: design an evaluation for a specific task that controls for contamination, format sensitivity and judge bias — and say for each control what it costs and what it would have hidden.

M08

Supervised fine-tuning and parameter-efficient adaptation

≈6 h

A pretrained model completes text; it does not follow instructions, because nothing in the objective asked it to. Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations is what converts one into the other, and it does most of the work that people attribute to the reinforcement-learning stages that follow. This module also covers LoRA, which is the adaptation method almost everyone actually uses and which is frequently applied without knowing what it modifies.

Concepts

  • Instruction tuning
    • The format: prompt–response pairs with a chat template, loss masked to the response tokens only, which is a detail that silently ruins results when omitted
    • Chat templates and special tokens as the interface contract — a mismatch between training and serving templates is one of the most common causes of a fine-tune that mysteriously underperforms
    • The quality-over-quantity finding: a few thousand carefully curated examples can outperform hundreds of thousands of scraped ones, which supports the view that SFT is teaching format and style rather than knowledge
    • The superficial-alignment hypothesis, stated as a hypothesis: pretraining supplies the capability, alignment selects the sub-distribution. The evidence is suggestive and the claim is contested by the RLVR results in M10
  • Where the data comes from
    • Human demonstrations, expensive and highest-quality; synthetic generation from a stronger model, with the licensing questions that raises; self-instruct methods that bootstrap from a small seed set
    • Rejection sampling: generate many candidates, keep those a filter or verifier accepts, and fine-tune on the survivors — the bridge to M09 and M10, and the cheapest way to get most of their benefit
  • Parameter-efficient fine-tuning
    • LoRA: freeze the base weights and learn a low-rank update ΔW = BA with rank r ≪ d, adding a fraction of a percent of trainable parameters. Merge at inference so there is no latency cost
    • The choices that matter — rank, alpha scaling, and which modules are adapted; adapting attention projections only is the common default and adapting the feed-forward layers too is usually better when it matters
    • QLoRA: quantise the frozen base to 4 bits and train adapters in higher precision, which is what makes single-GPU fine-tuning of large models possible
    • When LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning: large distribution shifts and new capabilities rather than new formats — the low-rank constraint is a real capacity limit and rank is the knob
    • Serving many adapters against one base model, which is the deployment pattern that makes per-customer models economic
  • Catastrophic forgetting and its mitigations — mixing in pretraining data, lower learning rates, and the practical necessity of evaluating on capabilities you were not trying to change

Work through

  • Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback — the InstructGPT paper. Read the SFT stage carefully; the RL stage is M09's material and the paper is a useful demonstration of how much SFT alone achieved.
  • LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models — the method, and the intrinsic-rank argument behind it.
  • QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs — 4-bit base weights, paged optimisers and the NF4 datatype; the paper that made this accessible on consumer hardware.
  • LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment — the thousand-example result and the superficial-alignment hypothesis, read as a hypothesis with contested evidence.
  • TRL — the library's SFT trainer, and specifically its handling of chat templates and completion-only loss masking.
  • The Hugging Face LLM Course — the fine-tuning chapters, for the tooling path through this module.

Practice

Fine-tune a small base model on an instruction dataset three ways: full fine-tuning, LoRA on attention projections only, and LoRA on all linear layers — matching trainable-parameter counts where you can. Evaluate on held-out instructions and on a capability you did not train (say arithmetic or a knowledge benchmark). Then deliberately mismatch the chat template between training and inference and measure the damage. You are done when you can quantify both the LoRA-placement effect and the template-mismatch effect.

Where people go wrong

Computing the loss over the whole sequence rather than masking to the response. The model then spends much of its capacity learning to generate prompts, which is not the task, and the result is a fine-tune that is subtly worse than the base model at following instructions while looking fine on training loss. It is a one-line fix, it is the default in good libraries and not in hand-rolled loops, and it accounts for a large share of disappointing first fine-tunes.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state exactly which weight matrices a given LoRA configuration modifies and how many parameters that is, and describe a task where LoRA will underperform full fine-tuning with the reason expressed in terms of rank.

M09

Preference optimisation: RLHF, DPO, GRPO and what changed

≈8 h

This is the module where the field has moved fastest, and where most people's knowledge is one generation stale. The progression from RLHF through DPO to GRPO is not a sequence of refinements — each step removed a specific component that was expensive or unstable, and knowing which component and why is what lets you choose an algorithm rather than copy one. It also sets up M10, since GRPO is the machinery that reasoning training runs on.

Concepts

  • Classical RLHF, in its three stages
    • Collect pairwise preferences from human annotators; fit a reward model under the Bradley–Terry model; optimise the policy against it with PPO, plus a KL penalty to the reference model to stop it drifting
    • Four models in memory at once — policy, reference, reward, value — which is the memory cost that everything after this tries to reduce
    • Reward hacking as the central failure: the policy finds inputs where the reward model is wrong, and the KL penalty is the only thing restraining it. Length exploitation is the canonical instance and is measurable
    • Reward-model overoptimisation, with a measured relationship between KL distance and true-quality decline — the scaling law for how much optimisation a reward model can take before it hurts
  • Direct preference optimisation
    • The insight: the optimal policy under a KL-regularised reward objective has a closed form in terms of the reward, so the reward can be reparameterised in terms of the policy — turning the whole pipeline into a supervised classification loss on preference pairs
    • What that removes: the reward model, the value model, the sampling loop, and most of the instability. What it keeps: a frozen reference model
    • The known defects: length bias, sensitivity to the reference model, degradation when preference data is off-policy, and the fact that it optimises a proxy of a proxy
    • The successor family, each removing one more thing — SimPO removing the reference model with a length-normalised implicit reward, KTO working from binary thumbs-up/down signals rather than pairs, IPO addressing DPO's overfitting behaviour, ORPO folding preference into the SFT stage
  • GRPO, now the dominant on-policy method
    • Sample a group of responses per prompt, score them, and compute each one's advantage by normalising against the group's mean and standard deviation — which removes the value network entirely
    • Why that matters: the critic was roughly a quarter of the memory and a large share of the instability, and a group-relative baseline turns out to be sufficient
    • Variants that address its known issues — DAPO's decoupled clipping and dynamic sampling, token-level rather than sequence-level loss normalisation, and the removal of the KL term entirely in some reasoning recipes
    • The current consensus pipeline: SFT for format, then GRPO or a DPO variant for preference, then verifiable-reward training for reasoning where a verifier exists
  • Constitutional and AI-feedback methods — replacing human preference labels with model-generated critiques against a written set of principles, which is what makes preference data affordable at scale and which relocates rather than removes the human judgement

Work through

  • Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback — reread it here for the full three-stage pipeline and the ablations separating SFT's contribution from RL's.
  • Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model — the derivation is the content; follow it line by line rather than taking the loss on faith.
  • DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models — where GRPO is introduced, in section 4. The group-relative advantage is a two-line change with large consequences.
  • SimPO: Simple Preference Optimization with a Reference-Free Reward — for the length-normalised reward and the removal of the reference model.
  • KTO: Model Alignment as Prospect Theoretic Optimization — alignment from binary feedback, which is the practical answer when pairwise data is unaffordable.
  • Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback — the AI-feedback pipeline and the critique-and-revise loop.
  • A Little Bit of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — Lambert's book, which is the only book-length treatment of this material and is written by someone who has run these pipelines.
  • Spinning Up in Deep RL — the policy-gradient and PPO derivations, which the RLHF papers assume you already have.

Practice

Take an SFT model and align it three ways on the same preference dataset: DPO, SimPO, and GRPO with a simple programmatic reward. Track peak memory, wall-clock time, win rate against the SFT baseline judged by an LLM, and — critically — mean response length. You are done when you can show length inflation in at least one method and say which mechanism in that method permits it.

Where people go wrong

Reporting a win rate without controlling for length. Every method in this module can improve a judged win rate simply by producing longer answers, because both human annotators and LLM judges prefer length independently of content — and this accounts for a substantial share of reported alignment gains. Report mean length alongside win rate, or use a length-controlled metric. If your win rate rose and your responses got 40% longer, you have not yet shown anything.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name what each of RLHF, DPO and GRPO keeps in memory during training and what each one removed relative to its predecessor — and explain the DPO reparameterisation well enough to say why it needs a reference model and SimPO does not.

M10

Reasoning, test-time compute and verifiable rewards

≈7 h

The most consequential change in this field since instruction tuning is the discovery that a model can be trained to spend more computation at inference — to produce a long chain of intermediate tokens before answering — and that this can be learned from automatically checkable rewards with no human labels at all. This module covers that shift, and it comes after M09 because the mechanism is GRPO with a different reward source.

Concepts

  • Chain of thought, from prompt to trained behaviour
    • The original finding: prompting for step-by-step reasoning improves accuracy on multi-step tasks, and the effect appears only above a certain scale
    • Self-consistency: sample many chains and take the majority answer, trading inference compute for accuracy — the first clean demonstration of the test-time-compute tradeoff
    • The interpretability caveat that has held up: the stated chain is not reliably the computation the model performed, so it is a performance technique rather than an explanation
  • Test-time compute as a scaling axis
    • The finding that additional inference compute can substitute for additional parameters, with a favourable exchange rate on hard problems and an unfavourable one on easy ones
    • The methods: sequential revision, parallel sampling with selection, and search against a verifier — and the result that the optimal strategy depends on problem difficulty
    • Process reward models scoring each step versus outcome reward models scoring only the answer; the labelling cost of the former and the credit-assignment problem of the latter
  • Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
    • The core idea: for mathematics, code and structured tasks the reward is a program — a unit test, a proof checker, an exact-match on a canonical answer — so it is free, fast, consistent, and cannot be gamed by verbosity
    • The DeepSeek-R1 result: applying GRPO with purely verifiable rewards to a base model produces long chains, self-checking and backtracking without any supervised reasoning traces. Response length grows on its own as training proceeds, which is the striking empirical observation
    • The R1-Zero-versus-R1 distinction: pure RL produced strong reasoning with poor readability and language mixing; a small cold-start SFT stage before RL fixed the presentation. The pipeline everyone now uses descends from this
    • Distillation of reasoning: fine-tuning smaller models on a strong model's chains transfers much of the capability at a fraction of the cost, and often beats running RL on the small model directly
  • The open questions, stated as open
    • The verifier problem: RLVR works where verification is cheap, and most valuable tasks — writing, analysis, judgement, open-ended engineering — have no verifier. Extending it is the field's central practical difficulty
    • Whether RLVR creates new capability or elicits and sharpens what the base model could already sometimes do; the evidence is genuinely mixed and the pass@k analyses cut both ways
    • Reward hacking against verifiers — tests gamed rather than passed — which is the classical specification problem arriving in a new venue
    • Cost: reasoning models spend far more tokens per answer, which moves the economics discussed in M21 substantially

Work through

  • Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models — the original, including the scale threshold below which it does not help.
  • DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning — the central paper of this module. Read the R1-Zero section and the response-length curve carefully.
  • Let's Verify Step by Step — process versus outcome supervision, with the labelled-step dataset that made the comparison possible.
  • Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Can Be More Effective Than Scaling Model Parameters — the compute-exchange-rate result and the difficulty dependence.
  • DAPO: An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale — a fully open RLVR recipe with the specific GRPO modifications that made it stable.
  • A Little Bit of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — the reasoning-training chapters, for how this fits into the wider post-training picture.
  • Efficient NLP — its reasoning-model and test-time-compute explainers, for the mechanisms in short form.

Practice

Run a small RLVR loop yourself: take a small instruct model, a set of arithmetic or simple-code problems with programmatic verification, and GRPO. Log accuracy and mean response length against training step. Then compare three inference strategies on a held-out set at matched token budgets: greedy, self-consistency over k samples, and best-of-k selected by your verifier. You are done when you can plot accuracy against total inference tokens for all three and identify where each wins.

Where people go wrong

Reading the chain of thought as an explanation of the answer. Models produce chains that reach the correct answer through invalid steps, and chains that do not reflect the features actually driving the prediction — this has been demonstrated by perturbing the reasoning and observing the answer unchanged. A chain is a computation that improves accuracy, not a justification, and treating it as an audit trail in a high-stakes setting is a category error.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why RLVR needs no human labels and what exactly bounds its applicability, and state the R1-Zero result along with what the cold-start SFT stage was added to fix.

M11

Inference: the KV cache and everything that follows from it

≈8 h

Training happens once; inference happens on every request, and it is where the economics of this entire field are decided. The central fact is that generation is memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound, and almost every serving technique follows from that one observation. This module is also where M03's grouped-query attention finally makes sense — it exists to make this module's arithmetic work.

Concepts

  • The two phases, which have opposite characteristics
    • Prefill processes the whole prompt in one pass: compute-bound, parallel, high arithmetic intensity
    • Decode generates one token at a time: memory-bandwidth-bound, because each step reads the entire KV cache and all the weights to produce a single token
    • Time to first token versus inter-token latency as the two user-visible metrics, governed by different bottlenecks — which is why one configuration cannot optimise both
  • The KV cache, and the arithmetic you must be able to do
    • Size = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × sequence_length × batch × bytes. Compute it for a real model at a long context and notice that it exceeds the weights
    • This is why grouped-query attention exists, why context length is expensive, and why batch size is memory-limited rather than compute-limited
    • PagedAttention: manage the cache in fixed-size blocks like virtual memory, eliminating the fragmentation and over-reservation that wasted most of it. The single largest throughput improvement in open serving
    • Prefix caching and radix-tree reuse of shared prompt prefixes, which for system-prompt-heavy workloads removes most prefill cost
  • Batching and scheduling
    • Static batching's waste when sequences finish at different times; continuous batching, which admits new requests as slots free and is now universal
    • Chunked prefill to stop a long prompt stalling every decode in flight
    • Prefill–decode disaggregation: run the two phases on separate hardware pools sized for their different bottlenecks, transferring the cache between them. Now supported across the major open serving stacks and the standard architecture at scale
  • Making each token cheaper
    • Speculative decoding: a small draft model proposes several tokens, the large model verifies them in one forward pass, and rejection sampling makes the output distribution exactly the target model's. Free latency, no quality cost — the acceptance rate is the only variable
    • Self-speculative variants — Medusa heads, EAGLE, n-gram lookup — which remove the need for a separate draft model
    • Quantisation: weight-only 4-bit (GPTQ, AWQ) as the standard for memory-bound decode, FP8 for compute-bound prefill, and KV cache quantisation as the separate lever that buys context length
    • What quantisation actually costs, measured on tasks rather than on perplexity — the degradation is real, task-dependent, and usually smaller than the memory saving is worth

Work through

  • Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention — the vLLM paper, and the clearest statement of why cache fragmentation dominated serving cost.
  • Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding — read the rejection-sampling proof; the fact that the output distribution is unchanged is what makes the technique free rather than a tradeoff.
  • Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference — the partitioning and latency/throughput analysis, which is the systems foundation for the rest.
  • GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers — for how 4-bit weight quantisation is actually done and what it costs.
  • vLLM documentation — the deployment and performance-tuning guides; the configuration options are a good map of this module's concepts.
  • SGLang documentation — for RadixAttention and structured-output support, and as the second implementation to compare against.
  • SGLang: Efficient Execution of Structured Language Model Programs — the RadixAttention paper, for automatic prefix-cache reuse across requests.
  • Trelis Research — serving walkthroughs with real configurations and real cost numbers.

Practice

Serve one model under a real serving stack and measure: throughput and latency against batch size until you hit the memory wall; the same with a quantised model; and the same again with speculative decoding on and off, recording the acceptance rate. Compute the KV cache size analytically for each configuration and check it against measured memory. You are done when your predicted maximum batch size matches the observed one and you can explain any gap.

Where people go wrong

Sizing a deployment by parameter count. A 7-billion-parameter model in bf16 is 14 GB of weights, and at a 32k context with a large batch its KV cache can exceed that — so the model that "fits comfortably" serves four concurrent requests. The cache is the binding constraint at any useful context length, and it is what grouped-query attention, cache quantisation and paged management all exist to attack. Do the cache arithmetic before choosing hardware.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute a model's KV cache size for a given context and batch, explain why decode is memory-bandwidth-bound while prefill is not, and say why speculative decoding does not change the output distribution.

M12

Long context

≈6 h

Context windows went from two thousand tokens to a million in five years, and the naive reading — that retrieval is therefore obsolete — is wrong for reasons this module makes precise. Long context is three separate problems: making attention affordable, making position encodings extrapolate, and getting the model to actually use the middle of what it was given. The third is the one that decides whether a long context is useful.

Concepts

  • Position extrapolation, which is what makes extension possible
    • Why a model trained at 4k fails at 8k: the RoPE rotations reach angles never seen in training and attention becomes noise
    • Position interpolation — compress positions into the trained range — and the NTK-aware and YaRN refinements that scale different frequency bands differently, since high-frequency dimensions encode local order and low-frequency ones encode global position
    • Continued pretraining on long documents as the necessary complement; extension is not free
    • The finding that attention sinks — the first few tokens absorbing attention mass — are load-bearing, and that dropping them breaks streaming generation
  • Making it affordable, which is M11's problem restated
    • The KV cache grows linearly with context, so a million-token context is a memory problem before it is a compute problem
    • Cache eviction and compression: keeping heavy hitters, sliding windows plus sinks, and quantised caches
    • Prefill cost is quadratic, so time to first token becomes the dominant latency at long contexts — and prefix caching is what rescues repeated-prefix workloads
  • Whether the model uses it, which is the part that matters
    • Lost in the middle: retrieval accuracy is high at the beginning and end of the context and drops substantially in the middle, a robust finding that persists in models with very large advertised windows
    • Needle-in-a-haystack tests and their inadequacy — near-perfect retrieval of a single planted fact says almost nothing about multi-fact reasoning over a long document
    • Harder benchmarks requiring aggregation across many positions, where scores fall off far earlier than the window size suggests
    • The honest summary: effective context is usually well below advertised context, and the gap is task-dependent and must be measured
  • The comparison that actually matters — long context versus retrieval, decided by cost (prefill on every request versus an index), latency, freshness, and whether the relevant material is identifiable in advance. M14 develops the other side

Work through

  • Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts — the position-dependence result, which is the single most useful finding in this module.
  • YaRN: Efficient Context Window Extension of Large Language Models — the frequency-band-aware interpolation that is now the standard extension method.
  • Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks — the attention-sink finding and its use for unbounded streaming.
  • RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding — reread the frequency structure here; extension methods all manipulate it.
  • vLLM documentation — the prefix-caching and long-context configuration notes, for what this costs in practice.

Practice

Measure effective context yourself. Build a needle-in-a-haystack test across positions and context lengths and plot the heatmap — you should reproduce the middle degradation. Then build a harder variant requiring the model to aggregate three facts placed at different depths, and plot the same grid. You are done when the two heatmaps disagree substantially, because that difference is what "effective context" means and why single-needle tests are not evidence.

Where people go wrong

Treating the advertised context window as usable capacity. A model that accepts a million tokens will accept them; whether it attends to material in the middle is a separate empirical question, and the answer is usually that performance degrades well before the limit. Test with a task shaped like yours, at the depths you will actually use, before designing a system that depends on the full window — and cost the prefill, which is paid on every request.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why RoPE extension needs different treatment for different frequency bands, and design an experiment that distinguishes a model's advertised context from its effective context on your task.

M13

In-context learning, and what it actually is

≈5 h

Prompting is the interface almost everyone uses and the one with the least theory attached. This module covers what is actually established — which is more than folklore and less than the prompt-engineering literature claims — and connects it to the mechanistic evidence from the deep-learning page, because "in-context learning is implemented by induction heads" is one of the few places where a capability has been traced to a circuit.

Concepts

  • The phenomenon
    • Zero-, one- and few-shot prompting; performance improving with examples without any weight update
    • The surprising ablation: with enough demonstrations, randomising the labels in the examples costs far less accuracy than removing the examples. The format and the label space carry much of the benefit, not the input–label mapping
    • Sensitivity to example order, selection and formatting, which is large enough to swamp many reported prompting improvements
  • The mechanisms that have been proposed and the evidence for each
    • Induction heads: a two-head circuit that finds a previous occurrence of the current token and copies what followed. Their formation coincides with a phase change in in-context-learning ability, which is the strongest mechanistic evidence available
    • The implicit-gradient-descent account, showing that transformers can implement optimisation steps in their forward pass on simple regression tasks; suggestive, and demonstrated on toy settings rather than on language
    • Task location rather than task learning: the prompt selects among behaviours already present, which fits the random-label result better than a learning account does
    • The honest position: these are compatible partial accounts, not competing complete ones
  • What reliably works in practice, separated from what does not
    • Clear task specification, explicit output format, and demonstrations that cover the space of inputs — the reliable ones
    • Chain-of-thought prompting on multi-step tasks, from M10
    • Structured output constraints, which help more than most phrasing changes
    • What does not survive scrutiny: politeness, threats, incantations, and most named "techniques" that have not been evaluated against a matched baseline
    • Automatic prompt optimisation as the systematic alternative to manual iteration, and why it needs the evaluation discipline from M07 to mean anything
  • System prompts and the practical framing — the model has no persistent state, so everything it "knows" about the task is in the context on every request, which is the fact that makes M18's injection problem structural

Work through

  • Language Models are Few-Shot Learners — reread section 3 here, now as evidence about a mechanism rather than a capability announcement.
  • Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations: What Makes In-Context Learning Work? — the random-label ablations, which are the most informative experiments in this area.
  • In-Context Learning and Induction Heads — the mechanistic account and the phase-change evidence.
  • Prompt Engineering Guide — a reasonably disciplined survey of techniques; read it with M07's scepticism and check claims against a baseline.
  • DSPy — programmatic prompt optimisation, which replaces manual iteration with a search against a metric you have to define first.
  • Exploring Language Models — its visual explanations of prompting and in-context mechanisms, when notation is the obstacle.

Practice

Reproduce the random-label result on a classification task: measure accuracy with zero examples, with k correct examples, and with k examples whose labels are shuffled. Then measure the spread across ten random example orderings and five formatting variants. You are done when you can report the ordering spread as a number and compare it against the improvement claimed by any prompting technique you were considering adopting.

Where people go wrong

Adopting a prompting technique on the basis of a demonstration rather than a measurement. Prompt performance varies by several points across example orderings and formatting alone, so a technique shown to work on a handful of cherry-picked examples has not been shown to work at all. Build the evaluation from M07 first, then measure the technique against a matched baseline. Most named techniques do not survive this, and the ones that do are unglamorous.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the random-label finding and what it implies about whether in-context learning is learning, and describe the induction-head evidence in enough detail to say what it does and does not establish.

M14

Retrieval-augmented generation

≈7 h

Retrieval is the standard answer to the problems that a fixed, stale, unattributable parametric memory creates. It is also the component most often built badly, because the failures are quiet — a system that retrieves the wrong passages still produces fluent answers. This module treats retrieval quality as the thing to measure, since generation quality is bounded by it and no amount of prompt work recovers a bad retrieval.

Concepts

  • The pipeline and its failure points
    • Ingest, chunk, embed, index, retrieve, rerank, assemble the context, generate — and the fact that the first and last stages get the least attention and cause the most failures
    • Chunking as the underrated decision: fixed-size with overlap, semantic, or structure-aware; too small loses context, too large dilutes the embedding. Late chunking and contextual chunk headers as the current improvements
    • Parsing quality for real documents — tables, multi-column PDFs, scanned pages — which is where most production retrieval quality is actually lost
  • Retrieval itself
    • Dense embeddings and approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (HNSW, IVF); recall/latency tradeoffs and the parameters that control them
    • Sparse retrieval — BM25 — which remains a strong baseline and beats dense retrieval on rare terms, identifiers and exact matches
    • Hybrid retrieval with reciprocal rank fusion as the reliable default, because the two methods fail on different queries
    • Cross-encoder reranking of a larger candidate set, which is usually the highest-value single addition to a mediocre pipeline
    • Query transformation: rewriting, decomposition into sub-queries, and hypothetical document embeddings
  • Evaluation, kept separate for the two stages
    • Retrieval metrics — recall@k, MRR, nDCG — measured against a labelled set you have to build. Without this you cannot tell a retrieval failure from a generation failure
    • Generation metrics: faithfulness to the retrieved context, answer relevance, and citation correctness
    • The diagnostic that isolates the problem: feed the model the gold passages directly. If the answer is now right, retrieval is your bottleneck; if not, generation is
  • The variants and the decision
    • Agentic retrieval, where the model issues its own queries iteratively; graph-based retrieval for questions requiring aggregation across documents
    • Long context versus retrieval versus fine-tuning: retrieval for changing, large or attributable corpora; long context when the material is small enough and identifiable; fine-tuning for format, style and behaviour rather than for facts
    • The framing worth keeping: fine-tuning changes how the model behaves, retrieval changes what it knows, and confusing the two is the most common architectural error in this area

Work through

  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks — the original RAG paper, where retrieval and generation were trained jointly rather than bolted together.
  • Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering — dense retrieval's foundation, and the comparison against BM25 that still frames the hybrid argument.
  • Seven Failure Points When Engineering a Retrieval Augmented Generation System — an empirical study of what actually breaks in deployed systems.
  • BEIR: A Heterogeneous Benchmark for Zero-shot Evaluation of Information Retrieval Models — the benchmark showing how badly dense retrievers generalise across domains, which is the argument for hybrid.
  • Ragas — a retrieval-and-generation evaluation framework; use it to build the two-stage evaluation rather than eyeballing outputs.

Practice

Build a retrieval system on a real document set and evaluate the stages separately. Label a set of query–passage pairs, then measure recall@k for BM25, dense, and hybrid with reranking. Vary the chunk size across four settings and watch retrieval quality change more than any generation-side tweak. Finally run the gold-passage diagnostic. You are done when you can state, with numbers, which stage is your bottleneck and how much each intervention bought.

Where people go wrong

Debugging generation when retrieval is broken. If the right passage is not in the context, no prompt change will produce a correct answer — but the output stays fluent and plausible, so the failure looks like a model problem. Always measure retrieval independently, and run the gold-passage test before touching the prompt. A large share of RAG systems that are "not working" have a chunking or parsing problem two stages upstream.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: given a task, argue for retrieval, long context or fine-tuning with a cost, latency and freshness argument — and describe the experiment that would tell you whether your existing system's failures are retrieval or generation.

M15

Tool use and agents

≈7 h

A model that can call functions stops being a text generator and becomes something that acts, which changes both what it can do and what can go wrong. This module covers the mechanism — which is much simpler than the discourse suggests — and then the failure modes, which are where the real content is. The Classical AI page's module on whether language models can plan is the companion piece, and reading them together is the point.

Concepts

  • The mechanism, demystified
    • Tool calling is structured generation: the model is trained to emit a schema-conforming call, the runtime executes it, and the result is appended to the context. There is no execution inside the model
    • Tool definitions as prompt content, which means tool descriptions are prompt engineering and their wording measurably changes selection accuracy
    • Parallel and sequential calls; error results fed back as observations; the loop that continues until the model emits a final answer
    • Standardised tool interfaces, which turn every integration into a client rather than a bespoke adapter
  • Agent patterns, in order of ambition
    • ReAct: interleave reasoning traces with actions, so the model states why before acting. Still the baseline that more elaborate scaffolds must beat
    • Plan-and-execute versus reactive loops, and the tradeoff between committing early and adapting
    • Reflection and self-critique, which help when there is a signal to critique against and produce confident nonsense when there is not
    • Multi-agent arrangements, and the honest assessment that they frequently underperform a single well-prompted model while costing several times more — the burden of proof is on the multi-agent design
    • Memory: context as working memory, external stores as long-term memory, and the compaction problem as conversations grow
  • Where agents fail, which is the module's real content
    • Error compounding: per-step reliability of 95% gives 60% over ten steps, and long-horizon tasks are therefore bounded by step reliability rather than by capability
    • No principled recovery from a failed action; loops and repeated identical failures
    • Context exhaustion over long trajectories, and lossy compaction discarding the thing that mattered
    • The planning question from the Classical AI page: whether the model is planning or producing plan-shaped text, and the evidence that verification must come from outside
    • Evaluation as the binding constraint — trajectory-level benchmarks are expensive, noisy and quickly contaminated, so most agent claims rest on weak measurement
  • The security surface, which M18 develops — any agent with tools and untrusted input has an injection problem, and the combination of tool access, private data and external communication is the specific pattern to design against

Work through

  • ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models — the pattern everything else is a variation on.
  • Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools — for self-supervised learning of when to call a tool, which is the training-side counterpart.
  • SWE-bench: Can Language Models Resolve Real-World GitHub Issues? — the benchmark that made agent evaluation concrete, along with its contamination and scaffold-sensitivity caveats.
  • Model Context Protocol — the tool-interface specification, for how integrations are standardised in practice.
  • Building Effective Agents — an engineering guide that argues for the simplest pattern that works and is unusually direct about when not to build an agent.

Practice

Build a ReAct agent with three tools over a task requiring at least five steps, and instrument every step. Measure per-step success and end-to-end success across fifty runs, then check whether the end-to-end rate matches the product of the per-step rates. Then vary one tool's description wording and measure the change in selection accuracy. You are done when you can state your compounding curve and show that a description change moved tool selection.

Where people go wrong

Adding agents to a problem that a single call with good retrieval would solve. Each additional step multiplies cost, latency and failure probability, and multi-agent systems in particular frequently lose to one well-prompted model on the same task at a fraction of the price. The test to apply before building: can this be done in one call with the right context assembled beforehand? Build the loop only when the answer is genuinely no — usually because the next action depends on the result of the last.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the end-to-end success rate implied by a per-step reliability over an n-step task, and name the specific combination of capabilities that makes an agent architecturally unsafe against injection.

M16

Decoding and structured generation

≈5 h

The model gives you a distribution over the next token; decoding is how you turn that into text, and it changes output quality more than most prompt work. This module also covers constrained generation, which is what makes the tool calling in M15 reliable rather than hopeful — and which has a subtlety about probability distortion that most users of it have never considered.

Concepts

  • Sampling and its parameters
    • Greedy and beam search: appropriate for translation and summarisation, and the source of degenerate repetition in open-ended text. Beam search's decline in this field is worth understanding — maximising likelihood produces bland, repetitive output because human text is not maximally likely
    • Temperature as a rescaling of the logits; top-k and top-p (nucleus) truncation, and min-p as the more recent alternative that adapts the threshold to the distribution's peak
    • Repetition and frequency penalties, and their tendency to damage legitimate repetition in code and structured text
    • The practical guidance: low temperature for extraction and code, higher for creative generation, and greedy when you need reproducibility — but note that greedy is not deterministic across batch sizes on GPU
  • Constrained decoding
    • Mask the logits at each step so only tokens permitted by a grammar can be sampled, which makes invalid output impossible rather than unlikely
    • JSON schemas, regular expressions and context-free grammars compiled to finite-state machines over the token vocabulary; the precomputation that makes this near-free at generation time
    • The subtlety worth knowing: constraining renormalises the distribution and can therefore change the model's answer, not merely its format. A model forced into a schema may produce a different — sometimes worse — response than one asked politely and parsed
    • The tokenisation complication: valid strings can be unreachable because of how the grammar interacts with token boundaries
  • Getting more from the same model at inference
    • Self-consistency and best-of-n with a scorer, from M10, as decoding strategies rather than training ones
    • Contrastive and guided decoding — steering generation with a second model or a classifier — and where the cost is justified
  • Reproducibility, which is harder than it looks — floating-point non-determinism across batch sizes and kernels means fixing the seed and the temperature does not guarantee identical output, which matters for regression testing and for M07's evaluation

Work through

  • The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration — nucleus sampling, and the argument that likelihood maximisation is the wrong objective for open-ended text.
  • Efficient Guided Generation for Large Language Models — the finite-state-machine approach to constrained decoding, with the precomputation that makes it cheap.
  • SGLang documentation — its structured-output documentation, which is the most complete practical treatment of grammar-constrained generation.
  • vLLM documentation — the sampling-parameters reference, for exactly what each knob does to the logits.
  • CS224N: Natural Language Processing with Deep Learning — its generation and decoding lectures, which cover this material in a taught format.

Practice

On one model and one prompt set, sweep temperature and top-p and measure output diversity (distinct n-grams) and task accuracy on a scoreable task. Then run an extraction task three ways: unconstrained with parsing, prompted for JSON with parsing, and grammar-constrained. Record parse-failure rate and accuracy for each. You are done when you can show a case where constraining fixed the format and changed the answer — which is the module's non-obvious point.

Where people go wrong

Assuming constrained decoding is free. Masking logits renormalises the distribution over the permitted tokens, so the model can be pushed onto a path it would not otherwise have taken — most visibly when a schema forces an answer before the model has generated the reasoning that would have produced a better one. Put reasoning fields before answer fields in your schema, and check accuracy under constraint rather than only parse rate.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why beam search produces worse open-ended text than sampling despite finding higher-likelihood sequences, and describe how grammar-constrained decoding can change a model's answer rather than only its format.

M17

Interpretability of language models

≈5 h

The deep-learning page introduced mechanistic interpretability in general; this module is what it has established about language models specifically, and what it enables. It is placed here because the two modules that follow — safety and hallucination — both depend on knowing what can and cannot currently be established about a model's internals, and because activation steering is one of the few control methods that is neither prompting nor training.

Concepts

  • What has been found in language models
    • Induction heads and the in-context-learning connection, from M13
    • Feed-forward layers behaving as key–value memories, with identifiable factual associations localisable to specific layers and editable — with the caveat that edited facts often fail to propagate to their logical consequences
    • Fully reverse-engineered circuits for narrow behaviours in small models, such as indirect object identification
    • Features found by sparse autoencoders at scale, including abstract and multilingual ones, and the demonstration that amplifying a feature changes behaviour in the predicted direction
  • The methods, and what each licenses
    • Activation patching and causal tracing for causal claims; probing for correlational ones, with control tasks to bound probe capacity
    • Logit lens: decode intermediate residual states through the output embedding to watch a prediction form across layers
    • Sparse autoencoders and the dictionary-learning framing; the open question of whether the recovered features are the right decomposition
    • Attribution graphs and circuit tracing as the current attempt to scale circuit analysis beyond hand-work
  • What it is useful for right now
    • Activation steering: add a direction to the residual stream at inference to shift behaviour — cheaper than fine-tuning, more reliable than prompting, and currently blunt
    • Detecting when a model's internal state is inconsistent with its output, which is the most promising route to the deception and hallucination questions in M19
    • Model editing for factual updates, with the propagation caveat above
    • Evaluating whether a stated chain of thought corresponds to the computation performed — the M10 caveat given a method
  • The honest limits — most results are on small models or narrow behaviours, the field lacks agreed standards for what counts as an explanation, and interpretability illusions (a compelling story that does not survive intervention) are a documented failure mode

Work through

  • In-Context Learning and Induction Heads — the strongest circuit-to-capability link available.
  • Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT — causal tracing and the ROME editing method, and the clearest demonstration that facts have locations.
  • Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning — sparse autoencoders applied to a real model, with the feature analysis worked through.
  • Interpretability in the Wild: A Circuit for Indirect Object Identification — a complete reverse-engineered circuit, and the methodology template for doing it yourself.
  • TransformerLens — the library; M17's exercises are impractical without it.

Practice

Take a small open model and locate a factual association with causal tracing — corrupt the subject tokens, then restore activations layer by layer to find where the fact lives. Edit it with a rank-one update and verify the change. Then test propagation: does the model's answer to a question that depends on the edited fact also change? You are done when you can show the edit succeeding on the direct query and failing to propagate, because that gap is the current state of model editing.

Where people go wrong

Accepting an interpretability story that has not been tested by intervention. It is easy to find a neuron, a head or a feature whose activation pattern tells a satisfying story, and much harder to show that ablating or patching it changes behaviour in the predicted way. Correlational evidence in this area is abundant and frequently misleading; the causal test is cheap and is what separates a result from a narrative.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe how causal tracing establishes a location for a fact, and explain why editing that location changes the direct answer while frequently failing to change its downstream implications.

M18

Jailbreaks, prompt injection and the security model

≈7 h

Two failures are constantly conflated and they are completely different problems. A jailbreak is a user getting a model to do what its trainers forbade — a training and policy issue. A prompt injection is a third party in the data stream taking control of a model acting on someone else's behalf — an architectural issue with no known general solution. The second is the one that decides whether the agents in M15 can be deployed, and it deserves the weight this module gives it.

Concepts

  • Jailbreaks
    • The techniques: role-play and persona framing, hypothetical and fictional wrappers, incremental escalation, low-resource languages, encodings and ciphers, and many-shot conditioning with a long context of compliant examples
    • Automated attacks: gradient-based adversarial suffixes that transfer across models, and search-based attacks using an attacker model
    • Why they persist: refusal is a learned behaviour over a distribution, and the space of prompts is unbounded — safety training shifts probabilities rather than installing a constraint
    • Defences and their costs: refusal training, classifiers on input and output, constitutional methods, and the false-refusal rate that every one of them raises
  • Prompt injection, which is the structural problem
    • The root cause: instructions and data occupy the same channel. A model reading a web page, an email or a document cannot distinguish "content to process" from "instructions to obey", because there is no mechanism that separates them
    • Indirect injection — the payload is in retrieved content rather than in the user's message — which is the realistic attack against any RAG or agent system
    • The lethal trifecta: an agent with access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a way to communicate externally. Any two are manageable; all three permit exfiltration, and this is a design-time property rather than a runtime one
    • Why it is not a training problem: unlike jailbreaks, there is no correct behaviour the model could learn, since the malicious instruction is indistinguishable in form from a legitimate one
    • What actually helps: privilege separation between planning and execution, capability restriction so the agent cannot reach both private data and an outbound channel, deterministic policy checks outside the model, human confirmation on consequential actions, and treating all model output as untrusted input to whatever consumes it
  • The rest of the surface — training-data poisoning and backdoors, model and prompt extraction, membership inference, and denial of service through expensive generations
  • Evaluation and process — red teaming (manual and automated), the difference between a capability evaluation and a safety evaluation, and the fact that a defence measured against fixed attacks tells you very little, which is the adversarial-evaluation lesson from the deep-learning page in a new setting

Work through

  • Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection — the paper that established indirect injection as the practical threat.
  • Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models — automated suffix attacks that transfer, which is what makes jailbreaking a systems problem rather than a curiosity.
  • Many-shot Jailbreaking — the long-context attack, and a clean illustration of a capability creating a vulnerability.
  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications — the practitioner's checklist, and the right artefact to review a design against.
  • Simon Willison's blog — the most consistent ongoing coverage of prompt injection, and the source of the lethal-trifecta framing.

Practice

Build a small RAG agent with a tool that can send data somewhere, then attack it: plant an injection in a retrieved document that instructs the agent to exfiltrate the private context. Get it working. Then implement three defences — an input classifier, an instruction-hierarchy prompt, and a capability restriction that removes the outbound tool when untrusted content is in context — and measure attack success under each. You are done when you can show that only the last one is robust, and explain why the first two are probabilistic.

Where people go wrong

Trying to solve prompt injection with a prompt. "Ignore any instructions in the retrieved content" is itself just text in the same channel as the attack, and every variant of it has been defeated. The same applies to classifier-based filtering, which is an arms race with a false-positive cost. The only defences that hold are architectural: restrict what the agent can reach, separate planning from execution, and enforce policy outside the model. Design for the assumption that injection will succeed.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the difference between a jailbreak and a prompt injection in terms of who the attacker and the victim are, name the three capabilities whose combination permits exfiltration, and explain why no amount of training fixes the second problem.

M19

Hallucination and calibration

≈5 h

Fabrication is the failure mode that keeps these systems out of the domains where they would be most valuable, and it is widely misdiagnosed as a bug that better training will remove. It is structural: the objective rewards plausible continuations, nothing in it distinguishes recalled from invented, and — as recent analysis makes explicit — the evaluation regime actively penalises abstention. This module covers why, and what actually reduces it.

Concepts

  • Why it happens, at three levels
    • The objective: next-token prediction rewards fluent plausible continuation. A confident wrong answer and a confident right one are indistinguishable to the loss when the fact was not in the data
    • The data: errors and contradictions in the corpus, and the long tail where a fact appeared too rarely to be learned reliably. Memorisation of rare facts is a function of duplication count
    • The incentives, which is the sharpest framing: benchmarks score exact matches and give no credit for "I don't know", so both pretraining and post-training reward guessing over abstaining. Hallucination is partly a rational response to how models are graded
    • The useful distinction: intrinsic hallucination contradicts the provided source; extrinsic hallucination is unsupported by it. Retrieval addresses the second and not the first
  • Detecting it
    • Self-consistency: sample several answers and measure agreement; semantic entropy clusters answers by meaning rather than surface form and is the stronger version
    • Token-level probabilities and their limitations — models are reasonably calibrated on multiple-choice and much less so on free-form generation, and post-training reliably degrades calibration
    • Internal-state probes, from M17, which can predict incorrectness better than the model's own stated confidence
    • External verification against a source, which is the only method that scales to arbitrary claims
  • Reducing it, with costs attached
    • Retrieval with enforced grounding and citation, which converts a knowledge problem into a retrieval problem and moves the failure to M14's pipeline
    • Abstention training and calibration-aware objectives that reward "I don't know" — effective, and it lowers scores on benchmarks that do not credit abstention, which is why it is under-adopted
    • Verification passes, self-critique with a source, and the reliability ceiling that self-critique alone hits
    • Decoding-time methods that contrast layers or contexts to favour grounded continuations
  • The measurement problem — automatic hallucination metrics are themselves models and inherit the same failures, so evaluation here is expensive and the reported numbers should be treated with the scepticism M07 established

Work through

  • Why Language Models Hallucinate — the incentive argument: models guess because the grading rewards guessing. The most useful reframing available.
  • Detecting Hallucinations in Large Language Models Using Semantic Entropy — semantic-entropy detection, which is the strongest sampling-based method and needs no labels.
  • Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know — on self-evaluation and calibration, including where models' own confidence is and is not informative.
  • Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation — for the intrinsic/extrinsic taxonomy and the mitigation landscape.
  • Ragas — the faithfulness metrics, for measuring grounding in a retrieval system rather than asserting it.

Practice

Build a fabrication detector. Take a factual question set with known answers, sample five responses per question at temperature 1, cluster them by semantic equivalence, and compute semantic entropy. Plot entropy against correctness and measure the detector's AUC. Then compare it against the model's stated confidence and against mean token probability. You are done when you can rank the three signals by how well they predict error — and semantic entropy should win.

Where people go wrong

Treating hallucination as a defect that the next model will fix. It follows from the training objective and is actively reinforced by evaluation practices that give no credit for abstention — a model trained to maximise benchmark score is trained to guess. Systems that need reliability get it from architecture: retrieval with enforced citation, external verification, and abstention paths. Waiting for a model that does not fabricate is not a strategy.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain how benchmark scoring incentivises fabrication, distinguish intrinsic from extrinsic hallucination and say which retrieval addresses, and describe semantic entropy well enough to say why it beats raw token probability.

M20

Small models, distillation and on-device

≈5 h

Most deployed language-model workloads do not need a frontier model, and the gap between what a small model can do and what a large one can do has narrowed faster than the headline capabilities suggest. This module covers how small models are made good — which is mostly data and distillation rather than architecture — and it is where M05's overtraining argument and M10's reasoning-distillation result meet.

Concepts

  • What makes a small model good
    • Massive overtraining relative to Chinchilla, from M05, which is the single biggest factor
    • Data quality over quantity: the textbook-quality-data result, where a small model trained on carefully filtered and synthetically generated material substantially outperforms its size class — with the contamination caveat that such curation makes hard to rule out
    • Distillation of reasoning traces from a stronger model, which transfers much of M10's capability far more cheaply than running RL directly on the small model
  • Distillation, properly
    • Response distillation (train on the teacher's outputs) versus logit distillation (match the full distribution, which carries far more information per token) versus feature matching
    • On-policy distillation, where the student generates and the teacher scores its own outputs, avoiding the exposure-bias mismatch of purely offline data
    • The licensing question, which is a real constraint: many model terms prohibit training competitors on their outputs
  • Compression after training
    • Quantisation from M11, applied more aggressively for on-device — 4-bit weights as the standard, with quantisation-aware training when post-training quantisation degrades too much
    • Pruning, structured and unstructured, and the hardware-support gap that makes unstructured sparsity often unrealisable as speedup
    • Depth and width pruning followed by continued training, which is a cheap way to produce a smaller sibling of an existing model
  • The routing pattern that is now standard practice — classify request difficulty, send most traffic to a small model and escalate the rest, and measure the quality/cost frontier rather than a single model's score. Most production workloads are dominated by requests a small model handles perfectly
  • On-device constraints: memory ceilings, thermal and battery limits, prefill latency on mobile hardware, and the privacy and offline arguments that make it worth the effort

Work through

  • Textbooks Are All You Need — the phi line's data-quality argument, read together with its contamination criticisms.
  • DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning — reread the distillation section here; the finding that distilling beats running RL on the small model is the practical headline.
  • Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network — Hinton et al.'s original, for why soft targets carry more information than hard labels.
  • AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization for LLM Compression and Acceleration — the quantisation method most used for on-device, and the salient-weight insight behind it.
  • Llama.cpp — the practical on-device stack; its quantisation format documentation is the clearest guide to the quality/size tradeoff at each bit width.

Practice

Distil a small model on a narrow task three ways: fine-tune on the teacher's text outputs, fine-tune on its full logits, and fine-tune on teacher-scored samples from the student's own generations. Evaluate all three plus the undistilled small model and the teacher. Then quantise your best student to 8-bit and 4-bit and measure the degradation per task. You are done when you can plot quality against cost for six configurations and identify the frontier.

Where people go wrong

Benchmarking a small model against a frontier model on general benchmarks and concluding it is unusable. The relevant comparison is on your task distribution after task-specific distillation, where the gap is usually far smaller and sometimes absent — and where the cost difference is an order of magnitude. The routing pattern exists because most production traffic is easy; measure what fraction of yours actually needs the large model before paying for it on all of it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why logit distillation transfers more per example than response distillation, and design a routing setup with a stated escalation criterion and a measurement of what it costs in quality.

M21

Serving economics and the build decision

≈5 h

Every technical choice in this syllabus eventually resolves into a cost per request and a latency, and the decisions that actually get made — API or self-host, which model, prompt or retrieve or fine-tune — are economic ones informed by the mechanisms. This module assembles that arithmetic, and it is the one most directly useful to anyone responsible for a system rather than a model.

Concepts

  • Where the money goes
    • Input and output tokens priced differently because prefill and decode have different bottlenecks — and output tokens typically cost several times more, which is why M10's reasoning models change budgets so sharply
    • Prompt caching, which makes a long stable system prompt nearly free after the first call and changes the design of every prompt-heavy application
    • Self-hosting arithmetic: hardware amortisation, utilisation, and the break-even volume against API pricing — usually much higher than intuition suggests, because idle accelerators are the dominant cost
    • Batch and off-peak pricing tiers for latency-tolerant workloads
  • The latency budget
    • Time to first token versus tokens per second, and which one users actually perceive for a given interaction pattern
    • Streaming as a perception fix rather than a throughput one
    • The throughput/latency tradeoff in batching: larger batches raise throughput and raise per-request latency, and the operating point is a product decision
    • Reasoning tokens as a latency cost that is invisible in the output
  • The intervention ladder, cheapest first — a better prompt, then few-shot examples, then retrieval, then a fine-tune, then a larger model — and the discipline of measuring each step against the evaluation from M07 before taking the next
    • The specific question fine-tuning answers: format, style, latency and cost at fixed quality. It is a poor way to add knowledge, which is M14's job
  • Operating the system — caching at the semantic level, fallback and retry policy, rate limits and quotas, monitoring for quality drift when a provider updates a model behind a stable name, and regression testing against a fixed evaluation set as the only defence against silent behaviour change
  • The build-versus-buy decision, with the factors that actually decide it: data residency and privacy, latency floors, cost at projected volume, the need for a frozen model version, and the engineering capacity to run inference infrastructure

Work through

  • Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference — reread for the latency/throughput Pareto analysis, which is the formal version of this module's tradeoff.
  • vLLM documentation — the benchmarking and autoscaling guides, for measuring your own numbers rather than trusting published ones.
  • Building Effective Agents — for the argument that the simplest sufficient architecture is usually the right one, which is this module's ladder stated from the engineering side.
  • Interconnects — Lambert's newsletter, which covers the model-release and pricing landscape with more technical judgement than the trade press.

Practice

Build a cost model for a real application: expected requests, tokens in and out, cache hit rate, and the API price against a self-hosted configuration whose throughput you have actually measured. Find the break-even volume. Then walk the intervention ladder on the same task — prompt, few-shot, retrieval, fine-tune — measuring quality and cost at each rung. You are done when you can state the cheapest rung that meets your quality bar and the break-even point in requests per day.

Where people go wrong

Self-hosting for cost reasons at low volume. An accelerator costs the same whether it is busy or idle, so the break-even against API pricing sits at a utilisation most applications never reach — and the comparison usually omits the engineering time to run the stack, the on-call burden and the redundancy. Self-host for privacy, for a frozen model version, or for a latency floor you cannot otherwise hit; do it for cost only after measuring your actual utilisation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: compute the break-even volume between an API and a self-hosted deployment including utilisation, and justify a position on the intervention ladder with a measured quality and cost number for the rung below it.

M22

The frontier

≈4 h

This is the fastest-moving field in this library, and a syllabus that ends at settled material would be teaching 2024. These are the live disagreements — where competent people hold opposite views and the evidence is incomplete — and the skill is holding a position with its evidence attached and knowing what would change it.

Concepts

  • Where the marginal compute should go — pretraining scale, post-training, or inference-time computation. The reasoning results moved a lot of investment toward the last two, and whether that is a permanent reallocation or a temporary arbitrage is genuinely open
  • The verifier problem, which is M10's bound and the field's most consequential practical question
    • RLVR works where verification is cheap. Extending it to writing, analysis and judgement requires either learned verifiers — which reintroduce reward hacking — or a way to decompose open tasks into checkable pieces
    • Self-verification and debate-style protocols as candidate answers, with weak evidence so far
  • Data, from the deep-learning page, in its language-specific form — high-quality text exhaustion, the measured decay of repeated epochs, synthetic data with the model-collapse constraint, and whether curation can keep substituting for volume
  • Architecture: whether the transformer persists, hybrid attention/state-space models, and discrete diffusion for text as the non-autoregressive alternative that would change the entire inference stack in M11 if it worked at scale — the generative-models page develops the mechanism
  • Agents and long-horizon reliability — whether per-step reliability improves enough to make M15's compounding tractable, and whether the answer is better models or better scaffolds and verification
  • Evaluation, which is the bottleneck on knowing any of the above
    • Contamination is unfalsifiable at web scale; benchmarks saturate faster than they are replaced; agentic evaluation is expensive and noisy
    • The gap between benchmark performance and usefulness is now large enough that several widely reported advances did not transfer
  • The safety questions that are technical rather than speculative — capability evaluations for dangerous uses, scalable oversight when outputs exceed human ability to check, and injection remaining unsolved while agent deployment accelerates

Work through

  • Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Can Be More Effective Than Scaling Model Parameters — the quantitative statement of the reallocation argument.
  • arXiv cs.CL — follow specific groups rather than the firehose; this literature moves by lab, not by keyword.
  • Interconnects — for ongoing technical analysis of releases and post-training practice, which is where the tacit knowledge in M09 and M10 gets written down.

Practice

Pick one disagreement here and write a two-page position with the evidence on both sides and the experiment that would settle it. Then take the three most recent papers on the question and check whether your position survives. You are done when you can state what would change your mind — and, ideally, when one of the three papers has already changed it.

Where people go wrong

Forming views about capability from benchmark tables. Contamination cannot be ruled out at this scale, formatting alone moves scores by several points, and saturated benchmarks stop discriminating long before they stop being quoted. Ask what was held constant, whether the baseline got the same tuning budget, and whether the evaluation could be contaminated — and weight your own hands-on experience on tasks you understand above any leaderboard.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state two live disagreements with the strongest evidence on each side, and name the experiment that would resolve each — without asserting a conclusion the evidence does not support.

Capstone

Two projects: one that builds a model, one that builds a system. Do the first if you want to work on models, the second if you want to ship products, and both if you have the time.

Project 1 — a small language model, end to end. Trained by you, from a tokeniser to a served endpoint.

  • Scope: train a BPE tokeniser on your own corpus; build a data pipeline with filtering and deduplication and report attrition per stage; implement a modern architecture (RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, GQA); fit a scaling law on a ladder of sizes and use it to pick the final configuration; pretrain with a documented parallelism strategy and full instrumentation; fine-tune on instructions with correct loss masking; align with DPO or GRPO and report length-controlled win rates; then serve it with paged attention and speculative decoding and measure throughput, latency and KV cache usage against your own predictions.
  • Draws on: M02–M06, M08, M09, M11.
  • Done when: the served model follows instructions, your scaling-law prediction matched the final loss to within a few percent, and your KV cache arithmetic matched measured memory. Report all three numbers.
  • Stretch: add an RLVR stage on a verifiable task and show accuracy and mean response length rising together; or distil the result into a model half the size and plot the quality/cost frontier.

Project 2 — a retrieval application that is honestly evaluated and hard to attack.

  • Scope: build a RAG system over a real corpus with hybrid retrieval and reranking; build a labelled retrieval evaluation set and report recall@k separately from generation quality; implement an LLM judge and measure its position and verbosity bias before using it; add a hallucination detector using semantic entropy and report its AUC; add tool calling with grammar-constrained output; then red-team the whole thing for indirect injection and implement an architectural defence rather than a prompt-level one; finally, build the cost model and identify the cheapest rung of the intervention ladder that meets your quality bar.
  • Draws on: M07, M11, M14, M15, M16, M18, M19, M21.
  • Done when: you can present four numbers — retrieval recall@k, judge bias magnitude, hallucination-detector AUC, and cost per request — plus a working injection attack against your first design and evidence that your final design defeats it.
  • Stretch: route between a small and a large model with a measured escalation criterion, and report the cost saving at equal quality.

Mastery check

  • You can state the relationship between cross-entropy loss and compressed size, and why perplexity is not comparable across tokenisers.
  • You can predict a tokeniser's arithmetic failures from its merge rules.
  • You can name every architectural decision in an open-weights config and say what each optimises.
  • You can describe a pretraining data pipeline with attrition per stage and name the highest-value filtering step.
  • You can derive C ≈ 6ND and argue for a deliberately non-compute-optimal allocation.
  • You can specify a parallelism configuration for a stated cluster and name three causes of a loss spike.
  • You can design an evaluation controlling for contamination, format sensitivity and judge bias.
  • You can say which weights a LoRA config adapts and when it will underperform full fine-tuning.
  • You can say what each of RLHF, DPO and GRPO removed relative to its predecessor.
  • You can explain what RLVR produces without human labels, and where the verifier problem bounds it.
  • You can compute a KV cache size and explain why it, not the parameter count, limits batch size.
  • You can explain why speculative decoding leaves the output distribution unchanged.
  • You can distinguish advertised from effective context and design the experiment that measures it.
  • You can state the random-label in-context-learning result and what it implies.
  • You can diagnose whether a retrieval system's failures are retrieval or generation.
  • You can compute an agent's end-to-end success rate from its per-step reliability.
  • You can explain how constrained decoding can change an answer rather than only its format.
  • You can distinguish a jailbreak from a prompt injection and name the lethal trifecta.
  • You can explain why benchmark scoring incentivises hallucination.
  • You can compute the break-even volume between an API and self-hosting.

Papers & primary sources

  1. R-11 Language Models are Few-Shot Learners — Tom B. Brown et al., 2020

    GPT-3, and the paper that reorganised the field around prompting rather than fine-tuning. Read sections 1–3 for the in-context-learning framing and section 5 for the limitations, which are more candid than the paper's reputation suggests. Long; the appendices are reference material.

    Core Free Intermediate ~16 pp
    Used in M16
  48. R-58 Efficient Guided Generation for Large Language Models — Brandon T. Willard & Rémi Louf, 2023

    Compiling a regular expression or grammar into a finite-state machine over the token vocabulary, so that constrained decoding costs almost nothing at generation time. The precomputation trick is what made grammar-constrained output practical rather than theoretical.

    Free Advanced ~14 pp
    Used in M16
  49. R-59 Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT — Kevin Meng, David Bau, Alex Andonian & Yonatan Belinkov, 2022

    Causal tracing to locate a fact, then a rank-one weight edit to change it. Read it for the method rather than the application — the corrupt-then-restore protocol is the template for establishing that a component causally matters, which is M17's central technique.

    Core Free Advanced ~31 pp
    Used in M17
  50. R-60 Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning — Trenton Bricken et al., Anthropic, 2023

    Sparse autoencoders applied to a real language model, recovering features that are far more interpretable than neurons and demonstrably causal when amplified. The most complete worked example of the superposition-to-features programme, with the analysis shown rather than summarised.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M17
  51. R-61 Interpretability in the Wild: A Circuit for Indirect Object Identification — Kevin Wang, Alexandre Variengien, Arthur Conmy, Buck Shlegeris & Jacob Steinhardt, 2022

    A complete reverse-engineered circuit in a real model, with every claimed component verified by ablation. Read it as a methodology template — the discipline of validating each hypothesised role by intervention is what M17's trap warns you not to skip.

    Free Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M17
  52. R-62 Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection — Kai Greshake et al., 2023

    The paper that established indirect prompt injection as the practical threat: the payload lives in retrieved content, not in the user's message, so the attacker never touches the application. Read the taxonomy of attack goals; it is the checklist to design against.

    Core Free Intermediate ~20 pp
    Used in M18
  53. R-63 Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models — Andy Zou, Zifan Wang, Nicholas Carlini, Milad Nasr, J. Zico Kolter & Matt Fredrikson, 2023

    Gradient-based adversarial suffixes found on open models that transfer to closed ones. The transferability is the important part: it means jailbreaking is not a per-model engineering problem but a property of how these models are aligned.

    Core Free Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M18
  54. R-64 Many-shot Jailbreaking — Cem Anil et al., Anthropic, 2024

    Filling a long context with fabricated compliant exchanges defeats safety training, with effectiveness following a power law in the number of shots. A clean demonstration that a capability improvement — longer context — can itself create a vulnerability, which is the pattern to watch for.

    Free Intermediate
    Used in M18
  55. R-65 Why Language Models Hallucinate — Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala & Edwin Zhang, 2025

    The incentive argument, and the most useful reframing of this problem available: models guess because benchmarks reward guessing and give no credit for abstention. It makes hallucination partly an artefact of how we grade rather than solely of how we train, which changes what a fix would look like.

    Core Free Intermediate ~24 pp
    Used in M19
  56. R-66 Detecting Hallucinations in Large Language Models Using Semantic Entropy — Sebastian Farquhar, Jannik Kossen, Lorenz Kuhn & Yarin Gal, Nature, 2024

    Cluster sampled answers by meaning rather than surface form, then measure entropy over the clusters. It needs no labels and no task-specific training and outperforms token-probability baselines substantially. The strongest unsupervised detector available. (Nature 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Free Advanced ~8 pp
    Used in M19
  57. R-67 Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know — Saurav Kadavath et al., Anthropic, 2022

    Models can be trained to predict whether their own answers are correct, and their calibration is reasonable on multiple choice and much worse on free-form generation. Read it for the honest account of where self-evaluation works and where it does not.

    Free Advanced ~35 pp
    Used in M19
  58. R-68 Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation — Ziwei Ji et al., 2022

    The intrinsic/extrinsic taxonomy and a survey of mitigation approaches across generation tasks. Predates the current generation of models and remains the best organising framework — the distinction between contradicting a source and being unsupported by one is what tells you whether retrieval will help.

    Free Intermediate ~38 pp
    Used in M19
  59. R-69 Textbooks Are All You Need — Suriya Gunasekar et al., Microsoft, 2023

    A small model trained on filtered and synthetically generated "textbook-quality" data substantially outperforming its size class. Read it together with the contamination criticisms it attracted — the result is real and the magnitude is contested, which is itself a good lesson about evaluating data-quality claims.

    Free Intermediate ~26 pp
    Used in M20
  60. R-70 Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network — Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals & Jeff Dean, 2015

    The original distillation paper, and the dark-knowledge argument: a teacher's full output distribution says far more per example than its argmax, because the relative probabilities of the wrong answers encode similarity structure. Everything in M20 rests on this.

    Core Free Intermediate ~9 pp
    Used in M20
  61. R-71 AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization for LLM Compression and Acceleration — Ji Lin et al., 2023

    The observation that a small fraction of weights — identified by activation magnitude rather than by weight magnitude — carry most of the quantisation error, and protecting those recovers most of the loss. The standard method for on-device 4-bit deployment.

    Free Advanced ~18 pp
    Used in M20
  62. R-72 SGLang: Efficient Execution of Structured Language Model Programs — Lianmin Zheng et al., 2023

    RadixAttention for automatic prefix-cache reuse across requests, plus a language for expressing structured generation programs. The prefix-sharing result is the one that matters commercially: for system-prompt-heavy workloads it removes most prefill cost.

    Core Free Advanced ~20 pp
    Used in M11
