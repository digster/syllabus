Artificial intelligence

Classical Artificial Intelligence

Before AI meant training a network, it meant building a program that could decide what to do — by searching, by proving, by planning, by computing an expectation. This syllabus covers that discipline in the scope Russell and Norvig gave it: agents and rationality, search and heuristics, constraints and SAT, logic and knowledge representation, planning, probabilistic reasoning over time, decisions under uncertainty, and multi-agent systems. Most of it predates deep learning, all of it still ships, and none of it is obsolete — the SAT solver in your chip verifier and the planner in your warehouse are here, not in a transformer.

What this topic is

Classical artificial intelligence is the study of how a program can decide what to do. Its organising abstraction is the agent: something that perceives an environment and acts on it, and whose quality is judged by a performance measure external to itself. Everything else follows from asking how such an agent should compute its next action under different assumptions about the world. If the world is known and deterministic, the answer is search. If actions have structure and goals are declarative, it is planning. If the world is only partly observable and noisy, it is probabilistic inference followed by an expected-utility calculation. Each of those answers is a body of algorithms with proofs attached, and the proofs are the point — a classical AI result usually tells you what the algorithm cannot do as sharply as what it can.

The field is often described as the part of AI that lost, which is wrong in a way worth correcting early. Symbolic AI lost the competition to learn perception and language from data, comprehensively, and this syllabus does not dispute that. But the algorithms here run the world's chip verification, airline crew rostering, warehouse routing, spacecraft command sequencing, package dependency resolution and program analysis, and they do it because the problems have hard constraints, need optimality guarantees, or must produce an artefact a human can audit. The interesting modern question is not which paradigm won; it is where the boundary sits, which is exactly what M22 examines with the evidence on whether language models can plan.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes that you can write and debug programs of a few hundred lines in a language of your choice, that you remember what a graph, a queue and a recursive function are, and that you have met probability at least once — conditional probability, independence and expectation. You do not need calculus except in two places, linear algebra except in one, or any machine learning at all. You do need to be willing to write proofs of the "here is why this terminates and returns the optimum" kind, because that is what most of the theory in this field looks like.

  • Algorithms and complexity, if graph traversal and big-O have gone rusty — Chapters 1–3 and 22 of Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach assume them rather than teach them, so patch this first.
  • Probability, needed from M13 onward — Chapter 12 of Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach is a compressed refresher aimed at exactly this gap.
  • Propositional and first-order logic, if you have never written a formal proof — the logic chapters of Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents are gentler than AIMA's and free to read online.
  • Linear algebra, needed only for the Kalman filter in M14 — any first course covers enough; matrix inverse and covariance are the whole requirement.

Not covered here: machine learning in all its forms. AIMA devotes six chapters to it and this library gives it four pages instead — Classical Machine Learning for statistical prediction, Deep Learning for neural networks including deep RL, and the two generative-AI pages for language and everything else. This page owns MDPs, POMDPs and tabular reinforcement learning, because those are decision-making theory rather than learning theory; it hands over to Deep Learning the moment a function approximator appears. Natural language processing and computer vision are also out: AIMA's chapters on them are now historical, and the honest modern treatment lives on the deep-learning pages.

How to use this

  • Working knowledge (≈40 h): M01–M03, M05, M07, M08, M13, M15, M16. That is the spine an interview or a graduate qualifying exam actually tests, and it is enough to read most of the field's literature. Do the M03 heuristic project; it is the exercise that converts "I know what A* is" into "I know why A* is".
  • The solver route (≈45 h): M01–M06, M10, M11, M20. Search, constraints, SAT and planning, ending with the record of what those techniques shipped. This is the path if you came here because something at work is a scheduling or configuration problem and you suspect it has a name. Handbook of Satisfiability and the The International Planning Competition domains are the continuation.
  • The uncertainty route (≈50 h): M01, M12–M19. Probability, time, utility and sequential decisions, ending in robotics. Skips logic and planning entirely. This is the path toward robotics, control and decision support, and Algorithms for Decision Making is written for exactly this reader.
  • Full course (≈136 h): every module in order, every checkpoint answered out loud before moving on. M19 and M21 are the two you can skip without breaking anything later; everything else is load-bearing for something downstream.
  • Already working in this area: start at M06, then M12, then M22. The gap between a standard first course and what the field knows is widest at modern SAT solving, at the nonmonotonic-reasoning literature that a first course skips entirely, and at the current evidence on language models and planning.

What you'll be able to do

  • Formulate a messy real problem as a state space, a CSP, a SAT instance, a PDDL domain or an MDP — and argue which of those framings is the right one.
  • Prove A* optimal under a consistent heuristic, and name the exact step of the proof that fails when the heuristic is merely admissible and the search is over a graph.
  • Design an admissible heuristic by relaxation or by pattern database, and measure the node expansions it saves rather than guessing.
  • Explain conflict-driven clause learning to someone who knows DPLL — implication graph, first-UIP cut, learned clause, non-chronological backtrack — and say why the learned clause is sound.
  • Encode a scheduling problem in MiniZinc or CP-SAT and know why a constraint solver beats a hand-rolled backtracker on it.
  • Write a PDDL domain, run it through a modern planner, and explain what the delete relaxation discards and why the resulting heuristic stays admissible.
  • Read a Bayesian network, apply d-separation to decide an independence question, and carry out variable elimination by hand on a small one.
  • Implement filtering with a particle filter, break it by starving it of particles, and name the failure by its proper name.
  • Derive the Bellman optimality equations and explain value iteration's convergence as a contraction-mapping argument rather than as an empirical fact.
  • Say precisely what the deadly triad is and predict which of three RL setups will diverge.
  • State the frame problem without using the word "frame", and explain why probability answers it and more rules do not.
  • Assess a claim that a language model "planned" something — distinguishing the model producing a plan from the model calling a planner, and knowing what PlanBench measured.

Syllabus

M01

What "artificial intelligence" was supposed to mean

≈4 h

Every later module is an answer to the question "what should this agent do next?", and the answer depends entirely on how you defined the question. The field spent thirty years arguing about whether the goal was to imitate human thought or to act well, and the modern textbook treatment picks one — rational action — and builds everything on it. If you skip this you will spend the rest of the syllabus quietly assuming AI is about imitating people, and half the design decisions will look arbitrary.

Concepts

  • The four definitions of AI, arranged as a two-by-two — thinking versus acting, humanly versus rationally — and the consequences of choosing the acting-rationally cell
    • Acting humanly gives you the Turing test, which is an operational definition and a terrible engineering specification: it rewards deception, punishes speed, and gives no partial credit
    • Thinking humanly gives you cognitive science, which is a different field with different standards of evidence
    • Acting rationally gives you a performance measure you can optimise against and prove things about, at the cost of saying nothing about whether the system resembles a mind
  • The agent abstraction — percept sequence, agent function, agent program — and the PEAS description (performance measure, environment, actuators, sensors) as the first thing you write about any system
    • The performance measure lives outside the agent, and specifying it badly is the origin of nearly every alignment failure in M21
    • Agent program versus agent function: the function is a lookup table over all percept sequences, the program is the thing that has to fit in memory
  • Environment properties as the taxonomy that predicts which algorithm applies — fully versus partially observable, deterministic versus stochastic, episodic versus sequential, static versus dynamic, discrete versus continuous, single- versus multi-agent
    • Each property maps to a chapter: partially observable plus stochastic is why M16 needs POMDPs, multi-agent is why M07 and M18 exist
    • The classification is of your model, not of reality — a poker table is not really stochastic, it is partially observable, and choosing which fiction to adopt is a modelling decision with consequences
  • Rationality versus omniscience, perfection and success — a rational agent maximises expected performance given what it knows, so a rational decision can have a terrible outcome without having been wrong
    • Bounded rationality (Simon) and satisficing: the optimal decision procedure is usually not computable, so the real target is a good decision within a compute budget
    • Reflex, model-based, goal-based, utility-based and learning agents as a ladder of increasing internal state, each rung buying a capability the one below cannot express
  • The physical symbol system hypothesis (Newell and Simon, 1976) as the field's founding empirical bet, stated precisely enough to be falsifiable
    • What it actually claims: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action
    • Why the deep learning era is evidence against "necessary" but not obviously against "sufficient", which is a distinction most retellings drop

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapters 1 and 2 in full. Chapter 2's environment taxonomy is the one page of this book you will return to most.
  • Computing Machinery and Intelligence — read the whole paper, including the nine objections Turing raises and answers. It is much stranger and better than its summaries.
  • Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search — the Newell and Simon Turing lecture, for the physical symbol system hypothesis in the authors' own words.
  • On our best behaviour — Levesque's IJCAI award lecture, on why the Turing test rewards the wrong thing and what Winograd schemas were meant to fix.
  • The Quest for Artificial Intelligence — Chapters 1–5, for what the founders thought they were doing, written by someone who was there.
  • CS 188: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — lecture 1 and the course's framing; the Berkeley course is the closest thing this material has to a standard delivery.
  • MIT 6.034: Artificial Intelligence — lecture 1, for Winston's version of the same framing; he is blunter than AIMA about what the field promised and did not deliver.
  • The Winograd Schema Challenge collection — read the design criteria, not the examples — they specify what a test that resists gaming has to do.

Practice

Write full PEAS descriptions and environment classifications for three systems: a chess engine, a warehouse robot, and one AI product you have actually used this month. For the third, write the performance measure the vendor optimises and the performance measure you would have specified, and note where they differ. You have done this properly when the two differ in at least one way that would change the system's behaviour.

Where people go wrong

Reading "rational" as "optimal" or as "logical". A rational agent maximises expected performance given its knowledge and its compute budget — so a rational agent can be beaten by a lucky one, can act on a false belief that was well-supported, and can deliberately stop thinking to act in time. The whole of M15 is an attempt to make this precise, and readers who arrive there still equating rationality with correctness find utility theory baffling.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: take a system someone describes as "an AI agent", classify its environment on all six axes, and use that classification to predict which algorithm family from this syllabus it must be using — and then say what would have to change about the environment to make a simpler family sufficient.

M02

Search: the first hammer, and the four questions

≈6 h

M01 established that an agent has to choose an action; search is the first and most general way to choose one, and it is the substrate under planning (M10), games (M07) and every solver in M05 and M06. The specific thing to take from this module is not the algorithms — they are two dozen lines each — but the habit of interrogating any search procedure with the same four questions, which is the analytic move the whole field runs on.

Concepts

  • Problem formulation as the actual skill — initial state, actions, transition model, goal test, path cost — and the fact that choosing the state representation decides the difficulty before any algorithm runs
    • Abstraction: the 8-puzzle's states are not board images, and a route-finding state is not a car; every removed detail is a claim that the removed detail cannot affect the solution
    • A worked contrast: the same delivery problem as a state space over vehicle positions versus as a set-partitioning problem, and how the choice determines which literature you end up in
  • Tree search versus graph search, and the closed list (explored set) as the difference between them
    • Why tree search on a graph with cycles does not terminate, and why the fix costs memory proportional to the states seen
    • The frontier as a priority queue: every uninformed algorithm in this module is the same code with a different queue discipline, which is worth implementing once to see
  • The four questions to ask of any search algorithm — completeness, optimality, time complexity, space complexity — expressed in branching factor b, depth d and maximum depth m
    • Breadth-first: complete, optimal only for uniform step costs, O(b^d) in both time and space — and it is the space that kills it
    • Uniform-cost (Dijkstra with a goal test): complete and optimal for non-negative costs, and the reason the goal test happens at expansion rather than generation
    • Depth-first: O(bm) space, which is its entire justification; not complete on infinite spaces, never optimal
    • Iterative deepening: DFS's space with BFS's guarantees, at a repeated-work cost that is a constant factor because the last level dominates the sum
  • Bidirectional search, and why it is harder to use than it looks — you need a predecessor function and a meeting test, and the theoretical b^(d/2) is often eaten by both
    • Where it genuinely wins: symmetric domains with a single known goal state, such as puzzle solving

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 3 sections 3.1–3.4. Read the table of algorithm properties at the end and reconstruct each row rather than memorising it.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents — Chapter 3, which develops the generic search algorithm more explicitly than AIMA and is free to read online.
  • CS 188: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — the uninformed-search lectures and, more importantly, the discussion-section worksheets, which are where the node-counting intuition comes from.
  • The Berkeley Pac-Man projects — Project 1 is the canonical implementation exercise for this module and takes a few hours.
  • The Berkeley AI Materials site — the version-stable home of the projects, if the current semester's page has moved.
  • MIT 6.034 lecture videos — lectures 2 and 3, which work the search algorithms by hand on the board.
  • aima-python — read search.py after implementing your own; the generic-search abstraction there is the one worth copying.

Practice

Implement BFS, DFS, uniform-cost and iterative deepening behind one interface, with a counter for nodes generated and nodes expanded. Run all four on the 8-puzzle from a scrambled start and on a small road network. You are done when your node-expansion counts match the theoretical ordering, and when you can explain the one case where they do not — which will be uniform-cost on a graph with many equal-cost paths.

Where people go wrong

Testing for the goal when a node is generated rather than when it is expanded, in uniform-cost search. It looks like a harmless optimisation and it silently destroys optimality: the first goal node you generate is the one at the end of the cheapest edge you have looked at, not the one at the end of the cheapest path. The same bug reappears in A* in M03, where it is even easier to miss because the answers are usually still good.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the space complexity of each algorithm without looking, and then answer the question that actually matters — given a problem with branching factor 10 and solution depth 12, which of the four can you run on a laptop, and what changes if the depth is 30?

M03

Heuristics, A*, and the proof that makes it work

≈7 h

M02's algorithms are blind, and blind search runs out of memory on anything real. A* is the single most-used algorithm in this syllabus and the one most often used incorrectly, because its guarantee rests on a condition people routinely violate and then report that it "still works fine". This module is where you learn to construct heuristics deliberately rather than guess them, which is the transferable skill — the same relaxation argument reappears as the delete relaxation in M10.

Concepts

  • Greedy best-first versus A*: f(n) = g(n) + h(n), and the precise role of each term
    • Greedy search is fast and wrong; A* is uniform-cost search that has been told where the goal is
    • A* with h = 0 is uniform-cost; A* with a perfect h expands only the solution path — the whole design space lies between those two
  • Admissibility (h never overestimates) and consistency (h(n) ≤ c(n, n′) + h(n′), the triangle inequality), and why the two conditions are not the same
    • Consistency implies admissibility; the converse fails, and the counterexample is small enough to draw
    • Tree-search A* is optimal with admissibility alone; graph-search A* needs consistency, or it needs to reopen closed nodes — this is the most commonly skipped detail in the field
    • The optimality proof: suppose a suboptimal goal G₂ is expanded; there is a node n on the optimal path still on the frontier; f(n) ≤ C* < f(G₂); contradiction. Locate exactly where admissibility is used and where consistency is used
  • Constructing heuristics rather than guessing them
    • Relaxation: drop a precondition from the problem and solve the easier problem exactly — the cost of that exact solution is admissible by construction, which is a theorem and not a coincidence
    • Manhattan distance and misplaced tiles as two relaxations of the same puzzle, with the dominance relation between them
    • Pattern databases (Culberson and Schaeffer): precompute exact costs for a subproblem, look them up; additive/disjoint PDBs and why the additivity condition matters
    • Landmark heuristics and the ALT trick used in road networks
    • Dominance and effective branching factor as the measurements that decide between two candidate heuristics
  • Living within a memory budget — IDA*, recursive best-first search, SMA*, and the practical alternative of giving up optimality on purpose
    • Weighted A* with f = g + w·h: bounded suboptimality by a factor of w, which is a guarantee rather than a hope
    • Anytime A* and anytime repairing A*, which produce a plan immediately and improve it while you wait
  • What actually beats A* in production route planning — contraction hierarchies and hub labelling — and why the answer is preprocessing rather than a better heuristic
    • The lesson generalises: when the graph is fixed and queries are many, moving work offline beats improving the online search

Work through

  • A Formal Basis for the Heuristic Determination of Minimum Cost Paths — the 1968 original. Short, readable, and the source of the admissibility condition in the authors' own terms.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 3 sections 3.5–3.6, including the pattern-database material, which many courses skip.
  • CS 188: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — the informed-search lectures; the consistency-versus-admissibility treatment here is unusually careful.
  • The Berkeley Pac-Man projects — Project 1's corners and food-search heuristics, which force you to design an admissible heuristic under adversarial grading.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents — Chapter 3's treatment of branch-and-bound, which is the same idea arriving from the optimisation side.
  • Depth-First Iterative-Deepening: An Optimal Admissible Tree Search — Korf's IDA*, plus the analysis of why repeated work costs only a constant factor.
  • Pattern Databases — Culberson and Schaeffer's paper — read it before building the pattern database in the practice exercise.
  • AIMA exercises — the Chapter 3 proof exercises; they are the check on whether you can actually reproduce the optimality argument.

Practice

Solve the 15-puzzle with A* under three heuristics: misplaced tiles, Manhattan distance, and a 6-tile pattern database you build yourself by breadth-first search backwards from the goal. Record nodes expanded and wall-clock time for twenty random solvable instances. You are done when the PDB expands at least an order of magnitude fewer nodes than Manhattan, and when you can explain why it is still admissible despite being enormously more informed.

Where people go wrong

Using an inadmissible heuristic — usually a scaled or hand-tuned one — and reporting that A* "works fine". It does work: it returns a path, quickly. What it no longer returns is the optimal path, and it will not tell you when it has failed to. If you deliberately trade optimality for speed, weighted A* is the version that at least bounds the damage by a stated factor. The second, subtler version of this trap is running graph-search A* with a merely admissible heuristic and never reopening closed nodes, which loses optimality without anyone having intended a tradeoff at all.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: reproduce the A* optimality proof at a whiteboard, then answer "which line breaks if h is admissible but not consistent, and what code change repairs it?" — and separately, given a new problem, produce an admissible heuristic by naming the constraint you relaxed.

M04

When systematic search is hopeless

≈5 h

A* from M03 keeps a frontier, and on a problem with a million variables the frontier does not fit anywhere. Local search abandons completeness and optimality in exchange for constant memory, and for a large class of real problems it is the only thing that runs at all. This module also installs the honest assessment of genetic algorithms, which is otherwise a topic people carry bad intuitions about for years.

Concepts

  • The shift in framing: from a path to a goal, to a state that is itself the answer — and the consequence that memory becomes O(1) and the notion of "optimal" becomes local
    • The landscape metaphor made precise: state space plus objective function, with plateaus, ridges, shoulders and local optima as named failure geometries
    • Complete-state formulations: for n-queens you start with all queens placed, not with an empty board, which is the move that makes local search applicable at all
  • Hill climbing and its repairs — sideways moves, stochastic hill climbing, first-choice, random restarts — and the analysis of when restarts suffice
    • If a restart succeeds with probability p, expected restarts is 1/p; this turns "it gets stuck" into a cost you can compute
  • Simulated annealing: the Metropolis acceptance rule, the temperature schedule, and the convergence theorem that requires a schedule too slow to use
    • Accepting a worsening move with probability e^(−ΔE/T) is what lets it escape local optima; T → 0 turns it back into hill climbing
    • The theoretical guarantee (logarithmic cooling reaches the global optimum with probability 1) is real and useless — every practical schedule is geometric and unguaranteed, and knowing this stops you looking for the schedule that fixes it
  • Local beam search, and why it is not just k parallel hill climbs — the population concentrates, which is both the point and the failure mode
    • Stochastic beam search as the fix for concentration, and its resemblance to natural selection
  • Genetic algorithms, assessed honestly — crossover, mutation, selection, and the conditions under which crossover actually contributes
    • Crossover helps when the representation makes meaningful sub-solutions contiguous, which is a strong assumption most encodings violate
    • On most benchmarks a well-tuned GA is matched by simulated annealing or by iterated local search with far fewer knobs; the no-free-lunch theorems are the formal statement of why no metaheuristic dominates
  • Local search in continuous spaces, and the bridge to gradient methods — where the objective is differentiable, everything in this module is superseded by the optimisation material in the machine-learning pages
    • Online search and LRTA* for the case where you cannot even simulate the environment before acting

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 4 sections 4.1–4.2, plus 4.5 on online search.
  • A New Method for Solving Hard Satisfiability Problems — the GSAT paper, which is the moment local search was shown to beat systematic search on a problem everyone assumed needed completeness.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents — its local-search chapter, which is more careful than AIMA about the distinction between the search space and the objective.
  • Handbook of Satisfiability — the stochastic local search chapter, for how these ideas look after thirty years of engineering.

Practice

Solve n-queens for n = 1,000,000 with min-conflicts local search, and time it. Then try backtracking with MRV on the same instance and watch it fail to terminate. Write down the number of steps min-conflicts took as a multiple of n — for this problem it is famously near-constant, and seeing that yourself is the point of the exercise.

Where people go wrong

Reaching for a genetic algorithm because the problem "sounds evolutionary". A GA has four or five interacting hyperparameters and a representation choice, and crossover only earns its keep if that representation puts related decisions next to each other. Before writing one, run random-restart hill climbing and simulated annealing as baselines. If the GA does not beat them convincingly, you have bought a great deal of tuning surface for nothing — and the literature is full of published GAs that were never compared to a restart baseline.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe a problem you would solve with A* and one you would solve with local search, and justify each by an explicit statement about the size of the frontier and about whether you need a proof of optimality — not by which algorithm you find more elegant.

M05

Constraint satisfaction: search that can see inside the state

≈6 h

In M02–M04 the state was an opaque blob and the algorithm could only expand it. A CSP exposes the state's internal structure — variables, domains, constraints — and every technique in this module is a way of exploiting that structure to prune before searching. This is the direct ancestor of M06's SAT solvers, and the framing that lets you recognise scheduling, rostering and configuration problems as one problem with a mature solver ecosystem.

Concepts

  • The CSP formulation — variables, domains, constraint scopes and relations — and the immediate payoff that a goal test can now be decomposed
    • Binary versus n-ary constraints, and the dual-graph transformation that makes everything binary at a cost
    • Constraint graph, and the fact that its structure alone predicts tractability, which is the deepest idea in the module
  • Backtracking search, and the three decisions that turn it from useless to competitive
    • Variable ordering: minimum remaining values (fail-first), with degree heuristic as the tie-break
    • Value ordering: least-constraining-value (fail-last), and why the asymmetry between the two heuristics is not a mistake
    • Inference during search: forward checking versus maintaining arc consistency, and the tradeoff between pruning power and per-node cost
  • Constraint propagation as inference in its own right — node, arc, path and k-consistency
    • AC-3 (Mackworth, 1977): the queue of arcs, the revise step, and the O(ed³) bound with e edges and domain size d
    • What arc consistency does not give you: an arc-consistent CSP can still be unsatisfiable, and the 3-colouring of a triangle with domains {R,G,B} is the two-line counterexample
    • Global constraints — alldifferent propagated by bipartite matching (Régin) — as the reason CP solvers beat generic backtracking on real scheduling problems
  • Intelligent backtracking: conflict sets, backjumping, conflict-directed backjumping, and no-good learning
    • This is CDCL in embryo — M06 takes exactly this idea and industrialises it
  • Structure exploitation, where the theory pays
    • A tree-structured constraint graph is solvable in O(nd²) — directional arc consistency then a single sweep, no backtracking at all
    • Cutset conditioning and tree decomposition: complexity exponential in treewidth rather than in n, which is the same parameter that governs Bayesian-network inference in M13

Work through

  • Consistency in Networks of Relations — Mackworth's original, which introduces arc consistency and AC-3 and is still the clearest statement of what propagation buys.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 6 in full, including section 6.5 on structure, which is the part courses drop for time.
  • Handbook of Constraint Programming — Chapters 3 and 4 for propagation and search; this is the reference when AIMA's treatment runs out.
  • MiniZinc — work through the tutorial and model two problems in it; the modelling language forces the CSP framing in a way that reading does not.
  • OR-Tools CP-SAT solver — the scheduling examples, which show what a CP solver with a SAT engine underneath does to problems that defeat hand-written search.
  • AISpace2: interactive tools for learning AI — step AC-3 through a graph-colouring problem and watch domains shrink; propagation is much easier to see than to read.
  • The MiniZinc Challenge — read two winning models before writing your own — modelling is where the performance is.

Practice

Write a Sudoku solver twice: once as plain backtracking over cells in row order, once with MRV plus degree tie-breaking plus AC-3 propagation. Instrument both with a node counter and run them on a published set of hard 17-clue puzzles. You are done when the second version solves every puzzle with node counts in the tens rather than the millions — and when you can point at the specific puzzle feature that makes the naive version blow up.

Where people go wrong

Modelling a genuine CSP as a generic state-space search — encoding a partial assignment as an opaque state and handing it to A*. Every technique in this module becomes inexpressible the moment you do that, because they all require the algorithm to see the variables. The tell is a search that explores structurally identical states in many different orders: a CSP formulation makes that commutativity explicit and searches each assignment once.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a tree-structured CSP needs no backtracking, and then use that to explain what cutset conditioning is buying and what it costs — in terms of the same treewidth parameter you will meet again in M13.

M06

SAT: the search algorithm that ate industry

≈7 h

M05's no-good learning is a good idea that a research community spent twenty-five years engineering into something that routinely disposes of instances with millions of variables — a problem that is NP-complete and was, in 1990, considered hopeless in practice. SAT is the strongest available demonstration that worst-case complexity says little about real instances, and it is the engine underneath modern planning (M10), verification, and much of CP (M05). If you learn one solver deeply in this syllabus, learn this one.

Concepts

  • The problem and its encodings — CNF, the Tseitin transformation, and why "convert to CNF" is not exponential if you do it correctly
    • Naive distribution blows up; Tseitin introduces a variable per subformula and stays linear, at the cost of equisatisfiability rather than equivalence
    • Encoding matters more than solver choice: cardinality constraints encoded three different ways differ by orders of magnitude in solve time
  • DPLL (1962) — unit propagation, pure literal elimination, splitting — as the backtracking search of M05 specialised to Boolean variables
    • Unit propagation is where essentially all the work happens, and its efficiency is the thing everything else is built around
  • CDCL, the 1996–2001 revolution, mechanism by mechanism
    • The implication graph: decisions, implied literals, decision levels, and the conflict node
    • Conflict analysis and the first-UIP cut: which cut of the implication graph you take determines the learned clause, and 1UIP is the empirical winner
    • The learned clause is a logical consequence of the formula, which is why adding it is sound — this is the sentence to be able to say out loud
    • Non-chronological backjumping: return to the second-highest decision level in the learned clause, not to the previous decision
    • VSIDS activity scores, phase saving, and restarts — the heuristics that separate a solver that works from one that does not
    • Two-watched-literals as the data structure that makes propagation cheap and backtracking free
    • Clause database reduction and the LBD/glue metric for deciding what to keep
  • Why it works when theory says it should not — the random 3-SAT phase transition at a clause-to-variable ratio near 4.26, and the observation that industrial instances are nothing like random ones
    • Structured instances have community structure and small backdoors; hardness of random instances near the threshold is not evidence about hardness of your instance
    • Proof complexity as the honest limit: CDCL is resolution, and resolution has exponential lower bounds on pigeonhole formulas, which is why your solver will genuinely never do PHP(n)
  • Beyond plain SAT — DRAT proof logging, incremental solving under assumptions, MaxSAT, #SAT, and SMT as SAT plus theory solvers
    • Where this shipped: hardware equivalence checking, bounded model checking, package dependency resolution, symbolic execution, and the Boolean Pythagorean triples proof

Work through

  • A Machine Program for Theorem-Proving — the 1962 DPLL paper. Two pages, and the algorithm is still recognisable in every modern solver.
  • GRASP: A Search Algorithm for Propositional Satisfiability — where conflict-driven clause learning is introduced. Read the implication-graph figures carefully.
  • Chaff: Engineering an Efficient SAT Solver — watched literals and VSIDS, and a good demonstration that in this field the engineering is the contribution.
  • Handbook of Satisfiability — Chapters 3 and 4 (CDCL, look-ahead). This is the reference work; read it after the two papers, not before.
  • Armin Biere's talks and tutorials — his Simons Institute talk A Personal History of Practical SAT Solving is the best single account of why the solvers got fast.
  • Z3 — use it as an SMT solver on a small verification problem, to see what theory reasoning adds on top of the Boolean core.
  • The SAT Competition — the benchmark archive, for instances that are not toys.
  • Theoretical Foundations of SAT/SMT Solving — the Simons Institute programme's boot-camp talks, for the theory behind the engineering.
  • The SAT Association — the community's home; the summer-school materials are the best structured route past this module.

Practice

Implement DPLL with unit propagation, confirm it on small random 3-SAT, then add conflict analysis with the first-UIP cut, learned clauses and non-chronological backjumping. Benchmark both versions against a competition solver on three families: random 3-SAT at ratio 4.26, the pigeonhole formulas PHP(n) for n up to 10, and one structured instance from the competition archive. You are done when your CDCL version beats your DPLL version by orders of magnitude on the structured instance, and when all three solvers fail on the pigeonhole formulas — that agreement is the practical face of the resolution lower bound.

Where people go wrong

Concluding from NP-completeness that a SAT encoding is a dead end, and hand-writing a bespoke search instead. For structured industrial instances the opposite is true: thirty years of solver engineering will comfortably beat your bespoke search, and it comes with proof logging and incremental solving for free. The complementary error is treating the encoding as an afterthought — solver choice typically changes runtime by a small factor, while a better encoding of the same constraints changes it by orders of magnitude.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: draw an implication graph for a small conflict, take the first-UIP cut, write down the learned clause, state the level it backjumps to, and — the part that matters — explain why adding that clause cannot remove any satisfying assignment.

M07

Games: search when something is searching back

≈7 h

Every search so far assumed the world was indifferent. Introduce an adversary and the value of a node stops being a cost and becomes the outcome of a fixed point over two optimising players — which is a genuinely different object, and the source of both minimax and, forty years later, the search half of AlphaGo. This module also carries the most instructive result in the syllabus about the relationship between search and learning, which M22 returns to.

Concepts

  • Minimax over a game tree, and alpha-beta pruning as the observation that most of the tree cannot change the answer
    • Alpha-beta returns exactly the minimax value — it is a pruning, not an approximation, and this is worth proving to yourself once
    • With perfect move ordering the effective branching factor is √b, so alpha-beta searches twice as deep in the same time; with worst-case ordering it saves nothing at all
    • Move ordering as the whole game in practice: killer moves, history heuristic, iterative deepening reusing the previous iteration's principal variation, transposition tables
  • Cutting off search — evaluation functions, quiescence search and the horizon effect
    • An evaluation function is a claim about the expected outcome from a position, and a linear weighted-feature one was state of the art for thirty years
    • Quiescence: never evaluate a position in the middle of a capture sequence; the horizon effect is what happens when you do
    • Deep Blue as engineering rather than insight — custom silicon, an opening book, an evaluation function tuned by grandmasters, and 200 million positions per second
  • Monte Carlo tree search, which replaces the hand-built evaluation function with statistics from random play
    • The four phases: selection, expansion, simulation, backpropagation
    • UCT (Kocsis and Szepesvári): treat each node's child choice as a multi-armed bandit and select by UCB1 — this is where the exploration constant comes from and why it is not arbitrary
    • Anytime, aheuristic and asymmetric: MCTS needs no domain knowledge, grows the tree unevenly toward promising lines, and can be stopped whenever you run out of time
    • Why it broke Go when alpha-beta could not: branching factor ~250 and no usable hand-written evaluation function
  • The synthesis — AlphaGo, AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero, MuZero — read as a sequence of removals rather than additions
    • AlphaGo: MCTS plus a policy network to narrow the search and a value network to shorten it, bootstrapped from human games
    • AlphaGo Zero: the human games removed; self-play only, one network with two heads
    • AlphaZero: the Go-specific parts removed; the same algorithm plays chess and shogi
    • MuZero: the rules removed; the model is learned, and planning happens in a latent space that need not resemble the board
    • The transferable lesson is that search and learned evaluation are complements — the search provides the improvement operator that generates the training targets
  • Imperfect information, where minimax simply does not apply
    • Why you cannot search a poker game tree the way you search chess: your optimal strategy depends on your opponent's beliefs about your private cards, so strategies must be stochastic and evaluated in expectation over information sets
    • Counterfactual regret minimisation and its convergence to a Nash equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games; abstraction and subgame re-solving as the engineering that made it fit
    • The milestones and what each actually claimed: heads-up limit hold'em solved (2015), Libratus beating professionals at no-limit (2018), Pluribus at six-player (2019) — where the theoretical guarantee stops applying at more than two players
  • Stochastic games and expectiminimax, for backgammon and anything with dice
    • Why alpha-beta pruning weakens dramatically once chance nodes intervene

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 5 in full. The alpha-beta worked example repays doing by hand once.
  • Bandit Based Monte-Carlo Planning — the UCT paper. Short, and it shows the bandit argument that justifies the selection rule.
  • Deep Blue — the retrospective by the team, worth reading for how much of the result was search engineering rather than chess knowledge.
  • Mastering the Game of Go with Deep Neural Networks and Tree Search — the AlphaGo paper; focus on how the networks are used inside the search rather than instead of it.
  • Mastering Atari, Go, Chess and Shogi by Planning with a Learned Model — MuZero, for the version where the rules themselves are learned.
  • Superhuman AI for Multiplayer Poker — Pluribus; read the discussion of why six-player has no equilibrium guarantee and why it worked anyway.
  • AlphaGo — The Movie — the Lee Sedol match documentary, which is the best available record of what move 37 and move 78 actually were.
  • Efficient Selectivity and Backup Operators in Monte-Carlo Tree Search — Coulom's paper, the other origin of MCTS, arriving from Go rather than from bandit theory.
  • Mastering the Game of Go Without Human Knowledge — AlphaGo Zero — read it as the removal of the human game database, and note what that did to strength.
  • A General Reinforcement Learning Algorithm That Masters Chess, Shogi and Go Through Self-Play — AlphaZero, plus the criticisms of its match conditions against Stockfish.
  • Heads-Up Limit Hold'em Poker Is Solved — for the precise definition of “solved” as bounded exploitability, which is the whole result.
  • Superhuman AI for Heads-Up No-Limit Poker: Libratus Beats Top Professionals — for nested subgame re-solving — search at decision time, in an imperfect-information game.

Practice

Write a Connect Four player three ways: plain minimax to fixed depth, alpha-beta with iterative deepening and a transposition table, and MCTS with UCT. Have them play a round robin of 100 games each way with a one-second-per-move budget, and record nodes visited. You are done when alpha-beta searches at least four plies deeper than plain minimax in the same second, and when you can say — from your own logs, not from reading — at which time budget MCTS overtakes alpha-beta.

Where people go wrong

Believing MCTS requires a neural network because AlphaGo had one. MCTS with random rollouts and no learned components is a complete, working algorithm and was strong at Go for eight years before AlphaGo existed. The networks changed the quality of the priors and the evaluation, not the search. Getting this backwards leads people to skip the search half entirely and be surprised when a policy network alone plays badly — which is precisely the failure mode M22 examines in language-model agents.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why alpha-beta gives no benefit at all under worst-case move ordering, and separately explain why counterfactual regret minimisation is needed for poker when minimax was enough for chess — in terms of information sets, not in terms of "poker has hidden cards".

M08

Propositional logic, and what it means for a machine to know something

≈6 h

Everything so far has been procedural: the agent's knowledge is whatever its code implies. A knowledge-based agent instead stores sentences and derives new ones, which decouples what it knows from how it acts — and lets you add a fact without editing an algorithm. That separation is the central claim of symbolic AI, and this module is where you meet it in the setting simple enough to prove things about. It is also the direct route into M06's SAT solvers from the other side: they are the same objects, arrived at from logic rather than from search.

Concepts

  • Syntax, semantics and the definition of entailment — α ⊨ β holds when β is true in every model where α is true
    • Entailment is about models, not about proof; the distinction between ⊨ (semantic) and ⊢ (syntactic, relative to an inference procedure) is the axis the rest of the module turns on
    • Soundness: everything derivable is entailed. Completeness: everything entailed is derivable. Both are properties of an inference procedure, not of the logic
    • The deduction theorem and the reduction of entailment to unsatisfiability, which is what makes M06's solvers usable as reasoning engines
  • Two ways to answer an entailment question, with opposite cost profiles
    • Model checking: enumerate models. Exponential and stupid, but complete and trivially correct — and, once you replace enumeration with a modern SAT solver, actually the winning approach
    • Theorem proving: apply inference rules. Resolution refutation on CNF is sound and complete for propositional logic, and the proof of completeness (via the resolution closure and ground resolution theorem) is worth following once
  • Restricted forms bought for tractability — definite clauses, Horn clauses, forward and backward chaining
    • Horn-clause entailment is decidable in linear time, and that single fact is why Prolog, Datalog and production-rule systems exist
    • Forward chaining is data-driven and computes everything derivable; backward chaining is goal-driven and computes only what the query needs — the same distinction reappears as progression versus regression in M10
  • The wumpus world as the standard worked example, and what it demonstrates
    • How a partially observable environment becomes a sequence of entailment queries
    • Where it breaks: the agent can prove a square is safe or prove it is unsafe, but very often can prove neither — and has no vocabulary for "probably safe". That gap is the entire motivation for M12 and M13
  • Effective propositional inference in practice — this is where M06 rejoins
    • Encoding a knowledge base and a negated query as a SAT instance, and reading UNSAT as "entailed"
    • Why nobody writes a bespoke propositional theorem prover any more

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 7 in full, including the wumpus-world development.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents — its propositional-logic chapter, which builds the proof procedures more slowly than AIMA and is free online.
  • Knowledge Representation and Reasoning — Chapters 1–4, for the deliberate case that representation is a subject in its own right rather than a preliminary.
  • Logic and Artificial Intelligence — the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, for the historical argument about what logic was supposed to do for AI.

Practice

Build a wumpus-world agent twice. First, with a hand-written propositional knowledge base and a resolution prover you write yourself. Second, by encoding the same knowledge as CNF and calling a SAT solver for each "is this square provably safe?" query. Run both on twenty random 4×4 worlds. You are done when the two agree on every query, the SAT version is dramatically faster, and you can name at least one board position where both agents are stuck because neither safety nor danger is entailed.

Where people go wrong

Reading entailment as material implication. "KB ⊨ α" is a statement about the relationship between two sentences across all models; "KB ⇒ α" is itself a sentence that may be true in some models and false in others. The confusion surfaces as an agent that concludes things it should not, and it is one of the few places in this syllabus where a purely notational misunderstanding produces silently wrong behaviour rather than an error.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: give an example of an inference procedure that is sound but incomplete, and one that is complete but unsound, and say which of the two you would rather ship and why — and then explain how model checking and theorem proving can both be complete while having completely different cost profiles.

M09

First-order logic, knowledge representation, and forty years of ontologies

≈8 h

M08's propositional logic cannot say "every square adjacent to a pit is breezy" — it can only say it separately about each square, which means the knowledge base grows with the world. First-order logic buys quantification, and the price is that entailment goes from decidable to semi-decidable. This module is also where you meet the field's most expensive empirical lesson: several decades and several fortunes were spent on hand-built knowledge bases, and the record of what that produced is something you should know first-hand rather than as folklore.

Concepts

  • Syntax and semantics — constants, predicates, functions, variables, quantifiers — and models as (domain, interpretation) pairs
    • The two quantifier mistakes everyone makes once: ∀x (King(x) ∧ Person(x)) instead of ∀x (King(x) ⇒ Person(x)), and ∃x (King(x) ⇒ Person(x)) instead of ∃x (King(x) ∧ Person(x))
    • Equality, unique-names and domain-closure assumptions, and how much they quietly do
  • Inference — the reduction to propositional logic and why it is a dead end, then the machinery that replaces it
    • Propositionalisation via Herbrand's theorem: complete, but generates infinitely many ground sentences when function symbols are present
    • Unification and the most general unifier; the occurs check and why implementations skip it
    • Generalised modus ponens, forward chaining (with the pattern-matching and incremental problems that Rete solves) and backward chaining as Prolog's execution model
    • Resolution with unification, Skolemisation, and the completeness result — plus the fact that first-order entailment is only semi-decidable, so a prover on a non-entailed query may simply never stop
  • Representation as an engineering discipline rather than an afterthought
    • Categories, objects and the substance/individual distinction; events, fluents and the situation and event calculi
    • Mental objects and propositional attitudes, which is where the logic starts to strain
    • The closed-world assumption and negation as failure — Prolog's semantics differ from classical logic here in a way that surprises people in production
  • Description logics and the tractability bargain
    • Deliberately restricted fragments (concepts, roles, subsumption) chosen so that classification is decidable and often polynomial
    • OWL, RDF and the Semantic Web's rise and retreat; what actually survived — schema.org, Wikidata, industrial knowledge graphs, and SNOMED CT in clinical systems
  • CYC as the field's longest-running experiment, and what it is evidence for
    • Thirty-plus years, millions of hand-authored assertions, a proprietary inference engine; read Lenat's own account before anyone else's
    • The honest reading: it demonstrated that commonsense knowledge is enormous and that hand-authoring it does not converge — which is an important negative result, not a scandal

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapters 8, 9 and 10. Chapter 10's ontology material is where most readers stop and where the interesting content starts.
  • A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle — Robinson's 1965 paper, the origin of unification and resolution.
  • Knowledge Representation and Reasoning — Chapters 5–9 on FOL and description logics; Brachman and Levesque are the authority on the tractability bargain.
  • CYC: A Large-Scale Investment in Knowledge Infrastructure — Lenat's own account, for what the project believed it was doing.
  • SWI-Prolog — do the quick-start and then write a small deductive database; Prolog is the fastest way to feel backward chaining and negation-as-failure.
  • Potassco: the Potsdam Answer Set Solving Collection — clingo's guide, so you meet answer set programming here rather than being surprised by it in M12.
  • Paradigms of Artificial Intelligence Programming: Case Studies in Common Lisp — Chapter 11: write the unifier. Nothing else makes first-order inference concrete this quickly.
  • CS 343: Artificial Intelligence — Mooney's logic and knowledge-representation slides, as a second explanation when AIMA's chapters are heavy.
  • KR: Principles of Knowledge Representation and Reasoning — browse a recent proceedings to see what the field still argues about.

Practice

Encode a small family-relations domain in Prolog — parents, siblings, ancestors, cousins — and then add a rule with left recursion and watch the query fail to terminate. Rewrite it in Datalog or clingo, where the same rule terminates, and explain the difference. You are done when you can state precisely what evaluation strategy Prolog uses and what changes in a bottom-up evaluator.

Where people go wrong

Expecting a first-order theorem prover to be a decision procedure. If the query is entailed, resolution will find the proof eventually; if it is not, the prover may run forever, and there is no fix — semi-decidability is a theorem, not an implementation limitation. This is exactly why the practical world uses restricted fragments: Horn clauses, Datalog, description logics, and the propositional encodings of M06. Choosing the weakest logic that expresses your domain is a skill, not a compromise.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why description logics deliberately give up expressive power, name a specific thing you can no longer say in one, and say what you get in exchange — with the phrase "decidable in polynomial time" attached to a specific reasoning task.

M10

Classical planning

≈7 h

M02–M04 searched over states you had to enumerate by hand; M08–M09 described the world declaratively but had no notion of acting. Planning is the join: actions are described declaratively, and the planner derives its own search space and — crucially — its own heuristic from that description. That last part is the reason this module exists as its own subject rather than as an application of M03. A planner that computes its heuristic automatically from the domain is doing something no A* implementation in M03 could do.

Concepts

  • Representation — STRIPS, ADL and PDDL: states as sets of ground fluents, actions as precondition/add/delete triples
    • The closed-world assumption doing enormous work: anything not stated is false, which is what makes state comparison cheap
    • The frame problem solved by fiat rather than by reasoning: unmentioned fluents persist. M12 explains what that fiat costs
    • Lifted versus grounded representations, and why grounding is often the memory bottleneck in real domains
  • Search directions and spaces
    • Progression (forward from the initial state) versus regression (backward from the goal), and why forward search won once good heuristics existed
    • Partial-order planning and the least-commitment tradition — historically dominant, now largely superseded, and worth understanding because HTN planning in M11 inherits from it
  • GraphPlan and the planning graph
    • Levelled proposition and action layers, mutex relations, and the fact that the graph is built in polynomial time even though planning is not
    • The planning graph as a relaxation that yields both a solution-extraction procedure and an admissible heuristic (level cost)
  • Heuristic search planning — the idea that made planners fast, and the one to take away from this module
    • The delete relaxation: throw away every delete effect, so nothing achieved is ever undone; the relaxed problem is still NP-hard to solve optimally but polynomial to solve approximately
    • h_add (assumes subgoal independence, inadmissible), h_max (admissible but weak), h_FF (extract a relaxed plan and count its actions), and the landmark heuristics — h_LM-cut being the standard admissible choice for optimal planning
    • Helpful actions and enforced hill climbing in FF, which is where the practical speed came from
    • Why the same relaxation trick from M03 reappears here, now applied automatically to a domain description rather than by a human
  • Planning as satisfiability, and the complexity picture
    • SATPLAN: bound the horizon, encode "is there a plan of length k", hand it to M06's solver, iterate on k
    • PlanSAT is PSPACE-complete; bounded PlanSAT is NP-complete — and the practical consequence is that plan length is what you pay for

Work through

  • STRIPS: A New Approach to the Application of Theorem Proving to Problem Solving — the 1971 original; the representation it introduced is still what PDDL is.
  • Fast Planning Through Planning Graph Analysis — GraphPlan. Read for the mutex conditions, which are the substance.
  • The FF Planning System: Fast Plan Generation Through Heuristic Search — the relaxed-plan heuristic and enforced hill climbing, which between them reset the field.
  • Planning as Heuristic Search — Bonet and Geffner's statement of the position that planning is just search with a derived heuristic.
  • Automated Planning and Acting — Chapters 2 and 3, the modern textbook treatment of classical planning.
  • The Fast Downward Planning System — Helmert's paper on the planner you will actually run, and its multi-valued representation.
  • planning.wiki — the AI planning and PDDL wiki — the practical PDDL reference; use it while writing your first domain.
  • Fast Downward documentation — the search-configuration reference; you will vary that string more than you vary anything else.
  • The planning.domains PDDL editor — use it for the first hour to get the syntax right before installing anything.
  • The International Planning Competition — take your test instances from the classical tracks rather than inventing them; they are calibrated.

Practice

Write two PDDL domains yourself: blocksworld, and a logistics domain with trucks, planes and packages. Run each through Fast Downward with three configurations — blind search, h_FF greedy, and A* with LM-cut. Record plan length, plan cost and search time for all six runs. You are done when you can explain why the LM-cut runs are slower but return shorter plans, and when you have found at least one instance where blind search does not terminate within your patience.

Where people go wrong

Writing a bespoke state-space search with a hand-tuned heuristic for a problem that is plainly a planning problem. The entire point of the PDDL formulation is that the heuristic is derived from the domain description, automatically, and a derived h_FF will typically beat the heuristic you would have hand-written — while also surviving a change to the domain, which yours will not. The second version of this error is writing a domain with hundreds of ground actions per state and then blaming the planner for grounding it slowly.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state exactly what the delete relaxation throws away, explain why the length of an optimal relaxed plan is an admissible heuristic for the original problem, and then explain why h_FF — which uses a relaxed plan that is not optimal — is not admissible, and why people use it anyway.

M11

Planning and acting, when the world does not hold still

≈6 h

M10's planner assumes a deterministic world, a known initial state, instantaneous actions and no other agents — and every one of those assumptions is false in the applications that actually pay for planning research. This module covers what changes when you relax them, and it is the module that explains why deployed planning systems look so different from the textbook algorithm. It is also the honest answer to "why doesn't everyone just use a planner": because the gap between a plan and an execution is where the engineering lives.

Concepts

  • Time and resources — durative actions, temporal constraint networks, and the point at which planning becomes scheduling
    • Simple temporal networks and their polynomial consistency check; disjunctive temporal problems and the jump back to NP-hardness
    • Resource-constrained project scheduling, and the observation that once resources are the binding constraint, CP and MIP solvers from M05 usually beat planners
    • The plan/schedule split as an architectural decision, and why many deployed systems plan and schedule in separate passes
  • Hierarchical task networks — planning by refinement rather than by search over primitive actions
    • Tasks, methods and decompositions; HTN planning is strictly more expressive than STRIPS and is undecidable in general
    • Why industry uses HTNs disproportionately: the method library encodes how a human expert wants the job done, so the plans are acceptable as well as valid
  • Acting under uncertainty about the state
    • Conformant planning: no observations at all, so the plan must work from every possible initial state — search over belief states
    • Contingent planning: sensing actions and branches, producing a policy rather than a sequence
    • The complexity cost of both, and why probabilistic planning is undecidable in the infinite-horizon partially observable case — the result that motivates M16's approximations
  • Execution, monitoring and repair — the loop that actual systems run
    • Action monitoring, plan monitoring and goal monitoring, as three different things to check
    • Replanning versus plan repair, and the honest finding that replanning from scratch is usually competitive because modern planners are fast
    • Continual planning and the "planning and acting" framing of Ghallab, Nau and Traverso, in which deliberation is interleaved with execution rather than preceding it
  • Multi-agent planning, briefly — cooperative planning, plan merging, and the point at which you should be reading M18 instead

Work through

  • Automated Planning and Acting — Chapters 4 and 5 (deliberation with refinement methods, acting), which are the reason to prefer this book over its 2004 predecessor.
  • On the Undecidability of Probabilistic Planning and Related Stochastic Optimization Problems — the result that bounds what any of this can achieve.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 11, on time, schedules, resources and hierarchical planning.
  • Unified Planning — the Python library; use it to express a temporal problem and hand it to more than one back-end planner.
  • ICAPS Summer School on Automated Planning and Scheduling — the recorded lectures, which cover temporal and probabilistic planning at more depth than any textbook chapter.
  • Dana Nau's planning lectures and course materials — the HTN material, explained by one of the people who formalised it.

Practice

Take your logistics domain from M10 and put it in a loop: generate a plan, execute one action, then with probability 0.2 corrupt the resulting state (a truck breaks down, a package is misrouted), and replan. Log total actions executed against the length of the original plan across fifty episodes. Then implement a contingent version that senses the truck's status before committing. You are done when you can say, from your own numbers, at what failure probability the contingent plan starts beating replanning.

Where people go wrong

Assuming that because replanning is cheap, uncertainty needs no special treatment. Replanning works when failures are detectable, recoverable and independent — and fails badly when an early action forecloses options you will need later. The canonical case is a plan that consumes an irreplaceable resource on a branch the agent would not have taken had it known what it would learn in step three. That is precisely what contingent planning, and later the POMDP formulation in M16, exist to handle.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe a domain in which replanning is adequate and one in which it is not, and characterise the difference in terms of irreversible actions and the value of information — the same value-of-information notion that M15 makes quantitative.

M12

Why logic alone was never going to be enough

≈5 h

M08–M11 built an agent that reasons from sentences it holds to be true. This module is the argument, made carefully rather than dismissively, that the approach cannot be completed — and it is placed here, before probability, on purpose. If you meet Bayesian networks as "the next chapter" they look like an alternative notation. If you meet them as the answer to a problem you have already banged your head against, the whole of M13 lands differently. It is also the module that stops you reading the AI winters as a morality tale.

Concepts

  • The three problems McCarthy and Hayes identified, stated precisely
    • The frame problem: describing what does not change when an action occurs, without writing O(actions × fluents) axioms. STRIPS solves it by assumption in M10; a logic that reasons about change has to solve it for real
    • The qualification problem: no action's preconditions can be completely enumerated. The car starts unless the battery is flat, unless the fuel is stale, unless there is a potato in the tailpipe — the list has no end, and any finite list is wrong
    • The ramification problem: an action's indirect consequences are unbounded, so listing effects has the same defect as listing preconditions
  • Nonmonotonic reasoning — the technical response, and how far it got
    • Why classical logic is monotonic (adding premises never retracts conclusions) and why commonsense reasoning is not
    • Default logic, circumscription, autoepistemic logic, and the negation-as-failure you already met in M09
    • Answer set programming as the branch that survived and became a practical tool: stable model semantics, clingo, and real use in configuration and bioinformatics
    • Truth maintenance systems, and where their bookkeeping goes when beliefs are retracted
  • The probabilistic answer, and why it is not merely another patch
    • Degrees of belief instead of categorical truth: the qualification problem dissolves because the unlisted exceptions are absorbed into a probability rather than needing enumeration
    • The Dutch book argument (de Finetti): if your degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, there is a set of bets you will accept that loses money in every outcome. This is the closest thing to a proof that probability is the right calculus
    • What is genuinely lost: probabilistic knowledge bases are harder to author, harder to explain, and need numbers nobody has
  • The other answer — Brooks's subsumption architecture and the case that representation itself was the mistake
    • "The world is its own best model": behaviour-based robotics, no central world model, competence layers
    • Where it won (insect-level robustness, real-time reactivity, the Roomba) and where it stalled (anything requiring foresight), which is a fair summary of the argument's resolution
  • The winters, read as economics rather than as refutation
    • The Lighthill report (1973) and the combinatorial-explosion critique; the 1987–93 collapse of the expert-systems market and the LISP machine industry
    • What was actually falsified: the promised timelines and the hand-authoring strategy. What was not: the algorithms, all of which still run

Work through

  • The Frame Problem — the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, which separates the narrow technical problem from the broad philosophical one that borrowed its name.
  • Intelligence Without Representation — Brooks's 1991 paper, read as a serious position rather than a provocation.
  • The Quest for Artificial Intelligence — the chapters on expert systems and the winters, from someone who lived through both.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 12 sections 12.1–12.2, where the case for probability is made compactly.
  • Potassco: the Potsdam Answer Set Solving Collection — the clingo guide's treatment of defaults and exceptions, so nonmonotonic reasoning is something you have run rather than read about.
  • Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence — McCarthy and Hayes, sections 1–3 — where the frame problem is named.

Practice

Write a rule base in clingo that decides whether a bird flies, then extend it — penguins, ostriches, birds with broken wings, birds in cages, dead birds, birds on the ground being carried. Add exceptions until the rule base is unmaintainable, and count how many you managed. Then write the same knowledge as a small Bayesian network in M13's notation and compare what each formulation requires you to state. You are done when you can say concretely what the probabilistic version stopped you having to enumerate.

Where people go wrong

Treating the winters as evidence that symbolic AI was a mistake. What failed commercially was a business model — hand-authored rule bases sold as products, with maintenance costs that grew faster than their value — and a set of funding promises. The underlying algorithms did not fail: SAT, CP, planning and description logics all had their best decades after the second winter. Reading the history as "symbolic lost, neural won" makes M20's list of shipped systems inexplicable.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the qualification problem in one sentence without using the word "qualification", and then explain in one further sentence why adding a probability distribution addresses it while adding more rules does not.

M13

Bayesian networks: independence as a data structure

≈8 h

M12 argued that an agent needs degrees of belief. The obvious objection is cost: a joint distribution over n binary variables has 2ⁿ − 1 numbers, so the representation is dead on arrival. Bayesian networks are the answer, and the insight is worth stating plainly — conditional independence is not a convenient approximation, it is a compression scheme, and the graph is the compressed format. Every inference algorithm here exploits that structure, and the treewidth parameter that governs their cost is the same one you met in M05.

Concepts

  • From the joint to the factorisation — the chain rule plus conditional independence gives P(x₁…xₙ) = ∏ P(xᵢ | parents(xᵢ))
    • The parameter count drops from exponential in n to exponential in the maximum number of parents, which is the entire economic argument for the representation
    • A Bayesian network is a claim about independence and a set of numbers; the graph alone is a falsifiable model of the domain
  • Reading independence off the graph — d-separation, and the three canonical triples
    • Chain (X → Y → Z) and fork (X ← Y → Z): blocked by observing Y
    • Collider (X → Y ← Z): blocked until you observe Y or any descendant, at which point it opens. This is the one that is counter-intuitive and the one that generates real bugs — explaining away is a mechanism, not a curiosity
    • Markov blanket: parents, children and children's other parents; the set that renders a node independent of everything else
  • Exact inference and its complexity
    • Inference by enumeration, and why it is the naive baseline
    • Variable elimination: sum out variables in some order, with factors as the data structure; the cost is dominated by the largest intermediate factor
    • Elimination order matters enormously and finding the best one is NP-hard; the induced width of the ordering is the exponent
    • The junction tree (clique tree) algorithm as the version that answers many queries at once
    • Exact inference is #P-hard in general, so all of this is about exploiting structure, not about beating the worst case
  • Approximate inference, for when the treewidth is hopeless
    • Direct sampling, rejection sampling and its wastefulness; likelihood weighting and where its variance explodes
    • Gibbs sampling and MCMC; convergence diagnostics and the fact that "it ran for a while" is not one
    • Loopy belief propagation: no guarantees, works surprisingly often, and is the direct ancestor of the decoding algorithms in modern error correction
    • Variational methods, briefly — the deterministic alternative, developed properly in the machine-learning pages
  • Where the numbers come from, and the line between association and causation
    • Elicitation from experts, estimation from data, and the fact that structure learning is a separate and harder problem
    • A Bayes net's arrows carry no causal meaning by default — the same distribution factorises over several different DAGs. Causal reading requires extra assumptions, and Pearl's later work is where those assumptions are made explicit

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapters 12 and 13, which together are the most efficient introduction in print.
  • Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems — Pearl's 1988 book, Chapters 3 and 4; the source of d-separation and still the clearest account of why the graph is the model.
  • Local Computations with Probabilities on Graphical Structures and Their Application to Expert Systems — Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter's junction-tree paper, the algorithm behind every exact inference engine.
  • Probabilistic Graphical Models: Principles and Techniques — Chapters 3, 9 and 10 for the rigorous version; use it as the reference when AIMA compresses too far.
  • pgmpy — build the networks from the reading in it, and check your hand-computed variable elimination against its output.
  • Stanford CS221: Artificial Intelligence — Principles and Techniques — the Bayesian-network lectures, which develop variable elimination unusually concretely.

Practice

Build the classic alarm network (burglary, earthquake, alarm, two neighbours calling) by hand. Compute P(burglary | johnCalls, maryCalls) three ways: full enumeration on paper, variable elimination on paper with two different elimination orders, and by sampling. Record the largest intermediate factor for each ordering. You are done when the two orderings give the same answer with visibly different work, and when your sampler needs a number of samples you can state to match three decimal places.

Where people go wrong

Reading the arrows as causal because they were drawn that way. A distribution over three variables factorises identically over X → Y → Z, X ← Y → Z and X ← Y ← Z; the data cannot distinguish them, and no amount of inference will. Bayesian networks encode conditional independence, and causal claims require assumptions the graph does not contain. The practical symptom is a network used to answer "what happens if we intervene on X" — a question the model was never given the information to answer.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: take an unfamiliar network, decide by d-separation whether two nodes are independent given some evidence set, and — the part that tests real understanding — name an additional observation that would destroy an independence that currently holds, and explain via the collider why.

M14

Reasoning over time

≈7 h

M13's networks describe a static world. Almost every agent that acts in a real environment instead observes a stream and must maintain a belief about a state it cannot see — where the robot is, which word was spoken, whether the machine is about to fail. The structure that makes this tractable is the Markov assumption, and this module is where you see the same recursive filtering equation instantiated as an HMM, a Kalman filter and a particle filter. Those three are one idea in three costumes, and recognising that is the point.

Concepts

  • The temporal model — states, evidence, transition model P(Xₜ | Xₜ₋₁) and sensor model P(Eₜ | Xₜ), plus the stationarity assumption
    • The first-order Markov assumption as an approximation you choose, with two repairs when it is wrong: increase the order, or enlarge the state
    • Enlarging the state is almost always the better repair, and it is why a velocity term appears in tracking models
  • The four canonical inference tasks, each a different query on the same model
    • Filtering P(Xₜ | e₁:ₜ) — the recursive forward algorithm, constant work per step, which is what makes online operation possible
    • Prediction P(Xₜ₊ₖ | e₁:ₜ), and the fact that it converges to the stationary distribution as k grows, so long-range prediction from a Markov model is worthless by construction
    • Smoothing P(Xₖ | e₁:ₜ) for k < t — the forward–backward algorithm, strictly more accurate than filtering and unavailable online
    • Most likely explanation — Viterbi, which is not the sequence of most likely states, and the difference is a real distinction people get wrong
  • Hidden Markov models as the discrete case
    • Matrix formulation of forward–backward; Baum–Welch for learning the parameters
    • Why HMMs dominated speech recognition for thirty years, and what replaced them
  • The Kalman filter as the continuous linear-Gaussian case
    • Its three assumptions — linear transition, linear observation, Gaussian noise — and the fact that under them the Gaussian belief is closed under both prediction and update, which is the whole reason it is exact and cheap
    • Prediction and update steps; the Kalman gain read as "how much do I trust this measurement relative to my model"
    • Extended and unscented variants for nonlinear systems; the EKF's linearisation error and how it diverges
  • Particle filters, for when the belief is not Gaussian and not discrete
    • Sequential importance sampling with resampling; particles as a sample-based representation of the belief
    • Degeneracy (all weight on one particle) and sample impoverishment after resampling — the two failure modes, with effective sample size as the diagnostic
    • Why the kidnapped-robot problem is the standard demonstration that a Kalman filter cannot represent a multi-modal belief and a particle filter can
  • Dynamic Bayesian networks as the general frame that contains all of the above, and unrolling versus recursive inference

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 14 in full; the unified treatment of filtering across representations is the reason to read this chapter rather than three separate tutorials.
  • A Tutorial on Hidden Markov Models and Selected Applications in Speech Recognition — Rabiner's tutorial, still the standard reference, and the three-problems framing organises the whole subject.
  • A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems — Kalman's 1960 paper, for the derivation in the original terms.
  • Novel Approach to Nonlinear/Non-Gaussian Bayesian State Estimation — Gordon, Salmond and Smith's bootstrap filter, the paper that introduced resampling and made particle filters work.
  • Probabilistic Robotics — Chapters 2–4 and 8, which are the best-written account of these filters anywhere, aimed at people who have to make them run.

Practice

Implement Monte Carlo localisation for a robot on a 2D grid with a noisy range sensor and noisy motion. Start it with a uniform prior over the whole map and watch the particle cloud collapse. Then break it three ways: reduce to 20 particles, remove the resampling step, and teleport the robot mid-run without telling the filter. You are done when you can name each failure correctly — impoverishment, degeneracy, and the kidnapped-robot problem — and when you have fixed the third with particle injection.

Where people go wrong

Reaching for a Kalman filter because the problem is "tracking". The Kalman filter is exactly optimal under linear dynamics with Gaussian noise and is badly behaved outside that, and the specific way it fails is that it cannot represent a bimodal belief at all — it will confidently report the average of two hypotheses, which is a location the robot has never been. If your belief could reasonably have two peaks, you need a particle filter or a mixture, and no amount of tuning process noise will fix it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why smoothing gives a strictly better estimate than filtering for the same time step, say why you nevertheless cannot use it in a control loop, and give a concrete example where the Viterbi path differs from the sequence of individually most likely states.

M15

Utilities, and how a preference becomes a number

≈5 h

M13 and M14 give the agent beliefs. Beliefs alone do not determine action — you also need to say what you want, and "what you want" has to become a single number before an expectation can be taken over it. That step is not a technicality: it is an axiomatic result, it is routinely botched in practice, and the botching is exactly the failure that M21's alignment material is about. This is a short module carrying disproportionate weight for M16 and M21.

Concepts

  • From preferences to utilities — the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and the representation theorem
    • Orderability, transitivity, continuity, substitutability, monotonicity, decomposability; the theorem says that an agent satisfying these behaves as if maximising expected utility for some utility function unique up to positive affine transformation
    • The transitivity argument as a money pump: an intransitive agent can be sold a cycle of trades that returns it to its starting position minus a fee, forever
    • What the theorem does and does not claim — it does not say the agent has a utility function in its head, only that its choices are consistent with one
  • The principle of maximum expected utility, and the shape of the utility function
    • Risk aversion, risk neutrality and risk seeking as curvature; certainty equivalent and insurance premium as the readable consequences
    • Money is not utility, and the log-utility answer to the St Petersburg paradox
    • Where human behaviour violates the axioms: the Allais paradox (certainty effect, violating substitutability) and the Ellsberg paradox (ambiguity aversion, violating the existence of a single probability). Both are reproducible, and both matter when the utility function is elicited from people
  • Decision networks (influence diagrams) — Bayesian networks extended with decision and utility nodes
    • Evaluating one: set the decision, run inference from M13, compute expected utility, take the argmax
    • Multi-attribute utility, dominance (strict and stochastic), and preference independence as the assumptions that make many-attribute problems tractable
  • The value of information, which is the module's most useful export
    • VPI as the expected improvement in decision quality from an observation, formalised as an expectation over the observation's possible values
    • Three properties worth internalising: VPI is never negative in expectation, it is not additive, and it is zero whenever the observation cannot change the decision — which is the test for whether a proposed measurement, experiment or A/B test is worth running at all
    • This is the quantitative version of the sensing question raised informally in M11
  • Where utility functions actually come from, and why that is the hard part
    • Elicitation, revealed preference, and proxy metrics; the standard failure of optimising a proxy that was only correlated with what you wanted
    • The direct line from here to M21: if the agent maximises a misspecified utility, competence makes the outcome worse rather than better

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 16 in full, including the value-of-information section, which is short and the most immediately applicable part of the chapter.
  • Algorithms for Decision Making — Chapters 6 and 7, which are more computational than AIMA and give runnable formulations.
  • Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control — Chapters 2 and 5, where Russell sets out why a fixed known objective is the wrong architecture; read it here rather than in M21 so the technical and the philosophical arrive together.
  • Stanford CS221: Artificial Intelligence — Principles and Techniques — the utility and decision lectures.

Practice

Build a decision network for a concrete choice with a diagnostic test in it — a medical screening decision or a predictive-maintenance inspection. Compute the optimal decision without the test, then compute the value of perfect information for the test result, and finally the value of the actual imperfect test given its sensitivity and specificity. You are done when you can state the exact test price above which running it is irrational, and when you have found a set of priors for which a highly accurate test is still worth nothing.

Where people go wrong

Treating expected monetary value as expected utility. They coincide only for a risk-neutral agent with a linear utility over money, which describes almost no real decision-maker facing a large downside. The tell is a recommendation that is correct in expectation and that no one would actually take — betting the company on a positive-EV coin flip. The fix is not to abandon the framework but to elicit the curvature, which is a small amount of work that changes the answer.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state which vNM axiom the Allais paradox violates and demonstrate the violation with the actual lotteries, and separately explain why the value of information for a very accurate sensor can be exactly zero.

M16

Sequential decisions: MDPs and POMDPs

≈8 h

M15 optimised a single decision. Almost nothing worth automating is a single decision — actions change the state you will face next, and the good move now may be the one that pays in twenty steps. The Markov decision process is the formalism that makes this precise, and it is the single most reused object in the second half of this syllabus: M17 is what happens when you do not know the transition model, and the deep RL on the sibling deep-learning page is what happens when the state space is too big to enumerate. Get the Bellman equations properly here and both follow easily.

Concepts

  • The MDP — states, actions, transition model P(s′ | s, a), reward R(s, a, s′), and a policy π mapping states to actions
    • Why the solution is a policy rather than a plan: the environment is stochastic, so a fixed action sequence has no meaning after the first surprise
    • Discounting: γ makes infinite-horizon returns finite and expresses a preference for sooner. It is part of the problem specification, not a hyperparameter — changing γ changes which policy is optimal
    • Finite versus infinite horizon, and why the optimal policy is stationary only in the latter
  • The Bellman equations, and the two algorithms that solve them
    • The Bellman expectation equation for a fixed policy; the Bellman optimality equation with the max, which is nonlinear and therefore not solvable by linear algebra
    • Value iteration as repeated application of the Bellman backup, and the proof that it converges: the backup is a γ-contraction in the max norm, so Banach's fixed-point theorem gives both existence and uniqueness of V*, plus a γᵏ error bound
    • Policy iteration — evaluation (a linear solve) alternating with greedy improvement — terminating in finitely many steps because there are finitely many policies and each is strictly better
    • Modified policy iteration, asynchronous and prioritised sweeping; the linear-programming formulation as the third route, which matters mainly because it extends to constraints
  • Partial observability, and the exact sense in which it is harder
    • The belief state: a probability distribution over states, updated by exactly the filtering equation from M14
    • The central result — a POMDP is an MDP over belief states — which is elegant and immediately fatal, because the belief space is continuous even when the state space is small
    • The value function over belief space is piecewise-linear and convex; α-vectors as its representation, and exact value iteration's explosion in their number
    • Point-based methods (PBVI, SARSOP) as the approximation that made POMDPs practical: back up at a sampled set of reachable beliefs rather than everywhere
    • Complexity: finite-horizon POMDPs are PSPACE-complete, and the infinite-horizon problem is undecidable — the result from M11 arriving in its natural home
  • Modelling honestly — reward specification, sparse versus shaped rewards, and potential-based shaping as the one transformation guaranteed to preserve the optimal policy
    • Reward hacking as the sequential version of M15's proxy problem, and why it appears here rather than being a modern discovery

Work through

  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 17 sections 17.1–17.4 for MDPs, then 17.5 for POMDPs.
  • Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction — Chapters 3 and 4. Sutton and Barto's treatment of the Bellman equations and dynamic programming is the clearest available, and it sets up M17 in the same notation.
  • Planning and Acting in Partially Observable Stochastic Domains — Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra's paper, which is where the belief-MDP construction is laid out properly.
  • Algorithms for Decision Making — Chapters 7, 9 and 20–22; it is the best modern source for point-based POMDP methods and it ships working code.
  • Introduction to Reinforcement Learning with David Silver — lectures 2 and 3 cover MDPs and dynamic programming and are the best video treatment of this material.
  • POMDPs.jl — a solver ecosystem where MDPs and POMDPs share an interface, so you can watch the same problem get harder when you hide the state.

Practice

Implement value iteration and policy iteration on a gridworld with a cliff and a stochastic wind. Plot the max-norm error against iteration for γ = 0.9, 0.99 and 0.999, and count policy-iteration steps for each. Then convert it to a POMDP by making the agent's position observable only through a noisy sensor, and solve it with a point-based method from a library. You are done when your error curves visibly decay at rate γ per sweep, and when you can show a γ for which the optimal policy takes the safe route and one for which it takes the cliff edge.

Where people go wrong

Treating γ as a tuning knob to be swept for best performance. The discount factor is part of the problem definition: it says how much a reward twenty steps out is worth relative to one now, and different values define genuinely different optimisation problems with different optimal policies. Tuning it to make your agent behave is a way of quietly changing the objective until the answer looks nice — and it hides the fact that the reward function, not the discount, was the thing specified wrongly.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: prove that the Bellman optimality backup is a contraction and explain what that buys you (existence, uniqueness, and a computable error bound), and then explain why "a POMDP is just an MDP over beliefs" is true and yet does not make POMDPs easy.

M17

Reinforcement learning without a model

≈7 h

M16 solved the MDP assuming you have P(s′ | s, a) and R. You usually do not. Reinforcement learning is what remains when the model is unavailable and the agent must learn to act from experience alone — and it is the last module of the classical decision-making arc, the point where this page hands over to the deep-learning page. The material here is tabular on purpose: every pathology of deep RL is already visible in the small case, and it is much easier to diagnose there.

Concepts

  • What changes when the model goes away — prediction versus control, and on-policy versus off-policy learning
    • The exploration/exploitation dilemma appears here and nowhere earlier, because in M16 you could evaluate an action without taking it
    • Model-free versus model-based RL, and the fact that learning the model and then planning with M16's algorithms is a legitimate and often superior strategy (Dyna as the hybrid)
  • Monte Carlo methods — learn from complete returns, unbiased, high variance, and require episodes to terminate
  • Temporal-difference learning, which is the field's signature idea
    • TD(0): update toward r + γV(s′) rather than toward the actual return — bootstrapping, i.e. learning a guess from a guess
    • The bias/variance trade against Monte Carlo, and TD(λ) with eligibility traces as the parameterised family that spans both ends
    • Why TD converges for tabular prediction, and the convergence conditions on the step size (Robbins–Monro)
  • Control — SARSA and Q-learning, and the difference that is easiest to feel by running them
    • SARSA is on-policy: it learns the value of the policy it is actually following, exploration included
    • Q-learning is off-policy: it learns Q* regardless of the behaviour policy, given sufficient exploration
    • The cliff-walking example, where SARSA learns the safe path and Q-learning learns the optimal-but-risky one, and both are behaving correctly
    • Expected SARSA and double Q-learning; maximisation bias and why the double estimator fixes it
  • Exploration, treated as its own problem
    • ε-greedy and its limitations; optimism in the face of uncertainty; UCB and the regret framing from the bandit literature — the same UCB1 that drove MCTS in M07
    • Why exploration in large state spaces is not solved by any of these, and is an open problem rather than a matter of tuning ε
  • Function approximation, and the deadly triad
    • Once states are too numerous to tabulate, values must be approximated, and the convergence guarantees above stop applying
    • The deadly triad — function approximation + bootstrapping + off-policy training — any two are safe, all three can diverge, and Baird's counterexample is the small concrete demonstration
    • This is exactly where the deep-learning page picks up: DQN's target network and replay buffer are engineering responses to this triad
  • TD-Gammon as the first result that made anyone believe this worked, and what it did and did not show

Work through

  • Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction — Chapters 5–7 and 11. Chapter 11's treatment of the deadly triad is the single best explanation of it in print.
  • Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences — Sutton's 1988 paper, where TD is introduced and analysed.
  • Q-learning — Watkins and Dayan's convergence proof, which is short and worth reading for the conditions it requires.
  • Temporal Difference Learning and TD-Gammon — Tesauro's account of the first large empirical success.
  • Reinforcement Learning: A Survey — Kaelbling, Littman and Moore's 1996 survey, for a snapshot of the field's concerns before deep RL reorganised them.
  • Introduction to Reinforcement Learning with David Silver — lectures 4 and 5, on model-free prediction and control.
  • Gymnasium — the environment API; use the classic-control and toy-text environments, which are small enough to stay tabular.

Practice

Implement SARSA and Q-learning on the cliff-walking gridworld with ε-greedy exploration, and plot both the learned paths and the reward per episode. Then anneal ε toward zero and watch the two policies converge to the same thing. Finally, implement Baird's counterexample with linear function approximation and off-policy updates, and watch the weights diverge. You are done when you can show all three plots and explain each, including why the divergence is not a bug in your code.

Where people go wrong

Assuming Q-learning's convergence guarantee survives function approximation. It does not, and this is not a small-print caveat: the tabular proof relies on every state-action pair having its own independently updatable entry, which is exactly what approximation destroys. Practitioners who learned Q-learning tabularly and then applied it with a neural network without a target network or replay buffer produce divergence and conclude that RL is unstable, when the specific failure has a name, a counterexample and a standard set of mitigations.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name the three components of the deadly triad, describe a setup that has all three and one that drops exactly one, and predict which diverges — and separately explain why SARSA and Q-learning learn different policies on cliff-walking without either being wrong.

M18

More than one agent

≈6 h

Every module since M13 has assumed a single decision-maker facing an indifferent world. M07 briefly broke that assumption for zero-sum games; this module breaks it properly. Once other agents are optimising too, "optimal" stops being well-defined without saying what everyone else does — and the concepts that replace it (equilibrium, mechanism, incentive compatibility) are what you need to reason about auctions, markets, voting, and any multi-agent system you might be asked to design.

Concepts

  • Normal-form games and solution concepts
    • Dominant strategies where they exist; iterated elimination of dominated strategies
    • Nash equilibrium, its existence in mixed strategies (Nash, 1950), and its two well-known problems: multiplicity, and the fact that computing one is PPAD-complete
    • Prisoner's dilemma, stag hunt, matching pennies and battle of the sexes as the four shapes that recur; the maximin/minimax coincidence in two-player zero-sum games, which is why M07 could avoid all of this
    • Pareto optimality and social welfare as separate criteria that equilibria need not satisfy
  • Repeated and sequential interaction
    • Finitely repeated games and backward induction's uncomfortable prediction; infinitely repeated games and the folk theorem, where cooperation becomes sustainable
    • Extensive-form games, subgame-perfect equilibrium, and imperfect information as the link back to M07's information sets
  • Mechanism design — the inverse problem: choose the rules so that self-interested play produces the outcome you wanted
    • Incentive compatibility and the revelation principle
    • Vickrey second-price auctions and truthfulness; the VCG mechanism as the general form, and its real weaknesses (revenue, collusion, computational cost) which explain why it is admired more than used
    • First-price versus second-price, revenue equivalence, and combinatorial auctions with their winner-determination problem — which is an M06 or M05 problem in disguise, and is where spectrum auctions meet integer programming
  • Social choice, and the impossibility results
    • Arrow's theorem: no rank-aggregation rule satisfies unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives simultaneously
    • Gibbard–Satterthwaite: every reasonable voting rule is manipulable, so strategic voting is a structural fact rather than a defect of a particular ballot design
    • Why this matters to system builders: any aggregation of preferences — recommendation, ranking, multi-stakeholder objectives — inherits these results
  • Multi-agent decision-making under uncertainty, briefly — Dec-POMDPs, their NEXP-completeness, and cooperative multi-agent planning

Work through

  • Multiagent Systems: Algorithmic, Game-Theoretic, and Logical Foundations — Chapters 3, 5, 6 and 10. Free online from the authors, and it is written for computer scientists rather than economists.
  • Equilibrium Points in N-Person Games — Nash's one-page 1950 note, worth reading purely for how short the existence proof's statement is.
  • Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders — Vickrey's paper, the origin of the second-price auction and of truthful mechanism design.
  • A Difficulty in the Concept of Social Welfare — Arrow's impossibility theorem in the original.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach — Chapter 18, for the AI-flavoured treatment including cooperative multi-agent planning.
  • Noam Brown's talks and papers on imperfect-information games — for what happens to equilibrium reasoning beyond two players, from someone who shipped it anyway.
  • AAMAS: Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems — the venue where this module's material is actively developed.

Practice

Run a sealed-bid auction simulation with agents whose valuations you control. Implement first-price and second-price rules, and let the first-price bidders shade their bids by a learned factor. Verify empirically that truthful bidding is a dominant strategy under the second-price rule and is not under the first-price rule, and check whether revenue equivalence holds in your simulation. You are done when you can state which assumption of revenue equivalence your simulation violates when it fails.

Where people go wrong

Speaking of "the" Nash equilibrium. Most games have several, they can differ enormously in welfare, and nothing in the concept says which one gets played — equilibrium selection is a separate and unsolved problem. Designing a multi-agent system on the assumption that the equilibrium you had in mind is the one that will occur is how mechanisms get gamed in production. Where you need a unique prediction, you have to design for it (dominant strategies) rather than assume it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: prove that truthful bidding is dominant in a second-price auction by the two-case argument, and then explain why VCG — despite being truthful, efficient and general — is rarely deployed, naming at least two of its concrete failure modes.

M19

Robotics and motion planning, as classical AI reads them

≈6 h

Robotics is where every thread of this syllabus is forced to touch reality at once: M14's filtering keeps the robot located, M16's decision theory chooses what to do, and motion planning is search (M02–M04) in a space that is continuous and high-dimensional. It earns its own module because that last difference is not cosmetic — the algorithms that work in configuration space are genuinely different, and they are among the most widely deployed results in this entire page.

Concepts

  • Configuration space as the reframing that makes planning possible
    • A robot becomes a point in a space whose dimension is its number of degrees of freedom; obstacles become forbidden regions in that space
    • Constructing C-space obstacles exactly is intractable beyond a few dimensions, which is why every practical method avoids constructing them at all and only tests membership
    • The topology matters: revolute joints make C-space a torus, not a box, and planners that assume otherwise take the long way round
  • The classical exact and decomposition methods, and why they do not scale — visibility graphs, cell decomposition, Voronoi-based roadmaps
    • Complete and elegant in two dimensions; exponential in the dimension, which for a 7-DOF arm is fatal
    • Potential fields as the fast approximate alternative, and their notorious local minima, which are not fixable by better tuning
  • Sampling-based planning, the family that actually shipped
    • Probabilistic roadmaps: sample, connect, query — designed for many queries in a static environment
    • Rapidly-exploring random trees: grow a tree biased toward unexplored regions by sampling and extending toward the sample; designed for single queries and easily extended to nonholonomic constraints
    • Probabilistic completeness — the probability of finding a solution tends to 1 as samples grow — and its precise weakness: it says nothing at all about a problem with no solution, so failure and slowness look identical
    • RRT* and asymptotic optimality: plain RRT converges to a suboptimal path almost surely, which was the motivating defect
    • Narrow passages as the standard hard case, and the sampling strategies aimed at them
  • Localisation and mapping
    • Monte Carlo localisation as the direct application of M14's particle filter — the same code, a map as the sensor model
    • SLAM's chicken-and-egg structure; EKF-SLAM and its O(n²) covariance, FastSLAM's Rao-Blackwellised factorisation, and the modern pose-graph/factor-graph formulation solved as sparse nonlinear least squares
    • Loop closure as the step that makes or breaks a map, and data association as the thing that actually goes wrong
  • Where learning took over and where it did not — perception is now entirely learned, planning and control mostly are not, and manipulation with contact is the live frontier
    • The honest current picture: classical planners still run in production robots, usually with learned perception in front of them

Work through

  • Planning Algorithms — Chapters 4, 5 and 7. LaValle's book is free online from the author and is the reference for sampling-based planning by the person who introduced RRTs.
  • Probabilistic Roadmaps for Path Planning in High-Dimensional Configuration Spaces — the PRM paper, for the argument that sampling beats construction.
  • Sampling-Based Algorithms for Optimal Motion Planning — Karaman and Frazzoli on RRT*, including the proof that plain RRT is almost surely suboptimal.
  • Probabilistic Robotics — Chapters 7–8 on localisation and 10–13 on SLAM.
  • The Open Motion Planning Library — run PRM, RRT and RRT* on the same scene through one interface rather than reimplementing each.
  • Leslie Kaelbling's talks and papers — her task-and-motion-planning talks, which are the bridge between this module, M11 and M16.

Practice

Implement RRT in a 2D world with polygonal obstacles and a narrow passage, then extend it to RRT*. Run each 100 times from the same start and goal, recording success rate, path cost and time. You are done when RRT* costs visibly converge downward with more samples while RRT's do not, and when you can produce a scene where both fail nearly always — and say whether that is because no path exists or because the passage is too narrow, which your planner cannot tell you.

Where people go wrong

Using a potential field as a planner rather than as a local controller. It is fast, needs no map preprocessing and works beautifully in demos — and it has local minima that are a property of the field's geometry, not of the tuning. A U-shaped obstacle traps it every time. The correct architecture is a global sampling-based planner producing waypoints with a local reactive controller (potential field or otherwise) between them, and conflating the two layers is one of the most common robotics-architecture mistakes.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what probabilistic completeness guarantees and, more importantly, what it does not — specifically why a planner that has run for an hour without a solution tells you nothing about whether one exists.

M20

The record: what classical AI actually shipped

≈5 h

You have now built nineteen modules of algorithms that a great deal of public commentary describes as obsolete. This module is the evidence, and it is here rather than at the start because you now know enough to read the systems rather than the summaries. It is also where the Bitter Lesson gets a fair hearing on both sides — that essay is the strongest argument against everything on this page, and a syllabus that omits it is not being honest with you.

Concepts

  • Expert systems: the boom, the products, and the specific reason they died
    • DENDRAL, MYCIN and R1/XCON — what each did and what each was measured against; XCON's reported savings and its rule count over time
    • The failure was maintenance, not accuracy: rule bases grew, interactions between rules became unanalysable, and the cost of keeping one current exceeded its value. This is a claim about software engineering, not about logic
    • Knowledge acquisition as the bottleneck the field named at the time and never solved by hand
  • Where these techniques won permanently, with the technique named in each case
    • SAT and SMT in hardware verification, equivalence checking and bounded model checking — every non-trivial chip taped out today has had M06's algorithms run over it
    • Constraint programming in crew rostering, timetabling, and production scheduling; the winner-determination problem in combinatorial spectrum auctions
    • Automated planning in spacecraft operations (NASA's Remote Agent on Deep Space 1, MAPGEN on the Mars Exploration Rovers) and in logistics
    • Search in routing, from road networks to warehouse robot fleets; Datalog in program analysis and in policy engines
    • Description logics in clinical terminologies, and Bayesian networks in diagnosis and risk
    • The common thread: hard constraints, an audit requirement, an optimality guarantee, or a need to explain the answer — the four conditions under which a learned model is not an acceptable substitute
  • The AI effect — the observation that solved problems stop counting as AI, and why that systematically distorts the field's self-assessment
    • Chess, route planning, spam filtering, speech recognition and OCR were all AI until they worked
  • The Bitter Lesson, and the strongest available response
    • Sutton's claim: methods that scale with computation beat methods that encode human knowledge, repeatedly, across sixty years — chess, Go, speech, vision
    • The response worth taking seriously: every example is a perception or evaluation problem with abundant data and a cheap simulator, and the essay's own examples (AlphaGo especially) retained search as the improvement operator. Where the bottleneck is combinatorial constraint rather than pattern recognition, no equivalent scaling result exists
    • The complementary reading — "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data" and Norvig's reply to Chomsky — for what the statistical turn actually claimed

Work through

  • The Quest for Artificial Intelligence — the expert-systems chapters; Nilsson names the systems and their measured results rather than the folklore.
  • The Bitter Lesson — Sutton's essay in full. It is one page, it is the strongest argument against this syllabus, and you should be able to state it in your own words.
  • The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Data — Halevy, Norvig and Pereira, for the data-over-models position stated by its own authors.
  • On Chomsky and the Two Cultures of Statistical Learning — Norvig's reply, which is the most careful account of what the statistical turn does and does not claim about understanding.
  • CYC: A Large-Scale Investment in Knowledge Infrastructure — revisit it here, now that you can read it as a claim about maintenance cost.
  • The SAT Competition — the results pages over two decades, which are the cleanest public record of sustained progress on an NP-complete problem.
  • Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans — Mitchell's chapters on the barrier of meaning, as a calibration check on your own reading of the record.
  • Rodney Brooks's blog — his annually scored predictions — the only public AI forecasting record with a scoring rubric attached.
  • AAAI: Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence — the open archive, which is where most of the historical papers on this page live.

Practice

Pick one deployed system from the list above — Remote Agent, a spectrum auction, a chip equivalence checker, a crew-rostering deployment. Find its primary paper or technical report, and write one page identifying which algorithm from M02–M19 is at its core, what the alternative would have been, and which of the four conditions (hard constraints, audit, optimality, explanation) made the classical approach necessary. You are done when you can name the algorithm precisely enough to point at the module.

Where people go wrong

Taking the Bitter Lesson as a general theorem. It is an inductive argument from a specific class of problems — ones with cheap simulators or vast data, where the task is fundamentally perception or evaluation — and it is well supported there. It has no equivalent evidence base for problems whose difficulty is combinatorial and whose answers must be provably correct: nobody scales their way to a verified chip or a feasible crew roster. Reading it as "compute always wins" is how organisations end up training a model to approximate a constraint solver.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name three production systems whose core is an algorithm from this page, state the algorithm in each case, and articulate the Bitter Lesson's argument in its strongest form and the specific class of problems where its evidence does not reach.

M21

Philosophy, and the alignment problem in its original form

≈5 h

The questions here are old, and they are usually presented as decoration at the end of a course. They are not decoration: M15 defined a rational agent as one that maximises a given utility function, and the entire modern alignment literature is the observation that we cannot write that function down. Reading Russell's argument immediately after doing the utility mathematics is what turns it from a position piece into a technical claim about a specific architecture.

Concepts

  • The Turing test, read properly
    • The imitation game as Turing actually specified it, and the nine objections he raises and answers — including the argument from consciousness, which he sets aside rather than refutes
    • Why it is a poor engineering target: it rewards deception and evasion, gives no partial credit, and can be passed by systems nobody would call intelligent
    • Replacements that were proposed and what each measures: Winograd schemas, the Lovelace test, and Chollet's skill-acquisition-efficiency definition with ARC as its instrument
  • The Chinese room, and the replies that matter
    • Searle's argument as a claim about syntax versus semantics, not about behaviour
    • The systems reply, the robot reply, and Searle's counters; the symbol grounding problem as the version of this question that has empirical content
    • Weak versus strong AI, and why engineering can proceed while leaving the strong question open
  • The value alignment problem, stated in M15's vocabulary
    • The King Midas problem: an agent that optimises a stated objective perfectly, where the stated objective omits something you cared about — and the sharper the optimisation, the worse the omission hurts
    • Goodhart's law and specification gaming as the empirical face of this, and reward hacking from M16 as its sequential version
    • Instrumental convergence: self-preservation and resource acquisition as sub-goals of almost any terminal goal, which is why the off-switch is not trivially safe
  • Russell's proposed architectural fix, which is a concrete technical proposal rather than a manifesto
    • The three principles: the machine's only objective is to maximise human preferences; it is initially uncertain what those are; human behaviour is the evidence about them
    • Uncertainty about the objective as the mechanism that makes deference rational — the off-switch game shows an agent uncertain about its reward prefers to allow itself to be switched off
    • Inverse reinforcement learning and cooperative IRL as the technical programme; and the honest difficulties — human preferences are inconsistent, change, and are not shared between people
  • Ethics that arrives before superintelligence — accountability for deployed decisions, automation bias, the distributional consequences of who the performance measure in M01 was written by

Work through

  • Computing Machinery and Intelligence — reread it here with nineteen modules behind you; the objections read very differently the second time.
  • Minds, Brains, and Programs — Searle's original, with the peer commentary if you can get it; the replies are half the value.
  • Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control — Chapters 5–7 and 9, the core of the argument and the three principles.
  • The Off-Switch Game — the formal result that an agent uncertain about its objective has an incentive to permit interruption.
  • Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning — the technical formulation of alignment as a two-player game with a shared but unknown reward.
  • On the Measure of Intelligence — Chollet's definition and the ARC benchmark; sections 1–3 are the argument, the rest is the specification.
  • The Chinese Room Argument — the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, for the four decades of replies laid out systematically.
  • 3 Principles for Creating Safer AI — the twenty-minute version of the book's argument, useful before or instead of the chapters.
  • Common Sense, the Turing Test, and the Quest for Real AI — Levesque's short book, the strongest statement of what the symbolic tradition thinks is still missing.
  • The Turing Test — the Stanford Encyclopedia entry, as a corrective on what the 1950 paper actually proposed.
  • The Reith Lectures: Living With Artificial Intelligence — the question-and-answer sections, where Russell defends the position live.
  • ARC-AGI — attempt five puzzles by hand; it is the fastest way to feel what “resists memorisation” means.

Practice

Take a system you have built or used, write down the objective it actually optimises as an equation, and then list three things you care about that the equation omits. For each omission, describe the behaviour a sufficiently capable optimiser would produce. Then write the objective you would replace it with, and repeat the exercise on your replacement. You are done when the second round still finds omissions — which it will, and noticing that is the point.

Where people go wrong

Treating alignment as a recent concern imported from outside the field, or as a purely speculative one about future systems. It is a direct consequence of the standard model this syllabus taught you in M15 and M16 — an agent maximising a fixed given objective — and its everyday form is the recommender optimising engagement, the scheduler optimising throughput while starving one queue, and the RL agent from M16 exploiting a reward bug. The speculative version and the mundane version are the same failure at different scales.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the King Midas problem using the vocabulary of M15 — utility function, expected utility, proxy — without using the word "alignment", and then explain why uncertainty about the objective changes an agent's incentive regarding its own off-switch.

M22

The frontier: language models, planners, and where the boundary really sits

≈5 h

This is the module the rest of the page was building toward. The live question in AI right now is what a language model can and cannot do that the algorithms here can, and it is asked badly almost everywhere — because answering it requires knowing what "planning" means precisely, which took you M10 and M11 to acquire. You are now in a position to read the evidence rather than the press release.

Concepts

  • The claim under test, stated so it can be falsified — can a language model produce a valid, executable plan for a novel problem instance, at scale, without a symbolic component in the loop?
    • PlanBench and its construction: Blocksworld and Mystery Blocksworld, where the second is the first with the predicate names obfuscated — a control that separates planning from retrieval of memorised patterns
    • The obfuscation result is the crux: performance collapsing under renaming is strong evidence about which mechanism was producing the answers
    • How reasoning-trained models changed the picture, and how much: real improvement on the standard domains, still degrading with plan length and still expensive per instance, with the same benchmark-contamination caveat that applies to every LLM evaluation
  • The architectures that actually work, and what each delegates
    • LLM-Modulo: the model generates candidates and critiques, an external verifier or planner checks them, and the guarantee comes entirely from the verifier — Kambhampati's framing, and the honest description of most deployed "agentic planning"
    • LLM as translator: natural language to PDDL, then a real planner such as Fast Downward solves it. Correctness moves to the domain-modelling step, where a human can inspect it
    • LLM as heuristic: the model proposes an ordering, a decomposition or a candidate action, inside a search that retains its guarantees
    • The distinction to hold onto: whether the artefact was verified, and by what. "The model planned it" and "the model called a planner" are different claims with different failure modes
  • Neuro-symbolic integration, separating what exists from what is proposed
    • What has shipped: differentiable relaxations of logic, learned heuristics for search and for SAT branching, program synthesis with a verifier, retrieval over knowledge graphs
    • What remains open: end-to-end learning of symbolic structure, systematic generalisation, and the perennial question of whether the symbols should be learned or given
    • The evaluation problem underneath all of it — a benchmark that a system can pass by retrieval measures nothing about reasoning, and most do
  • The other direction: learning inside classical solvers
    • Learned branching heuristics for SAT and MIP, learned planner portfolio selection, learned value functions inside MCTS — the AlphaZero pattern applied to solvers
    • Why this direction has been quieter and more successful than the reverse, and what it says about where learning helps
  • Verification of learned components, which is where classical AI's guarantees re-enter — SMT-based neural network verification, shielding, and runtime monitors as the practical pattern
  • Live disagreements worth being able to state fairly: whether scaling alone reaches reliable planning; whether search is a necessary component of general capability or an artefact of insufficient scale; and whether the compositional generalisation gap is a data problem or an architectural one

Work through

  • PlanBench: An Extensible Benchmark for Evaluating Large Language Models on Planning and Reasoning about Change — the benchmark and, more importantly, the obfuscation control.
  • Can Large Language Models Reason and Plan? — Kambhampati's statement of the negative case, with the evidence it rests on.
  • LLMs Can't Plan, But Can Help Planning in LLM-Modulo Frameworks — the constructive companion; this is the architecture pattern to steal.
  • Neural-Symbolic Learning and Reasoning: A Survey and Interpretation — for the taxonomy of integration approaches, so you can place any new proposal.
  • ICAPS: International Conference on Automated Planning and Scheduling — the current proceedings; the LLM-and-planning work is being published here and the reviewing is unsentimental.
  • The Fast Downward Planning System — you will need it again for the practice exercise below.
  • AI: A Guide for Thinking Humans — Mitchell's series on whether language models reason — the most careful non-partisan treatment available.
  • IJCAI: International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence — open proceedings back to 1969, and the award lectures are the best reading in each year.
  • JAIR: Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research — open access, and where the planning papers carry enough experimental detail to reproduce.

Practice

Take twenty Blocksworld instances of increasing length. Solve each three ways: ask a language model directly for a plan; ask it to emit PDDL and solve that with Fast Downward; and solve the hand-written PDDL from M10 directly. Validate every plan mechanically with a plan validator — never by reading it. Plot validity against plan length for all three. You are done when you have a curve rather than an anecdote, and when you can state at what plan length the direct approach falls below 50% valid.

Where people go wrong

Evaluating generated plans by reading them. Plans that look right and are invalid are the overwhelmingly common case — a precondition unmet at step 7, an object referenced that was consumed at step 4 — and human review catches these unreliably and expensively. Every claim in this area is only as good as its validator, which is why PlanBench's use of a mechanical checker matters as much as its choice of domains. The second trap is testing on Blocksworld alone, which is in every training corpus ever assembled; the obfuscated variant exists precisely because of that.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: read a paper or product claim about an "AI agent that plans", and answer three questions from the text — what was the artefact, what verified it, and would the result survive renaming every predicate in the domain?

Capstone

Two projects. The first is the integrative one and exercises most of the page; the second is shorter and is the honest test of whether M22 landed. Do the first; do the second only if you intend to work near language models.

Project 1 — a delivery agent that plans, senses and decides. Build an agent for a grid city with roads, one-way streets, depots and packages, where travel times are stochastic, some roads close without warning, and the agent's position is known only through a noisy sensor.

  • Scope: model the routing layer as a search problem with a heuristic you derive by relaxation; model package assignment as a CSP or a CP-SAT model; model the action sequence as a PDDL domain and solve it with a real planner; maintain position belief with a particle filter; choose between "deliver now" and "return for fuel" by expected utility with a value-of-information calculation on whether to query an expensive traffic sensor; and handle road closures by replanning, then compare against a contingent plan.
  • Draws on: M02, M03, M05, M10, M11, M14, M15, M16.
  • Done when: the agent completes 100 randomised episodes with a measured success rate, and you can produce a table showing, for each subsystem, what the naive alternative was and how much the principled version bought — nodes expanded with and without the heuristic, episodes lost with and without the particle filter, expected utility gained by the sensor query.
  • Stretch: add a second delivery agent with its own objective and make the depot a scarce resource, then implement an auction from M18 to allocate it. Report whether truthful bidding survived your implementation.

Project 2 — an honest evaluation of an LLM planner. Extend the M22 exercise into a small, publishable-quality evaluation.

  • Scope: three domains (Blocksworld, Logistics, and one you invent that cannot be in any training corpus), each in a plain and an obfuscated variant, at four plan lengths. Three conditions: direct generation, generate-PDDL-then-plan, and a classical planner. Mechanical validation throughout.
  • Draws on: M10, M11, M20, M22.
  • Done when: you have a validity-versus-length plot per domain and variant, and a written paragraph on what the obfuscation gap does and does not license you to conclude.
  • Stretch: add the LLM-as-heuristic condition — the model proposes an action ordering inside your own search — and report whether it beat the derived heuristic from M10.

Mastery check

  • Given an unfamiliar problem, you can argue for one of {state-space search, CSP, SAT, PDDL, MDP} as its right formulation, and say what each of the others would lose.
  • You can prove A* optimal under consistency and identify the exact step that fails for a merely admissible heuristic on a graph.
  • You can construct an admissible heuristic for a new problem by naming the constraint you relaxed, and predict its dominance relative to another.
  • You can draw an implication graph, take the first-UIP cut, and explain why the learned clause preserves satisfiability.
  • You can explain why a tree-structured CSP needs no backtracking, and connect that to treewidth in Bayesian-network inference.
  • You can say what the delete relaxation discards, why the relaxed optimal plan length is admissible, and why h_FF is not.
  • You can state the frame and qualification problems without their names, and say why probability answers them where more rules do not.
  • You can apply d-separation to an unfamiliar network and name an observation that would break an independence that currently holds.
  • You can explain why a Kalman filter cannot represent a bimodal belief and what it reports instead.
  • You can prove the Bellman optimality backup is a contraction and say what that buys.
  • You can name the deadly triad and predict which of two RL configurations diverges.
  • You can prove truthfulness of the second-price auction and name two reasons VCG is rarely deployed.
  • You can name three deployed systems whose core is an algorithm from this page, and the four conditions that make classical methods necessary.
  • You can state the Bitter Lesson in its strongest form and identify the problem class its evidence does not cover.
  • You can assess a claim that a language model planned something, by asking what the artefact was, what verified it, and whether the result survives renaming.

Where to go next

  • Classical Machine Learning — the AIMA chapters this page deliberately omitted, done properly. M13's networks needed their parameters from somewhere and M17's agent needed to generalise across states; both are learning problems, and the statistical framing there is what makes them answerable.
  • Deep Learning — where M17 hands over. Deep reinforcement learning, the deadly triad's engineering mitigations, and the learned evaluation functions that M07's AlphaZero lineage depends on are all developed there.
  • Generative AI: Large Language Models — the other side of M22. If you want to know what the systems being tested for planning ability actually are and how they are trained, that page is the mechanism behind this page's evidence.
  • Information Theory — M13's loopy belief propagation is the decoding algorithm for modern error-correcting codes, and the connection is not a coincidence: factor graphs are the shared formalism. That page develops the coding side.
  • Automated reasoning and formal verification — M06 and M09 stop at the door of a large field: SMT theories, interactive theorem proving in Lean, Coq or Isabelle, abstract interpretation and model checking. This is where the guarantees classical AI offers are actually cashed out in industry.
  • Operations research and combinatorial optimisation — M05's constraint programming has a sibling discipline with integer programming, cutting planes, column generation and the whole LP duality theory behind it. Any real scheduling problem eventually needs both literatures.
  • Causal inference — M13 was careful to say that Bayesian-network arrows are not causal. Pearl's later work, do-calculus and the potential-outcomes tradition are what you need to answer intervention and counterfactual questions, and they are a separate subject with separate assumptions.
  • Robotics, control and state estimation — M14 and M19 are the AI-flavoured slice of a much larger engineering discipline: optimal control, model-predictive control, contact dynamics and manipulation are where the field goes from here.

Papers & primary sources

Classical AI is unusual in that its foundational papers are short and readable — most of these are under twenty pages and several are under five. Where a DOI exists it is the link, because publisher URLs move and DOIs do not; several publishers refuse automated link checks, and every entry here has had its title, authors and year confirmed through Crossref or the arXiv API rather than from memory.

  1. R-27 Computing Machinery and Intelligence — Alan M. Turing, Mind, 1950

    Read all of it, not the first section. The imitation game occupies two pages; the remaining twenty are Turing anticipating and answering nine objections, several of which are still the objections people raise. His prediction of a 30% fooling rate by 2000 and his remarks on learning machines are both worth knowing precisely, because both are routinely misquoted. (Oxford University Press answers 403 to automated checks; Crossref confirms the DOI, title and 1950 date.)

    Core Paywalled Beginner ~28 pp
    Used in M01 M21
  2. R-28 Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search — Allen Newell & Herbert A. Simon, Communications of the ACM, 1976

    The Turing Award lecture that states the physical symbol system hypothesis and the heuristic search hypothesis. Worth reading for the care with which they frame both as empirical claims about the world rather than definitions — which is what makes the deep-learning era evidence rather than mere disagreement. (ACM returns 403 to automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Free Beginner ~19 pp
    Used in M01
  3. R-29 A Formal Basis for the Heuristic Determination of Minimum Cost Paths — Peter E. Hart, Nils J. Nilsson & Bertram Raphael, IEEE Transactions on Systems Science and Cybernetics, 1968

    The A* paper. Read it for the admissibility condition stated by its authors and for the optimality argument in its original form — both are cleaner here than in most textbook retellings. The 1972 correction note on consistency is the part textbooks fold in silently, and knowing it was a correction explains why the distinction is so often muddled. (IEEE Xplore answers 202 to automated checks; Crossref confirms authors, title and year.)

    Core Paywalled Intermediate ~9 pp
    Used in M03
  4. R-30 Depth-First Iterative-Deepening: An Optimal Admissible Tree Search — Richard E. Korf, Artificial Intelligence, 1985

    IDA*, and the analysis showing that iterative deepening's repeated work is a constant factor rather than a disaster. The argument — the last level dominates a geometric sum — is one you should be able to reproduce, because it recurs whenever anyone proposes restarting a search from scratch. (Elsevier 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Intermediate ~15 pp
    Used in M03
  5. R-31 Pattern Databases — Joseph C. Culberson & Jonathan Schaeffer, Computational Intelligence, 1998

    Where the idea of precomputing exact subproblem costs as an admissible heuristic is introduced. This is the single biggest practical improvement in heuristic search after A* itself, and the paper is direct about the memory-for-search tradeoff that makes it work. Read it before attempting M03's practice exercise. (Wiley 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Intermediate ~22 pp
    Used in M03
  6. R-32 Consistency in Networks of Relations — Alan K. Mackworth, Artificial Intelligence, 1977

    Node, arc and path consistency, and the AC-3 algorithm. Short, and the framing — propagation as inference that is separate from search — is what makes the rest of constraint programming make sense. Read it directly after AIMA Chapter 6's propagation section. (Elsevier 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Intermediate ~8 pp
    Used in M05
  7. R-33 A Machine Program for Theorem-Proving — Martin Davis, George Logemann & Donald Loveland, Communications of the ACM, 1962

    DPLL, in two pages. The remarkable thing is how little has changed: unit propagation and splitting are still the core of every solver, and the paper's discussion of memory constraints reads as quaint until you notice that memory locality is still what watched literals are about. (ACM 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Intermediate ~2 pp
    Used in M06
  8. R-34 GRASP: A Search Algorithm for Propositional Satisfiability — João P. Marques-Silva & Karem A. Sakallah, IEEE Transactions on Computers, 1999

    Conflict-driven clause learning, introduced. The implication-graph figures are the substance — work through one by hand and take the first-UIP cut yourself before reading their analysis. Everything modern in M06 descends from this paper and from Chaff. (IEEE Xplore answers 202; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Advanced ~28 pp
    Used in M06
  9. R-35 Chaff: Engineering an Efficient SAT Solver — Matthew W. Moskewicz, Conor F. Madigan, Ying Zhao, Lintao Zhang & Sharad Malik, DAC, 2001

    Two-watched-literals and VSIDS, and an order-of-magnitude speedup from data structures rather than from algorithms. The best available demonstration that in solver work the engineering is the research contribution, and a useful corrective if you have absorbed the idea that implementation detail is beneath a paper. (ACM 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Advanced ~6 pp
    Used in M06
  10. R-36 A New Method for Solving Hard Satisfiability Problems — Bart Selman, Hector Levesque & David Mitchell, AAAI, 1992

    GSAT: local search applied to satisfiability, at a time when everyone assumed SAT needed a complete method. Free from AAAI's own archive. Read it as the concrete instance of M04's argument — giving up completeness bought several orders of magnitude on instances nobody could otherwise touch — and note that the systematic solvers later caught up, which is the interesting part of the story.

    The UCT paper. Its contribution is the argument that node selection in a search tree is a multi-armed bandit problem, which is what turns MCTS from a heuristic into something with a regret bound.   50. R-76 Can Large Language Models Reason and Plan? — Subbarao Kambhampati, Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences, 2024

    The negative case stated carefully and with its evidence attached. Articles, essays & blogs

Short pieces that change how you read the rest. The Stanford Encyclopedia entries are the exception to the "avoid secondary sources" rule — they are written by specialists, cite the primary literature exhaustively, and are the fastest route into a philosophical debate you need to understand but not join.

  1. R-90 The Bitter Lesson — Richard S. Sutton, 2019

    One page, and the strongest argument against everything on this syllabus: methods that scale with computation beat methods that encode human knowledge, repeatedly, over sixty years. Read it, be able to state it in your own words, and then notice which problem class every one of its examples belongs to. That noticing is M20's content.

    Core Free Beginner ~1 p
    Used in M20
  2. R-91 On Chomsky and the Two Cultures of Statistical Learning — Peter Norvig, 2011

    Norvig's reply to Chomsky's dismissal of statistical language models, and the most careful thing written about what a probabilistic model does and does not claim to explain. Relevant far beyond linguistics: the distinction it draws between a model that predicts and a model that explains is exactly the distinction M13 needs when you ask whether a Bayesian network's arrows mean anything.

    Core Free Intermediate ~20 min
    Used in M20
  3. R-92 The Frame Problem — Murray Shanahan, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, revised 2016

    The essential service this entry performs is separating the narrow technical frame problem — how to axiomatise non-change efficiently, which was solved — from the broad epistemological problem philosophers borrowed the name for, which was not. Confusing the two is the standard error, and reading this before M12 prevents it.

    Core Free Intermediate ~40 min
    Used in M12
  4. R-93 The Chinese Room Argument — David Cole, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, revised 2020

    Four decades of replies to Searle, laid out systematically with the objections and counter-objections in order. Read it after R-71 rather than instead of it — the entry assumes you know the argument and is valuable precisely because it maps the debate rather than restating the thought experiment.

    Free Intermediate ~50 min
    Used in M21
  5. R-94 The Turing Test — Graham Oppy & David Dowe, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, revised 2021

    A careful reconstruction of what Turing actually proposed, the several distinct tests that have been attributed to him, and the objections that have accumulated. Useful in M21 as a corrective — a surprising amount of what "the Turing test" is said to be is not in the 1950 paper.

    Free Beginner ~45 min
    Used in M21
  6. R-95 Logic and Artificial Intelligence — Richmond Thomason, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, revised 2020

    The historical and conceptual argument for what logic was supposed to do for AI, and an unusually fair account of what happened to that programme. The sections on nonmonotonic reasoning and on the relationship between logic and probability are the ones that support M08, M09 and M12.

    Free Intermediate ~60 min
    Used in M08
  7. R-96 Rodney Brooks's blog — Rodney A. Brooks, ongoing

    The author of R-49, still writing, and the most useful single corrective to AI hype cycles — partly because he makes dated, falsifiable predictions and then scores them annually against reality. His posts on the seven deadly sins of predicting the future of AI and on the difference between performance and competence are the ones to start with.

    Free Beginner
    Used in M20
  8. R-97 AI: A Guide for Thinking Humans — Melanie Mitchell, ongoing

    Mitchell's newsletter, which does the thing this page most wants you to be able to do: take a published claim about AI capability, look at what was actually measured, and report the gap. Her series on whether LLMs can reason is the most careful non-partisan treatment of the M22 question available.

    Free Beginner

Practice: problem sets & project ideas

The Berkeley projects are the best assignments in this subject and are the single highest-value item on this page after the two textbooks. The competition archives are the other kind of practice: real instances, with a public record of what the best systems achieve on them.

    Six projects covering search, multi-agent search, MDPs and RL, Bayes nets and particle filters, each with an autograder.     Domains, problem instances and results from every competition, which is the benchmark suite the entire planning literature reports against.     Benchmark instances and solver results going back two decades — the cleanest public record of sustained practical progress on an NP-complete problem, and the source of non-toy instances for M06's exercise. Communities & staying current

Classical AI's literature is conference-driven, and the conferences are specialised: planning goes to ICAPS, knowledge representation to KR, multi-agent work to AAMAS, and AAAI and IJCAI take everything. Following the right one is more efficient than following the field.

  1. R-119 ICAPS: International Conference on Automated Planning and Scheduling — annual

    The planning community's venue, with open proceedings. It is also where the careful work on language models and planning is being published and reviewed by people who know what a plan is — which makes it a better source on that question than the general machine-learning venues. Start with the most recent proceedings' best-paper list.

    Core Free Advanced
    Used in M22
  2. R-120 AAAI: Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence — annual conference, plus AI Magazine

    The broad venue, and the publisher of the open archive that carries most of the historical papers on this page. AI Magazine is the underrated part: it publishes survey and retrospective articles aimed at practitioners, which is exactly the register missing from most conference proceedings.

    Free Intermediate
    Used in M20
  3. R-121 IJCAI: International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence — annual

    The other broad venue, with open proceedings back to 1969 — which makes it the best free archive of the field's history as well as its present. The award lectures, of which R-79 is one, are consistently the most useful reading in each year's proceedings.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M22
  4. R-122 AAMAS: Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems — annual

    Where M18's material is actively developed: mechanism design, cooperative and competitive multi-agent learning, social choice, and agent-based modelling. The most useful venue if the multi-agent half of this page turns out to be the part you need, and its blue-sky-ideas track is a good survey of what the community thinks is unsolved.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M18
  5. R-123 KR: Principles of Knowledge Representation and Reasoning — biennial, annual since 2018

    The venue for M09 and M12's material — description logics, nonmonotonic reasoning, ontologies, belief revision, and increasingly the interface between knowledge representation and learned models. Small, technical, and the place to look when you want to know whether a knowledge-representation idea has been tried.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M09
  6. R-124 The SAT Association — ongoing

    Runs the SAT conference and the competition, and maintains the summer-school programme. Follow it if M06 was the module that caught you: the community is small, its results are cumulative and measurable, and the annual competition makes progress unusually legible compared with most of AI.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M06
  7. R-125 JAIR: Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research — ongoing, open access

    Fully open access and the journal of record for longer, more thorough work than conferences accommodate — R-53, R-55 and R-65 all appeared here. Its planning and search papers in particular tend to include the experimental detail that conference page limits remove, which makes it the right venue to read when you want to reproduce something.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M22
