Classical Machine Learning

Machine learning before neural networks took the headlines — and still the right tool for most problems that arrive as a table. This syllabus covers the statistical core: what it means to learn from data at all, why generalisation is not guaranteed, the models that dominate structured prediction, and the validation discipline that separates a real result from a leak. It ends where the field is genuinely unsettled: double descent, causal questions asked of predictive models, and whether tabular foundation models change anything.

What this topic is

Classical machine learning is statistics with a different objective function. Where classical statistics asks what a dataset licenses you to conclude about a population, machine learning asks how well a rule fitted on this data will do on data it has never seen — and the shift from inference to prediction changes which questions matter. The central problem is that fitting the training data is trivially easy and worth nothing: a lookup table achieves zero training error and predicts nothing. Everything in this syllabus is a response to that fact, whether by restricting the model class, penalising complexity, averaging over many fitted models, or measuring honestly on held-out data.

"Classical" here means the methods that do not require learned representations: regularised linear models, kernel machines, tree ensembles, mixture models, Gaussian processes, and the theory that describes when any of them generalise. That family is not a historical curiosity. On the structured, tabular, modest-sized data that most organisations actually hold, gradient-boosted trees remain the strongest default and have survived every serious attempt to displace them — a finding that has been re-established, with better controls each time, roughly every two years since 2016. The reason to learn this material before or alongside deep learning is that the discipline it teaches — validation that does not lie, awareness of leakage, calibration, and the distinction between prediction and effect — transfers completely and is the part practitioners most often lack.

Before you start

This syllabus assumes linear algebra to the level of matrix multiplication, rank, eigenvectors and the SVD; probability to the level of random variables, expectation, variance, conditional distributions and the normal distribution; calculus enough to take a gradient and set it to zero; and enough programming to write a fitting loop and a plotting script. It does not assume any prior machine learning, any measure theory, or any deep learning.

  • Linear algebra, if the SVD is not something you can picture — it carries M03, M07 and M11, and any first course suffices, but Chapter 2 of The Elements of Statistical Learning assumes it rather than teaching it.
  • Probability and statistics, if maximum likelihood is unfamiliar — All of Statistics is the fastest complete refresher and is written for exactly this transition.
  • Convex optimisation, needed properly only in M05 and M07 — Chapters 2–5 of Convex Optimization cover duality and KKT, which is all this page uses.
  • Python with NumPy, or R — every exercise here can be done in either, and An Introduction to Statistical Learning ships in both languages.

Not covered here: neural networks of any kind. Multilayer perceptrons, convolutions, transformers, representation learning and deep reinforcement learning all live on Deep Learning, and generative modelling lives on the two generative-AI pages. Also absent: probabilistic graphical models — Bayesian networks, d-separation and exact inference are on Classical Artificial Intelligence, which owns them, and this page uses only the mixture models and Bayesian inference that stand alone. Reinforcement learning is likewise on those two pages. Finally, this is not an MLOps course: M18 and M20 cover what a modelling practitioner must know about deployment, and stop at the door of the serving infrastructure.

  • Working knowledge (≈35 h): M01–M04, M06, M08, M09, M14, M20. Enough to build a defensible tabular model, validate it honestly and explain the result. Do the M06 leakage exercise before anything else; it is the single highest-value hour on this page.
  • The practitioner route (≈60 h): the above plus M10, M11, M15, M17, M18, M19. Skips the theory and the Bayesian material. This is the path if you build models for a living and want to stop being surprised in production.   • The theory route (≈50 h): M01–M03, M05, M07, M12, M13, M16, M19. Heavy on why things work and light on tooling. Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms and Foundations of Machine Learning are the continuations.
  • Full course (≈110 h): every module in order, every checkpoint answered before moving on. M15 is the only one you can skip without breaking anything downstream.
  • Already working in this area: start at M06, then M13, then M16 and M19. The gaps in a self-taught practitioner's knowledge are almost always in honest validation, in what the theory does and does not promise, and in the difference between a good predictor and a causal claim — in that order of frequency.

What you'll be able to do

  • State the assumptions under which empirical risk minimisation is expected to generalise, and name which one a given dataset violates.
  • Derive the bias–variance decomposition for squared loss, and explain why it does not transfer cleanly to 0–1 loss.
  • Explain ridge regression through the SVD — which directions get shrunk, how much, and why — rather than as "a penalty that keeps weights small".
  • Diagnose a miscalibrated classifier from a reliability diagram and fix it without retraining.
  • Build a validation scheme that survives grouped, temporal and repeated-measure structure, and spot the leak in someone else's.
  • Derive the SVM dual, state the representer theorem, and say precisely what the kernel trick buys and what it costs.
  • Explain why random forests barely overfit as trees are added while boosted ensembles certainly do.
  • Tune gradient boosting deliberately — the interaction between learning rate, depth, subsampling and early stopping — instead of by random search alone.
  • Say what a t-SNE plot does not preserve, and produce convincing-looking clusters from pure noise to prove it.
  • Fit a Gaussian process, read its uncertainty band correctly, and distinguish epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty.
  • State a uniform-convergence bound and explain why it is vacuous for the models people actually deploy.
  • Show, with a simulation, that the most accurate predictor can give the wrong answer to an intervention question.
  • Read SHAP values correctly, including what happens to them under correlated features.
  • Name two fairness criteria that provably cannot hold simultaneously, and say what that forces you to choose.

Syllabus

M01

The learning problem, stated precisely enough to fail

≈5 h

Most people meet machine learning as a library call and never encounter the question the library is answering. That question — why should a rule fitted on past data work on future data at all? — has a precise formulation, a set of assumptions, and a theorem saying that without those assumptions nothing works. Every later module is a way of managing the gap the formulation exposes, so installing it now makes the rest of the page cohere rather than reading as a catalogue of algorithms.

Concepts

  • The formal setup — an unknown joint distribution over inputs and outputs, a hypothesis class, a loss function, and the split between risk (what you want) and empirical risk (what you can measure)
    • Empirical risk minimisation as the algorithm behind almost everything here, and the immediate observation that ERM over an unrestricted class returns a lookup table
    • The i.i.d. assumption doing all the load-bearing work, and the fact that it is false for essentially every real dataset — time series, repeated measures, network data, anything sampled by a process that changed
    • Approximation error versus estimation error: the gap from restricting the class, and the gap from having finite data. Every regularisation decision trades one for the other
  • Loss functions as assumptions in disguise
    • Squared error is the maximum-likelihood estimator under additive Gaussian noise, which is a claim about your data and not a neutral default
    • Absolute error estimates the conditional median, not the mean; quantile loss estimates a quantile; log loss estimates a probability. Choosing a loss is choosing what you are estimating
    • Surrogate losses: 0–1 loss is not differentiable, hinge and logistic are the convex surrogates, and consistency of a surrogate is a real theoretical question rather than a technicality
  • Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning as three genuinely different problems, plus the semi-supervised and self-supervised cases that sit between them
    • Why unsupervised learning is harder to evaluate rather than easier to do — there is no held-out answer, which M10 makes concrete
  • The no-free-lunch theorem, stated properly, and what it does and does not license
    • Averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm has identical expected off-training performance
    • What it actually means: performance comes from your inductive bias matching the world, not from the algorithm being clever. It does not mean all algorithms are equally good on real problems, and it is routinely over-claimed in both directions
  • Breiman's two cultures — data modelling versus algorithmic modelling — as the disciplinary split this whole page sits inside
    • The statistician's objection (a model you cannot interpret tells you nothing about the world) and the machine learner's reply (a model that fits the world badly tells you nothing either)
    • Why this argument is not settled and resurfaces in M16 and M17 in modern dress

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 2 in full. It is the densest twenty pages on this page and worth two passes.
  • Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms — Chapters 2–3, for the formal ERM setup and the first generalisation guarantee.
  • Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures — Breiman's paper with the published discussion, which is where the argument actually happens.
  • The Lack of A Priori Distinctions Between Learning Algorithms — Wolpert's no-free-lunch result in the original, to see what is and is not claimed.
  • Stanford CS229: Machine Learning — the opening lectures, for the same setup delivered as a course.
  • All of Statistics: A Concise Course in Statistical Inference — Chapters 6 and 7, if maximum likelihood and the bootstrap are not already comfortable.
  • Cross Validated — read the canonical answers on what generalisation means before forming your own vocabulary for it.

Practice

Generate 200 points from a degree-3 polynomial with noise. Fit polynomials of degree 1 to 25 by least squares and plot training and test error against degree on the same axes. Then refit with only 20 training points and watch the crossover move. You are done when you can state, from your own plot, the degree at which training error stops being informative about test error, and explain why that degree depends on the sample size.

Where people go wrong

Treating "more data always helps" as a law. It helps against estimation error and does nothing at all about approximation error: if your hypothesis class cannot express the target, a million more rows buys you a more precisely estimated wrong answer. The diagnosis is in the learning curve — when training and validation error have converged and both are unacceptable, you have a bias problem and collecting data is the expensive way to not fix it.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: take a dataset someone describes to you, state precisely how it violates the i.i.d. assumption, and say what that violation does to the validity of a standard cross-validation estimate — before writing any code.

M02

Bias, variance, and what the decomposition hides

≈5 h

M01 established that test error is what matters. This module decomposes it, which is the single most useful diagnostic frame in applied machine learning: it converts "the model is bad" into a question with a small number of answers. It is also, in its standard form, an approximation that quietly stops holding for the models the field now uses — and knowing where it breaks is what stops M19's double descent from looking like a paradox.

Concepts

  • The decomposition for squared loss — expected test error at a point equals irreducible noise plus squared bias plus variance — derived rather than quoted
    • The expectation is over training sets, which is the part that makes it conceptually slippery: bias and variance are properties of a fitting procedure, not of a fitted model
    • Irreducible error as the floor, and the practical importance of estimating it before setting a target
  • Why the clean decomposition is specific to squared loss
    • For 0–1 loss the analogous decomposition is not additive, and variance can reduce error when the bias points the wrong way — Domingos's unified decomposition is the standard treatment
    • The consequence: the picture is a good qualitative guide for classification and a bad quantitative one
  • Learning curves as the diagnostic instrument
    • Reading the gap and the level separately: a large gap that closes with data is variance, a small gap at a bad level is bias
    • What each shape implies you should do next — more data, a bigger class, more regularisation, better features — and the fact that these are largely mutually exclusive prescriptions
  • Regularisation as a variance-for-bias trade, made explicit
    • Every hyperparameter that "prevents overfitting" is a position on this curve; the effective degrees of freedom of a fitted model is the quantity being controlled
  • Where the classical picture fails, flagged now and resolved in M19
    • Interpolating models — those with zero training error — are supposed to be at the far right of the U-curve and disastrous. Empirically, past the interpolation threshold, test error often falls again
    • Hold this as an anomaly rather than a refutation: the decomposition is still correct arithmetic, the U-shaped picture of how variance behaves with capacity is what is wrong

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 7 sections 7.1–7.6, which is the authoritative treatment including effective degrees of freedom.
  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning — Chapter 2 section 2.2, for the same material at half the mathematical weight and with better pictures.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Section 4.7, for the decomposition in a Bayesian framing that makes the training-set expectation explicit.
  • Learning From Data — Abu-Mostafa's lectures 8 and 9, which develop bias–variance more carefully than any textbook chapter.
  • StatQuest — the bias–variance and cross-validation videos, if the decomposition does not land from the algebra.
  • ISL labs and exercises — the Chapter 2 lab, which builds the flexibility-versus-error picture empirically.

Practice

Estimate bias and variance empirically. Draw 200 independent training sets from a known generator, fit polynomial regressions of each degree on each, and at a fixed test point compute the mean prediction (for bias) and the spread of predictions (for variance) across the 200 fits. Plot both against degree. You are done when your empirical curves reproduce the textbook shape and when the sum of your three estimated components matches your directly measured test error to within sampling noise.

Where people go wrong

Reading "high variance" as an instruction to add regularisation. It is one of at least four valid responses — more data, more regularisation, a smaller feature set, or bagging (M08) — and they have very different costs and side effects. Bagging in particular reduces variance without adding bias, which is why random forests can be left almost untuned while a regularised linear model cannot. The diagnosis narrows the options; it does not pick one.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: look at a pair of learning curves and say whether collecting twice as much data is worth the money — and justify the answer by pointing at the gap and the asymptote separately.

M03

Linear regression, its geometry, and shrinkage

≈6 h

Linear regression is the model everyone thinks they already know and the one whose details do the most work later. Ridge and lasso are the two canonical regularisers, and understanding them through the SVD rather than as "penalties" is what makes every subsequent regularisation decision — in kernels, in boosting, in neural networks — legible. This is also where the gap between a good predictor and an interpretable set of coefficients first opens, which M16 and M17 spend two modules on.

Concepts

  • Least squares as projection — the fitted values are the orthogonal projection of y onto the column space of X, and the residual is what is left
    • The normal equations, and why you should never solve them as written: forming XᵀX squares the condition number. QR and SVD are the numerically sound routes, and this is a real failure in practice, not a purist's objection
    • The hat matrix, leverage, and its role in diagnostics
    • Gauss–Markov: least squares is best linear unbiased. Every word is a restriction, and abandoning "unbiased" is precisely what the rest of this module does
  • Ridge regression, understood through the SVD
    • Ridge shrinks the coefficient along each principal direction by a factor d²/(d² + λ), so directions with small singular values — the badly determined ones — are shrunk hardest. That is the whole intuition and it is not visible from the penalty form
    • Effective degrees of freedom as the trace of the hat matrix, giving a continuous notion of model complexity
    • Why ridge always has a λ that beats least squares in expected test error, which is the Stein-estimation result underneath
  • The lasso and sparsity
    • Why the L1 ball's corners produce exact zeros and the L2 ball's smooth surface does not — the geometric argument, drawn in two dimensions
    • Coordinate descent with soft thresholding as the algorithm that made it fast; LARS and the piecewise-linear regularisation path
    • What the lasso does badly: correlated predictors (it picks one arbitrarily), and p ≫ n where it saturates at n selected variables
    • Elastic net as the repair, and the grouping effect it buys
  • Reading coefficients, and the discipline required
    • Multicollinearity, variance inflation, and why a coefficient's sign can flip when you add a correlated variable
    • Standardisation before penalisation is not optional — the penalty is not scale-invariant, and forgetting this silently changes which variables get selected
    • The bridge to M16: a regression coefficient is a conditional association, and calling it an effect requires assumptions the regression does not contain

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 3 in full. Sections 3.4 and 3.8 are the core; the SVD treatment of ridge in 3.4.1 is the passage this module is built around.
  • Ridge Regression: Biased Estimation for Nonorthogonal Problems — Hoerl and Kennard's original, for the argument that accepting bias was a deliberate and defensible choice.
  • Regression Shrinkage and Selection Via the Lasso — Tibshirani's 1996 paper, still the clearest statement of what sparsity is for.
  • Regularization and Variable Selection Via the Elastic Net — Zou and Hastie, including the concrete failure cases of the lasso that motivated it.
  • Statistical Learning with Sparsity — Chapters 2 and 4, for coordinate descent and the path algorithms in depth.
  • Trevor Hastie's lectures and talks — his glmnet talks, where a co-author of the lasso papers explains what the path plot is actually showing.

Practice

Implement ridge by SVD and lasso by coordinate descent with soft thresholding, both from scratch. On a dataset with correlated predictors, plot the full regularisation path for each — every coefficient against log λ — and mark the λ chosen by cross-validation. You are done when your paths match glmnet's or scikit-learn's to numerical precision, and when you can point to two correlated variables and show the lasso arbitrarily selecting one while ridge splits the weight between them.

Where people go wrong

Fitting a penalised model on unstandardised features. The penalty applies equally to every coefficient, so a variable measured in millimetres gets a coefficient a thousand times larger than the same variable in metres and is penalised a thousand times harder. The model still fits and the error looks plausible, so nothing alerts you — but the variables the lasso selects are now partly determined by your unit choices. Standardise inside the cross-validation fold, not before splitting, or you have also created the leak M06 is about.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain, using the SVD, why ridge regression shrinks some directions far more than others, and predict which columns of a specific design matrix will have their coefficients most affected — before running the fit.

M04

Classification, and whether the numbers are probabilities

≈6 h

Classification is where the metric you choose starts making decisions for you, and where most applied mistakes happen. This module covers the two standard model families and then spends its real weight on calibration — because a score that is ranked correctly but is not a probability will pass every AUC check and then produce systematically wrong decisions the moment anyone multiplies it by a cost. That failure is invisible to the metrics people usually report.

Concepts

  • Logistic regression, properly
    • The log-odds parameterisation and why it is the natural link; maximum likelihood, the absence of a closed form, and iteratively reweighted least squares as Newton's method in disguise
    • Separable data and the divergence of the MLE — coefficients run to infinity, and regularisation is what actually stops it
    • Multiclass by softmax versus one-versus-rest, and where they differ
  • Generative versus discriminative classifiers
    • LDA, QDA and naive Bayes model p(x | y) and apply Bayes' rule; logistic regression models p(y | x) directly
    • The Ng and Jordan result: the generative model has higher asymptotic error but approaches it much faster, so it wins in the small-sample regime and loses in the large one — a crossover you can reproduce
    • Why naive Bayes classifies well while its probability estimates are badly wrong, which is the first calibration lesson
  • Metrics, and what each hides
    • Accuracy under class imbalance as the standard trap; the majority-class baseline as the mandatory comparison
    • ROC-AUC as a ranking measure that is insensitive to calibration and to class balance; precision–recall curves as the right choice when the positive class is rare
    • Threshold selection as a decision problem, not a modelling one — it belongs with the cost structure in M20
  • Calibration, the module's centre of gravity
    • Reliability diagrams: bucket the predictions, plot observed frequency against predicted probability, and look at the deviation from the diagonal
    • Proper scoring rules — log loss and the Brier score — and their decomposition into calibration and refinement (sharpness)
    • Platt scaling and isotonic regression as post-hoc fixes, fitted on held-out data; when each is appropriate and how isotonic overfits on small validation sets
    • Which models are badly calibrated out of the box and why: naive Bayes (independence assumption), SVMs (hinge loss estimates nothing probabilistic), boosted trees (pushed toward 0 and 1), bagged trees (pulled toward the middle)

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 4, for LDA, logistic regression and the comparison between them.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapters 9 and 10, which are the best modern treatment of discriminative versus generative classifiers.
  • Predicting Good Probabilities with Supervised Learning — Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana's empirical study of which model families are miscalibrated in which direction. This is the paper that makes the topic concrete.
  • scikit-learn documentation — the probability-calibration user guide, which has the reliability diagrams for every common estimator already plotted.
  • Frank Harrell's Statistical Thinking — his posts on why classification accuracy is the wrong metric — the sharpest available statement of this module's argument.

Practice

On an imbalanced binary dataset, fit logistic regression, a random forest, a boosted tree ensemble and an SVM. Plot all four reliability diagrams on one axis, and record ROC-AUC and Brier score for each. Then apply Platt scaling and isotonic regression on a held-out calibration split and re-plot. You are done when AUC is essentially unchanged by calibration while Brier score improves — because that gap is the entire point of the module.

Where people go wrong

Treating predict_proba output as a probability. For an SVM it is a monotone transform of a distance with no probabilistic meaning at all; for a boosted ensemble it is systematically pushed toward the extremes. Anyone who then computes an expected value — expected revenue, expected loss, a triage threshold — is multiplying a cost by a number that is not a probability, and the error is invisible in AUC. Check the reliability diagram before any score is used in an expectation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain how a model can have excellent AUC and terrible Brier score, construct such a model deliberately, and say which downstream uses are broken by it and which are unaffected.

M05

Optimisation, and why convexity was worth having

≈5 h

Every model so far has been defined by an objective and fitted by an algorithm you have so far taken on trust. This module makes the algorithms explicit, and it is placed here because the SVM in M07 cannot be understood at all without Lagrangian duality — the kernel trick is a consequence of the dual's form, not an independent idea. It is also the module that explains what deep learning gave up: this entire body of theory rests on convexity, and neural network objectives are not convex.

Concepts

  • Convexity and what it guarantees — every local minimum is global, and a certificate of optimality exists
    • Which of the models on this page are convex problems (linear and logistic regression with convex penalties, SVMs) and which are not (k-means, GMMs, neural networks, tree fitting)
    • Strong convexity and smoothness as the two parameters that determine convergence rates
  • First-order methods
    • Gradient descent and the condition number: convergence is governed by the ratio of largest to smallest curvature, which is why feature scaling is an optimisation issue and not only a regularisation one
    • Momentum and accelerated gradient; the O(1/k²) rate and what it means practically
    • Stochastic gradient descent, the variance/step-size tradeoff, and Robbins–Monro conditions on the schedule
    • Proximal methods and why they handle the non-differentiable L1 penalty cleanly
  • Second-order and coordinate methods
    • Newton's method, its quadratic local convergence and its O(p³) per-step cost; IRLS for logistic regression as exactly this
    • Quasi-Newton and L-BFGS as the compromise that most classical solvers actually use
    • Coordinate descent, and why it beats gradient methods for the lasso specifically — the per-coordinate subproblem has a closed form
  • Constrained optimisation and duality, which M07 needs
    • The Lagrangian, the dual function, weak and strong duality, Slater's condition
    • The KKT conditions, and complementary slackness as the statement that only active constraints have non-zero multipliers — which is exactly why only support vectors matter
    • Reading a dual problem as a change of variables from p unknowns to n unknowns, which is the structural reason kernels work

Work through

  • Convex Optimization — Chapters 2 and 3 for convexity, 5 for duality and KKT, 9 for descent methods. Free from Boyd's page, and Chapter 5 is the one M07 depends on.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapter 8, which surveys the optimisation methods in exactly the coverage a machine-learning reader needs.
  • Statistical Learning with Sparsity — Chapter 5, on proximal and coordinate methods for penalised problems.
  • Convex Optimization lectures — Boyd's Stanford lecture videos, if the book's Chapter 5 does not land on the first reading.
  • Distill — the interactive article on momentum, which shows the conditioning effect far better than any static figure.

Practice

Fit the same regularised logistic regression four ways: gradient descent with a fixed step, gradient descent with momentum, Newton/IRLS, and L-BFGS. Plot objective value against iteration and against wall-clock time — they will rank differently. Then artificially raise the design matrix's condition number by scaling one column by 1,000 and re-run. You are done when gradient descent's iteration count degrades by orders of magnitude while Newton's barely moves, and when you can explain that difference in terms of the condition number.

Where people go wrong

Tuning the learning rate to fix a problem that is conditioning. If the objective's curvature differs by a factor of 10⁴ across directions, no scalar step size is right — it is too large for the steep directions and too small for the flat ones, and you will oscillate along one while crawling along another. Standardising the features, or switching to a method that uses curvature, fixes it in one step. This is the same diagnosis that reappears as normalisation on the deep-learning page.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: write down the Lagrangian dual of a constrained quadratic problem, state the KKT conditions, and explain via complementary slackness why the solution depends on only a subset of the constraints — which is the result M07 turns into support vectors.

M06

Validation that does not lie, and the many ways it does

≈6 h

This module is deliberately placed before the model zoo, because everything after it is judged with the instrument built here. It is also, by a wide margin, the module with the highest ratio of consequence to difficulty: the mathematics is trivial and the failure modes are catastrophic and common. Published results, Kaggle leaderboards and production models have all been invalidated by leaks that a correct pipeline would have made impossible.

Concepts

  • The three-way split and what each set is for
    • A test set consulted twice is a validation set; a validation set used for a hundred model comparisons is a training set for your model-selection decision
    • The winner's curse: the best of k models evaluated on the same validation set has an optimistically biased score, and the bias grows with k. This is why the selected model's validation score is not an estimate of its performance
    • Nested cross-validation as the honest answer when you both tune and estimate, and its cost
  • Cross-validation, and its own bias and variance
    • k-fold, stratified k-fold, repeated k-fold, leave-one-out; why LOOCV has low bias and high variance and is rarely the right choice
    • k = 5 or 10 as the empirical compromise, and what the choice actually trades
    • The one-standard-error rule: choose the simplest model within one standard error of the best, which is a principled defence against selecting on noise
  • Leakage, in its several distinct forms — the practical heart of the module
    • Preprocessing leakage: fitting a scaler, imputer, feature selector or target encoder on all the data before splitting. The single most common bug in applied machine learning
    • Target leakage: a feature that is a consequence of the outcome rather than a predictor of it — the "was assigned to the intensive-care ward" column in a mortality model
    • Group leakage: the same patient, user or device appearing in both train and test, so the model memorises the entity rather than the pattern
    • Temporal leakage: any use of future information, including a random split on time-ordered data — the subject of M15
    • Duplicate leakage: near-duplicate rows straddling the split, which is endemic in scraped datasets
    • The diagnostic that catches most of them: a result that is much better than the domain expert expected is a leak until proven otherwise
  • Hyperparameter search, done deliberately
    • Grid versus random search, and Bergstra and Bengio's argument that random wins when only a few dimensions matter
    • Bayesian optimisation and its surrogate models; successive halving and Hyperband as the bandit framing that allocates compute rather than points
    • Search budget as a form of capacity: a large enough search over a validation set overfits it, and this is why the test set has to stay untouched

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 7 sections 7.10–7.12, including the famous "wrong way to do cross-validation" example with feature selection, which is worth reproducing.
  • Leakage in Data Mining: Formulation, Detection, and Avoidance — Kaufman, Rosset and Perlich's taxonomy, with real competition examples where leaks decided the winner.
  • A Survey of Cross-Validation Procedures for Model Selection — Arlot and Celisse, for the statistical properties of the estimator itself rather than the recipe.
  • scikit-learn documentation — the cross-validation and Pipeline guides. Pipeline exists precisely to make preprocessing leakage structurally impossible, and this is the argument for using it everywhere.
  • Optuna — for hyperparameter search with pruning; the ask-and-tell interface makes the search budget explicit rather than hidden.
  • Common pitfalls and recommended practices — scikit-learn's own document, the most concentrated leakage advice available; read it before the exercise.
  • Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference, and Social Science — Gelman on the garden of forking paths — the same selection problem seen from the statistics side.

Practice

Build a leak deliberately. Take a modest dataset, scale the features and fit a variance-threshold feature selector on the full data, then split and cross-validate — record the inflated score. Then reproduce the ESL feature-selection example: on pure noise with 5,000 features, select the 100 most correlated with the label using all the data, then cross-validate a classifier on those, and watch it report far better than chance on data with no signal whatsoever. Finally, rebuild both inside a proper pipeline and watch the scores collapse to chance. You are done when you have all three numbers written down.

Where people go wrong

Splitting after preprocessing. It feels tidier to clean the whole dataset first and split at the end, and it invalidates the result — the imputer, the scaler and especially any target encoder have all seen the test rows. The structural fix is never to hold a preprocessing step outside a pipeline object, so that fitting on a fold is the only thing the API lets you do. Treat any notebook where fit_transform is called before train_test_split as broken on sight.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: be handed a dataset with patients, multiple visits each, timestamps and a diagnosis column, and design a validation scheme that survives all four leakage types — and say what each of the three obvious wrong schemes would have over-reported.

M07

Kernels and support vector machines

≈6 h

The kernel trick is the most elegant idea in classical machine learning: an implicit map into an infinite-dimensional space at finite cost, arriving as a consequence of M05's duality rather than as a trick bolted on. SVMs dominated the 1995–2010 period and then lost, and understanding why they lost — a scaling wall, not a conceptual defect — is more instructive than either their rise or their current neglect. Kernel methods also remain the cleanest bridge to the Gaussian processes in M12, which are the same mathematics with a probabilistic reading.

Concepts

  • Maximum margin classification
    • The geometric margin, the optimisation problem, and why maximising the margin is a structural-risk argument rather than an aesthetic preference
    • Soft margin and the slack variables; C as the tradeoff between margin width and violations, and its equivalence to a regularisation parameter
    • Hinge loss as the convex surrogate that produces this, and the comparison with logistic loss — they differ mainly in what they do to well-classified points
  • The dual, and the trick that falls out of it
    • Deriving the dual from M05's Lagrangian; the observation that the data enters only as inner products xᵢᵀxⱼ
    • Replacing that inner product with a kernel k(xᵢ, xⱼ) computes an inner product in a feature space you never construct — with the RBF kernel that space is infinite-dimensional
    • Mercer's condition and positive semi-definiteness as the requirement for a valid kernel; how to build new kernels from old ones
    • Complementary slackness making most multipliers zero: the solution depends only on the support vectors, which is where the sparsity comes from
    • The representer theorem: for any penalised loss in an RKHS, the optimum is a finite combination of kernel evaluations at the training points — the general statement of which the SVM is one instance
  • Practical kernel machines
    • RBF, polynomial and linear kernels; the joint geometry of C and gamma and why a log-scale grid over both is the standard search
    • Kernel ridge regression and support vector regression as the same machinery for continuous targets
    • Kernels on structured objects — strings, graphs, sets — where feature engineering is genuinely hard and this is a real advantage
  • Why SVMs lost, stated precisely
    • The kernel matrix is n × n: O(n²) memory and roughly O(n²–n³) training. Past a few hundred thousand rows this is fatal, and the constant does not save you
    • No native probability output (Platt scaling is a bolt-on), awkward multiclass, and hyperparameters that must be tuned rather than defaulted
    • Tree ensembles matched them on tabular data with less tuning; neural networks beat them on perceptual data by learning features rather than fixing a kernel
    • What survives: random Fourier features and Nyström approximation, which turn a kernel machine back into a linear model at controlled approximation error, and the theory that underpins M12

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 12 sections 12.1–12.3, which derives the dual and the kernel formulation cleanly.
  • Support-Vector Networks — Cortes and Vapnik's 1995 paper, the soft-margin formulation in the original.
  • Foundations of Machine Learning — Chapters 5 and 6, for kernels, RKHS and the margin bounds, which is the rigorous version.
  • Learning From Data — lectures 14 and 15, which build the SVM geometrically before touching the algebra.
  • Cornell CS4780: Machine Learning for Intelligent Systems — Weinberger's SVM and kernel lectures, which derive the dual at the board.
  • Foundations of Machine Learning course materials — the kernel and margin-bound slides, as the step between the lectures and the proofs.

Practice

On a 2D toy dataset, fit an RBF SVM and plot the decision boundary, the margin and the support vectors for a grid of C and gamma spanning four orders of magnitude each. Count support vectors in each cell. Then scale one feature by 1,000 and re-run without restandardising. You are done when you can predict the shape of the boundary from (C, gamma) before plotting it, and when the unscaled run visibly collapses to a boundary that ignores the other feature.

Where people go wrong

Running an RBF SVM on unstandardised features. The kernel is a function of Euclidean distance, so a feature with a large numeric range dominates the distance entirely and the other features stop existing. Unlike the linear case, no amount of tuning gamma recovers this, because gamma is a single scalar applied to an already-distorted metric. The same applies to k-nearest neighbours, k-means and any other distance-based method — which is most of M10 and M11.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the representer theorem, explain via the dual why the kernel trick is possible at all, and give the specific computational reason SVMs are a poor choice at a million rows — in terms of the kernel matrix rather than "they are slow".

M08

Trees, bagging and random forests

≈5 h

A single decision tree is a poor model with two excellent properties: it needs no feature scaling, and it is unstable in a very specific way — small changes to the data produce large changes to the tree. Bagging turns that instability from a defect into the mechanism, which is one of the few genuinely surprising results in this field. Random forests are the strongest model on this page that you can run without tuning, which makes them the correct second baseline after a linear model.

Concepts

  • CART, and the greedy recursion
    • Splitting criteria — Gini and entropy for classification, sum of squares for regression — and the fact that Gini and entropy almost never disagree in practice
    • Why the algorithm is greedy: finding the optimal tree is NP-hard, so each split is chosen without regard to what it forecloses. The consequence is that trees miss XOR-like structure that a lookahead would find
    • Cost-complexity pruning and the weakest-link path; why growing then pruning beats early stopping
    • Handling of categoricals, missing values via surrogate splits, and monotone constraints — the practical features that keep trees in production
  • Bagging, and why it works
    • The bootstrap as the resampling device (Efron), and bagging as averaging over bootstrap-fitted models
    • The variance argument: averaging B i.i.d. estimators with variance σ² gives σ²/B, but bootstrap fits are correlated, so the variance floor is ρσ² — reducing ρ is therefore the whole game
    • Bagging reduces variance and leaves bias essentially unchanged, which is why it helps unstable low-bias models (trees) and does nothing for stable ones (linear regression)
    • Out-of-bag error: roughly 37% of rows are omitted from each bootstrap, giving a free validation estimate — and its subtle bias when the ensemble is small
  • Random forests as bagging plus decorrelation
    • Sampling a random subset of features at each split, which is the mechanism that reduces ρ; mtry as the one hyperparameter that matters
    • Why adding trees never overfits — the ensemble converges to a limit as B grows, so B is a compute budget rather than a capacity parameter. This is the property people find counter-intuitive and it follows directly from the averaging
    • Extremely randomised trees, which push randomisation into the split threshold itself
  • Variable importance, and its documented biases
    • Mean decrease in impurity is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features — a categorical with 50 levels will look important even when it is pure noise, and this is reproducible in a two-minute experiment
    • Permutation importance as the better default, and its own failure under correlated features (permuting one of two correlated columns understates both)
    • Conditional and grouped permutation importance as partial repairs; and the reminder from M16 that none of these is a causal effect

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapters 9.2 (CART) and 15 (random forests), including the correlation argument in 15.2.
  • Random Forests — Breiman's 2001 paper, which contains the generalisation-error bound in terms of tree strength and correlation.
  • Bagging Predictors — the earlier and simpler paper; read it first, because random forests only make sense as bagging with one extra idea.
  • Bootstrap Methods: Another Look at the Jackknife — Efron's original, for where the resampling device comes from.
  • Induction of Decision Trees — Quinlan's ID3 paper, for the information-gain framing that predates CART.
  • scikit-learn examples gallery — the permutation-importance-with-multicollinear-features example, which is this module's practice exercise already written.

Practice

Reproduce the impurity-importance bias. Build a dataset where the target depends only on one binary feature, then add a pure-noise categorical column with 100 random levels and a pure-noise continuous column. Fit a random forest and print mean-decrease-in-impurity importances — the noise columns will rank highly. Then compute permutation importance on held-out data and watch them fall to zero. You are done when you have both rankings side by side and can explain the mechanism.

Where people go wrong

Reading the default feature-importance output as a statement about the world. Impurity importance measures how often and how usefully a feature was split on, which rewards features that offer many possible split points regardless of signal. Practitioners routinely present these bars to stakeholders, and a high-cardinality identifier column will sit at the top. Use permutation importance on held-out data, and even then say "the model relies on this", never "this drives the outcome".

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why increasing the number of trees in a random forest cannot cause overfitting while increasing the number of rounds in gradient boosting certainly can — in terms of what each additional model is fitted to.

M09

Gradient boosting, and why it still wins on tables

≈6 h

Boosting is the opposite construction to M08's bagging — sequential rather than parallel, bias-reducing rather than variance-reducing — and it is the single most consequential model family in applied machine learning outside of deep learning. It is also the one whose hyperparameters genuinely interact, so tuning it by intuition fails. This module ends with the empirical question the field keeps re-asking and keeps getting the same answer to: why deep learning has not displaced it on tabular data.

Concepts

  • From AdaBoost to the statistical view
    • AdaBoost as reweighting: upweight the misclassified, fit again, combine with weights derived from each learner's error
    • The Friedman–Hastie–Tibshirani reinterpretation: AdaBoost is forward stagewise additive modelling under exponential loss, which turned a clever algorithm into a member of a family
    • Why exponential loss is a poor choice for noisy data — it grows exponentially in the margin, so a mislabelled point can dominate
  • Gradient boosting as functional gradient descent
    • Fit each new tree to the negative gradient of the loss at the current predictions; for squared loss those are the residuals, which is the special case people remember
    • The generality that follows: any differentiable loss works, which is why boosting handles ranking, quantile regression, survival and Poisson targets with no new machinery
    • The three regularisers and how they interact: learning rate (shrinkage), number of rounds, and tree depth. Lower learning rate needs more rounds — they are one knob, not two — while depth controls the order of interactions the model can express
    • Stochastic gradient boosting: row and column subsampling, which adds variance reduction to a bias-reducing method
    • Early stopping on a validation fold as the mandatory practice, because unlike M08 more rounds does eventually overfit
  • What the modern implementations actually changed
    • XGBoost: a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss, an explicit regularisation term on leaf weights and counts, sparsity-aware split finding that learns a default direction for missing values, and the weighted quantile sketch for approximate splits
    • LightGBM: histogram binning of features (turning split finding from sorting into counting), leaf-wise rather than level-wise growth, gradient-based one-side sampling and exclusive feature bundling
    • CatBoost: ordered target statistics for categorical encoding, and ordered boosting — both of which exist to remove a specific target leak that naive mean-encoding introduces, which is the M06 lesson appearing inside a model
  • The tabular question, with the evidence
    • Controlled comparisons keep finding tree ensembles ahead of neural networks on medium-sized tabular data, and the proposed mechanisms — robustness to uninformative features, resistance to non-smooth target functions, invariance to feature rotation being an advantage rather than a limitation
    • The methodological caveat that makes this argument careful: much of the tabular deep-learning literature under-tunes its baselines, and the finding survives mainly in papers that tune both sides under equal budgets

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 10, which is where the statistical view of boosting was worked out by three of its authors.
  • Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine — Friedman's paper. The functional-gradient framing is the thing to extract.
  • A Decision-Theoretic Generalization of On-Line Learning and an Application to Boosting — Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost, for the original weighting argument.
  • XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System — read sections 2 and 3 for the regularised objective and the split-finding algorithms.
  • CatBoost: Unbiased Boosting with Categorical Features — specifically for the target-leakage analysis, which is the clearest exposition of why naive mean encoding is broken.
  • Why Do Tree-Based Models Still Outperform Deep Learning on Typical Tabular Data? — the controlled benchmark, with the proposed explanations tested one at a time.
  • XGBoost documentation — the parameter guide and the tuning notes, which are unusually honest about which parameters matter.
  • LightGBM documentation — the features page, for histogram binning, leaf-wise growth, GOSS and EFB stated by the implementers.
  • CatBoost — run it against XGBoost on a categorical-heavy table and see how much of the gap is encoding rather than boosting.
  • ML Contests — its annual report, as observational evidence about what actually wins on tabular problems.

Practice

Take a tabular benchmark with a proper train/validation/test split. Fit a random forest with defaults, then tune gradient boosting with a fixed budget of 200 trials over learning rate, depth, subsample, colsample and L2, using early stopping on the validation fold. Record the test score of both, plus the untuned boosting baseline. Then plot validation loss against boosting round for three learning rates. You are done when the learning-rate/rounds tradeoff is visible in your own plot, and when you can say how much of the tuned model's advantage over the forest came from tuning rather than from the model family.

Where people go wrong

Early stopping on the test set. It is the easiest leak to commit because the API makes it a one-line argument, and it converts your test set into a hyperparameter — the number of rounds — chosen to optimise it. The reported score is then biased by an amount that grows with how much the loss curve wanders. Always pass a dedicated validation fold to the early-stopping callback, and if you have too little data for three splits, use nested cross-validation instead of borrowing the test set.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain what ordered boosting fixes and why the naive alternative leaks, and separately state the empirical case for tree ensembles over neural networks on tabular data along with the methodological caveat that makes it contestable.

M10

Clustering and mixture models, and the evaluation problem

≈6 h

Everything so far had a label to check against. Unsupervised learning removes it, and the consequence is not that the problem gets easier but that it stops being well-posed: there is no ground truth, so "which clustering is right" reduces to "which assumptions did you make". This module teaches the algorithms and then spends real time on that consequence, because clustering results are presented to decision-makers more confidently than almost anything else in this syllabus and deserve it less.

Concepts

  • k-means and its geometry
    • The objective (within-cluster sum of squares), Lloyd's algorithm as alternating assignment and update, and the guarantee: monotone decrease, convergence to a local optimum, no global guarantee
    • k-means++ seeding and its O(log k) approximation guarantee — a rare case where a smarter initialisation comes with a proof
    • The implicit assumptions: spherical, equally sized, equally dense clusters, and Euclidean distance being meaningful. Violating any of them produces confident nonsense
    • Choosing k — elbow, silhouette, gap statistic — and the honest assessment that all three are weak and frequently disagree
  • Gaussian mixture models and EM
    • The generative story, and soft assignment as the difference from k-means; k-means as the limiting case with spherical equal covariances
    • The E step (posterior responsibilities) and the M step (weighted MLE), derived rather than stated
    • Why EM never decreases the likelihood — the ELBO/Jensen argument — and why that guarantees neither a global optimum nor a good clustering
    • Degeneracy: a component collapsing onto a single point sends the likelihood to infinity, so unregularised MLE for GMMs is ill-posed. Covariance regularisation is not optional
    • EM's generality: the same argument covers missing data, hidden Markov models and factor analysis
  • Clustering families with different assumptions, and what each buys
    • Hierarchical: linkage criteria (single, complete, average, Ward), dendrograms, and single-linkage's chaining behaviour as a feature or a bug depending on the data
    • Density-based: DBSCAN's core/border/noise trichotomy, its ability to find non-convex clusters and to refuse to assign outliers, its sensitivity to eps, and HDBSCAN as the version that removes that parameter
    • Spectral clustering: the graph Laplacian, embedding into eigenvector space, then k-means there; effective for manifold-shaped clusters and expensive
  • Evaluation, and why it is the hard part
    • Internal indices (silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz) measure conformity to an assumed cluster shape, so they systematically favour the algorithm that shares their assumption
    • External indices (adjusted Rand, normalised mutual information) require labels, which is the thing you did not have
    • Stability-based assessment — does the clustering survive resampling? — as the most defensible general method
    • The unavoidable conclusion: clustering is exploratory, its output is a hypothesis, and it should be validated against something external before anyone acts on it

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 14 sections 14.3 and 14.5, covering clustering and the evaluation difficulty.
  • Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning — Chapter 9 in full. Bishop's EM derivation, including the general ELBO argument in 9.4, is the best in print.
  • Maximum Likelihood from Incomplete Data via the EM Algorithm — Dempster, Laird and Rubin's 1977 paper, which named and unified the method.
  • Least Squares Quantization in PCM — Lloyd's paper, written in 1957 and published in 1982, for where the algorithm actually comes from.
  • scikit-learn documentation — the clustering comparison page, which runs ten algorithms on six synthetic datasets and is the fastest way to see the assumption differences.
  • Bayesian Reasoning and Machine Learning — Barber's chapters on EM and mixture models, which treat inference rather than models as the organising subject.

Practice

Generate three datasets: isotropic blobs, two concentric circles, and blobs with very different densities and sizes. Run k-means, GMM, Ward hierarchical, DBSCAN and spectral clustering on all three, and produce a 3×5 grid of plots. Then compute silhouette scores for every cell. You are done when you can show at least one case where the algorithm with the best silhouette score produces the visually and substantively wrong answer — which is the module's argument in one figure.

Where people go wrong

Choosing k by the elbow method and then presenting the clusters as discovered segments. The elbow is frequently absent or ambiguous on real data, silhouette will happily prefer a different k, and none of it establishes that discrete clusters exist at all — k-means will partition a single uniform Gaussian into k neat pieces and report a respectable score. Before acting on a clustering, test whether the structure survives resampling and whether the clusters differ on a variable that was not used to build them.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: prove that EM never decreases the observed-data likelihood, explain why that says nothing about clustering quality, and describe the covariance degeneracy that makes unregularised GMM maximum likelihood ill-posed.

M11

Dimensionality reduction, and how the pictures lie

≈5 h

Reducing dimension serves three separate purposes — compression, denoising, and visualisation — and the methods that are good at the third are actively misleading if you read them as the first two. t-SNE and UMAP plots are among the most-published and most over-interpreted figures in applied machine learning, and the specific properties they do not preserve are well documented and widely ignored. This module exists mostly so you stop believing those plots.

Concepts

  • Principal component analysis from three directions that turn out to be the same
    • Maximise variance along orthogonal directions; minimise squared reconstruction error; take the leading left singular vectors of the centred data matrix
    • The eigenvalue spectrum, scree plots and proportion of variance explained; whitening and when it helps
    • PCA is unsupervised, so the directions of greatest variance need not be the directions that predict y — the two-cluster example where the discriminative direction is the last principal component is worth constructing
    • Probabilistic PCA and factor analysis as the generative versions, and how factor analysis differs by modelling per-variable noise
  • Beyond linear projections
    • Kernel PCA, which is M07's trick applied to the covariance operator
    • ICA, which seeks statistically independent rather than uncorrelated components, and where that matters (source separation)
    • Random projection and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma: a random linear map into O(log n / ε²) dimensions preserves all pairwise distances to within ε, independent of the original dimension. Cheap, and a useful sanity baseline
  • Manifold learning and neighbour embeddings
    • Classical MDS, Isomap (geodesic distances on a neighbour graph) and LLE as the earlier generation
    • t-SNE: model pairwise similarities as distributions in both spaces and minimise their KL divergence; the heavy-tailed Student-t in the low-dimensional space as the fix for crowding; perplexity as an effective neighbourhood size
    • UMAP: a fuzzy simplicial-set construction with a different loss, faster, and better at retaining some global structure — but sharing most of t-SNE's caveats
    • What neither preserves: cluster sizes (a tight cluster and a diffuse one can render identically), between-cluster distances (they are essentially arbitrary), and density. Both will also produce apparent clusters from data with none
    • Both are stochastic and hyperparameter-sensitive: different seeds or perplexities give visibly different pictures of the same data

Work through

  • The Elements of Statistical Learning — Chapter 14 sections 14.5–14.9, covering PCA through to the nonlinear methods.
  • UMAP: Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction — read the introduction and the discussion of what the method does and does not claim to preserve.
  • How to Use t-SNE Effectively — the Distill article, which is interactive and demonstrates every failure mode faster than any prose description.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapter 20, for probabilistic PCA, factor analysis and the manifold methods in one consistent notation.
  • UCI Machine Learning Repository — take a high-dimensional dataset from here for the embedding exercise, so the noise is real rather than simulated.

Practice

Sample 1,000 points from a single isotropic Gaussian in 50 dimensions — data with no cluster structure whatsoever. Run t-SNE at perplexities 5, 30 and 100, and UMAP at n_neighbors 5, 15 and 50, plotting all six. Several will show convincing separated clusters. Then take genuinely clustered data and show that the distances between clusters in the embedding do not track the distances in the original space. You are done when you have a figure you would be embarrassed to have published.

Where people go wrong

Measuring anything off a t-SNE or UMAP plot. "These two clusters are close, so those groups are similar" and "this cluster is bigger, so that subpopulation is more variable" are both unsupported — the algorithms optimise local neighbourhood preservation and explicitly trade away global geometry to do it. Use the embedding to generate hypotheses, then test them with a statistic computed in the original space. And never run t-SNE on the test set as part of a pipeline: it is transductive, and fitting it on all the data is the M06 leak in a new costume.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state exactly what PCA optimises, give a concrete case where discarding low-variance components destroys the predictive signal, and list the three properties a t-SNE plot does not preserve.

M12

Bayesian machine learning and Gaussian processes

≈7 h

Everything so far returns a point estimate and, at best, a confidence interval derived from asymptotics. The Bayesian treatment returns a distribution, which is the right object when the decision downstream is sensitive to uncertainty and when data is scarce — the two conditions under which classical machine learning most often fails silently. Gaussian processes are the payoff: M07's kernels, reread as priors over functions, giving exact posterior uncertainty in closed form.

Concepts

  • The Bayesian setup and what it changes
    • Prior, likelihood, posterior, and the posterior predictive — the last being the one that matters for prediction and the one people most often skip
    • Conjugacy as the tractable special case; the beta–binomial and normal–normal pairs as the ones to know by hand
    • Regularisation as a prior: ridge is a Gaussian prior on coefficients, lasso a Laplace prior. This is not an analogy — the MAP estimates are identical, which reframes every penalty in M03
    • Epistemic uncertainty (reducible by data) versus aleatoric uncertainty (inherent noise), and why conflating them produces confidently wrong extrapolation
  • Bayesian linear and hierarchical models
    • Bayesian linear regression in closed form, and the predictive variance growing away from the data — the behaviour that ridge regression has no way to express
    • Hierarchical models and partial pooling: the principled treatment of grouped data, and shrinkage toward group means as an automatic consequence rather than a heuristic
    • Model comparison by marginal likelihood, and its severe sensitivity to the prior — which is why information criteria and cross-validation are often preferred in practice
  • Approximate inference, since conjugacy runs out immediately
    • MCMC: Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the NUTS extension as the modern default
    • Diagnostics that are not optional — R-hat, effective sample size, divergent transitions, trace plots. A chain that has converged in appearance and not in fact is the standard failure, and divergences in HMC are the one diagnostic that actually catches geometry problems
    • Variational inference: turn integration into optimisation by maximising the ELBO over a restricted family; mean-field's characteristic underestimation of posterior variance, and why that bias matters exactly when you wanted the uncertainty
  • Gaussian processes
    • A prior over functions specified by a mean and a covariance kernel; conditioning on observations gives a closed-form Gaussian posterior — this is the whole method
    • Kernel choice as model choice: RBF for smooth functions, Matérn for rougher ones, periodic and linear kernels, and composition by addition and multiplication as a small modelling language
    • Hyperparameter learning by marginal likelihood, and the automatic Occam's razor it exhibits
    • The O(n³) wall from inverting the covariance matrix, and sparse/inducing-point approximations as the standard escape
    • Where GPs are the right answer: small n, expensive observations, and a need for calibrated uncertainty — which is exactly Bayesian optimisation, the hyperparameter-search method from M06

Work through

  • Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning — Chapters 3 and 6. Bishop's Bayesian linear regression and GP treatment are the standard route in.
  • Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning — Chapters 2, 4 and 5. Free from the authors, and the definitive text; Chapter 4 on covariance functions is the practically useful one.
  • Bayesian Data Analysis — Chapters 5 and 11–12, for hierarchical models and for MCMC diagnostics done properly.
  • Variational Inference: A Review for Statisticians — Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, which is the clearest statement of what VI trades away.
  • Stan documentation — the user's guide and, in particular, its diagnostics documentation, which explains divergent transitions better than any textbook.
  • PyMC — for building the models in Python; its example gallery covers hierarchical modelling and GPs with runnable code.
  • Statistical Learning — the Hastie and Tibshirani course segments on shrinkage, which are the frequentist counterpart to this module's priors.

Practice

Fit a Gaussian process to twenty noisy observations of a one-dimensional function, with an RBF kernel whose length-scale and noise are learned by marginal likelihood. Plot the posterior mean and a 95% band across a range that extends well beyond the data. Then fit ridge regression on the same points and overlay it. You are done when the GP's band visibly widens outside the data while ridge's prediction extrapolates with unchanged confidence — and when you can point to the part of the band that is aleatoric and the part that is epistemic.

Where people go wrong

Reading a converged-looking trace plot as evidence of a correct posterior. R-hat near 1 and stable traces tell you the chains agree with each other, not that they have found the whole posterior — multimodal or funnel-shaped geometries can trap every chain in the same place. HMC's divergent transitions are the diagnostic that actually detects this, and they are routinely ignored because the model still produces numbers. Treat any divergences as a modelling problem (usually a non-centred reparameterisation is needed), not as a sampler setting to turn down.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: show that ridge regression is the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior, explain what the full Bayesian posterior gives you that the MAP does not, and distinguish epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty on a specific GP plot.

M13

Statistical learning theory, and what it fails to explain

≈6 h

M01 asserted that restricting the hypothesis class buys generalisation. This module is the proof, and it is worth doing because the shape of the argument — uniform convergence over a class, with a complexity measure controlling the deviation — is how every generalisation guarantee in the field is constructed. It is equally worth doing to learn the theory's limits honestly: applied to the models people actually deploy, these bounds are numerically vacuous, and knowing that prevents both over-reliance and dismissal.

Concepts

  • The PAC framework
    • Probably approximately correct: with probability at least 1 − δ, the learned hypothesis has risk within ε of the best in class
    • Sample complexity as the quantity the theory delivers; realisable versus agnostic settings
    • The finite-hypothesis-class bound, which requires only a union bound and Hoeffding — do this derivation, because everything else is a refinement of it
  • Infinite classes and VC dimension
    • Shattering, the growth function, and VC dimension as the largest shatterable set size
    • Sauer's lemma: below the VC dimension the growth function is exponential, above it polynomial — the phase transition that makes infinite classes learnable at all
    • The VC generalisation bound, and the fundamental theorem of statistical learning: finite VC dimension is equivalent to PAC learnability
    • Computing VC dimension for the standard cases: thresholds, intervals, linear separators in d dimensions (d + 1), axis-aligned rectangles
  • Sharper and more modern complexity measures
    • Rademacher complexity: measure the class's ability to fit random noise, which is data-dependent and therefore tighter than VC
    • Margin-based bounds, which explain why an SVM with a large margin generalises better than its VC dimension alone suggests
    • Covering numbers, algorithmic stability, PAC-Bayes and compression bounds as the other routes — and the observation that PAC-Bayes is currently the only one producing non-vacuous numbers for large models
  • Structural risk minimisation, and where the programme stops
    • SRM as the formal version of the M02 tradeoff: minimise empirical risk plus a complexity penalty over a nested sequence of classes
    • Why the bounds are vacuous in practice — plug in a modern model's parameter count and the bound exceeds 1, which is the statement "error is at most 100%"
    • The deeper problem: uniform convergence is a guarantee over all functions in the class, and modern models are chosen by an algorithm whose implicit bias makes most of that class unreachable. Bounds that ignore the optimiser are bounding the wrong thing
    • This is exactly the gap M19's double descent lands in, and it is a live research area rather than a settled failure

Work through

  • Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms — Chapters 2–6 and 26. Free from the authors, and the most readable rigorous treatment; Chapter 6 is the fundamental theorem.
  • Foundations of Machine Learning — Chapters 2–3 for PAC and Rademacher complexity, which are sharper than Shalev-Shwartz on the empirical-process side.
  • A Theory of the Learnable — Valiant's 1984 paper, which introduced PAC learning and won a Turing Award.
  • Learning From Data — lectures 5–7, which build VC dimension more slowly and intuitively than either textbook.
  • Journal of Machine Learning Research — scan a recent learning-theory issue to see what the field currently thinks the bounds should be measuring.

Practice

Compute VC dimension by hand for three classes: thresholds on the line, axis-aligned rectangles in the plane, and linear separators in d dimensions — proving both the shattering and the non-shattering direction for each. Then take a real dataset, fit a linear classifier, and evaluate the VC bound numerically against the observed test error. You are done when you can state how many orders of magnitude apart they are, and explain why that gap is expected rather than an error in your arithmetic.

Where people go wrong

Using a generalisation bound as a model-selection criterion. The bounds are worst-case over all distributions and all functions in the class, and they are loose by orders of magnitude — a bound of 0.8 and a bound of 0.9 tell you essentially nothing about which of two models will do better on your data. Cross-validation from M06 estimates the quantity you care about directly and is what you should select on. The theory's value is explanatory: it tells you what property makes learning possible, not which model to ship.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: derive the finite-class PAC bound from Hoeffding and a union bound, state the fundamental theorem of statistical learning, and explain in one paragraph why uniform-convergence bounds are vacuous for the models the field now deploys.

M14

Features, encodings and the data layer

≈5 h

In practice this is where most of the variance in project outcomes comes from, and it is the part textbooks compress into a paragraph. It sits after the models rather than before because almost every decision here is model-dependent — trees do not need scaling, kernels and neighbours do; regularised linear models need standardisation, boosted trees handle raw categoricals — and you cannot make those calls until you know what each family assumes.

Concepts

  • Scaling and transformation, and which models care
    • Standardisation, min–max, robust scaling; the rule of thumb that anything using a distance, a dot product or a penalty needs it, and trees do not
    • Skew and heavy tails: log, Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson (the last handling zeros and negatives), quantile transforms; and the reminder that transforming the target changes what your loss is estimating
  • Categorical encoding, which is where the leaks live
    • One-hot for low cardinality; the dummy-trap and why it matters for penalised models but not for trees
    • Ordinal encoding, valid only when order is real, and the specific damage it does to distance-based models when it is not
    • Target/mean encoding: powerful and directly leaky, since the encoding of a row uses that row's own label. Out-of-fold encoding, smoothing toward the global mean, and CatBoost's ordered statistics from M09 are the three correct fixes
    • Hashing for very high cardinality; count and frequency encoding; learned embeddings as the deep-learning answer
  • Missing data, treated as a mechanism rather than a nuisance
    • MCAR, MAR and MNAR — and the crucial point that the difference is a claim about the world that the data cannot settle
    • Mean and median imputation and the variance they destroy; kNN imputation; MICE/iterative imputation and its treatment of uncertainty
    • Missingness indicators, which are often more informative than the imputed value — "this test was not ordered" is a strong signal about the patient
    • Under MNAR, no imputation method recovers the truth; the honest response is a sensitivity analysis, not a better imputer
    • Native handling in XGBoost and LightGBM, where a default direction is learned per split — usually the best available option on tabular data
  • Imbalance, and what actually helps
    • Class weights and threshold tuning as the first-line answers, since the problem is usually the decision rule rather than the fit
    • Random over- and under-sampling; SMOTE and its variants, and the accumulating evidence that SMOTE's benefit largely disappears once probabilities are calibrated and thresholds are tuned
    • The framing that resolves most of it: imbalance is a decision-theoretic problem from M20, not a data problem
  • Feature selection, and when it is worth doing
    • Filter, wrapper and embedded methods; the lasso from M03 as the canonical embedded case
    • Why regularisation usually makes explicit selection unnecessary for prediction, and why you might still want it for cost, latency or interpretability
    • Selection must happen inside the cross-validation fold — the ESL example from M06 is exactly this mistake

Work through

  • Feature Engineering and Selection: A Practical Approach for Predictive Models — Chapters 5–8 and 11, free online, and the only book-length treatment of this material with real experiments in it.
  • Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras and TensorFlow — Chapter 2, which builds a preprocessing pipeline end to end and is the practical template.
  • CatBoost: Unbiased Boosting with Categorical Features — reread section 3 here for the formal statement of target-encoding leakage.
  • scikit-learn documentation — the preprocessing and imputation guides, plus ColumnTransformer, which is the mechanism for keeping per-column logic inside the fold.
  • Bayesian Data Analysis — Chapter 18, for missing data treated as a modelling problem rather than a preprocessing step.

Practice

Take a dataset with a high-cardinality categorical column. Encode it four ways — one-hot, naive target encoding on the full training set, out-of-fold target encoding, and CatBoost's ordered statistics — and cross-validate the same model on each. The naive version will look best in cross-validation and worst on a held-out test set. You are done when you can show that inversion in a table, because it is the clearest single demonstration of leakage that this page contains.

Where people go wrong

Imputing missing values with the column mean and moving on. It shrinks the variable's variance, distorts every correlation it participates in, and — most importantly — throws away the fact that the value was missing, which is frequently the most predictive thing about the row. Add an indicator column alongside any imputation, and before choosing a method, ask whether the missingness mechanism is plausibly MNAR: if it is, no imputation is correct and the honest deliverable is a sensitivity analysis.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe a concrete MNAR mechanism in a domain you know, explain why no imputation method can recover the truth under it, and say what you would report instead.

M15

Time series, and the cross-validation trap

≈5 h

Time-ordered data breaks the i.i.d. assumption from M01 in the most consequential way available: it makes the standard validation scheme from M06 silently invalid, because a random split lets the model see the future. The classical forecasting toolkit is also genuinely competitive here — the M-competitions have repeatedly found that simple statistical methods are hard to beat — which makes this the module where the reflex to reach for a flexible model is most often wrong.

Concepts

  • The structure of the problem
    • Stationarity, why most methods assume it, and differencing and seasonal differencing as the transformations that induce it
    • Autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation as the diagnostic pair; reading an ACF/PACF plot to identify AR and MA order
    • Trend, seasonality and remainder; STL decomposition
  • The classical models
    • ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA: what each term does, and Box–Jenkins identification versus automatic order selection by information criterion
    • Exponential smoothing and the ETS state-space family, which is often the stronger of the two on real business series
    • Prediction intervals, and the fact that they widen with horizon in a way point-forecast metrics never reveal
  • Validation that respects time — the module's core
    • Why random k-fold is invalid: it trains on data that occurs after the validation points, so the model exploits information no deployment would have
    • Rolling-origin evaluation (time-series cross-validation): expanding or sliding window, refit at each origin, average the errors across origins
    • The gap/embargo period, needed when features use rolling windows so that a lookback at the start of the validation set does not reach into training data
    • Purged and embargoed k-fold for overlapping-label problems, where a label at time t depends on a window extending past t
  • Machine learning on time series, done carefully
    • Reframing forecasting as tabular regression with lag and rolling features — which is what makes gradient boosting from M09 applicable, and how most competition-winning forecasts are actually built
    • Every rolling feature is a leak waiting to happen: a centred window, a group statistic computed over the whole series, or a target-derived feature without a shift will all reach forward
    • Multi-step forecasting: recursive versus direct strategies, and their different error-accumulation behaviour
  • Evaluation, and the baseline that matters
    • Scale-dependent (MAE, RMSE) versus percentage (MAPE, and its failure at values near zero and its asymmetry) versus scaled (MASE) errors
    • The seasonal naive forecast — predict the value from one season ago — as the mandatory baseline, and how often it wins
    • The M-competitions' recurring finding: combinations of simple methods are extremely hard to beat, and complexity pays only with a lot of related series
    • Hierarchical forecasting and reconciliation, where forecasts must sum consistently across a product or region hierarchy

Work through

  • Forecasting: Principles and Practice — Chapters 3, 5, 8 and 9. Free online, and the best forecasting text in print; Chapter 5.10 is the time-series cross-validation treatment.
  • statsmodels — for ARIMA, ETS and the diagnostic plots; its time-series documentation doubles as a short course.
  • scikit-learn documentation — TimeSeriesSplit, and the note explaining why the ordinary splitters are wrong here.
  • XGBoost documentation — for the tabular-reframing route; combine it with lag features and rolling-origin validation.

Practice

Take a seasonal series with a few years of history. Evaluate four models — seasonal naive, ETS, auto-ARIMA, and gradient boosting on lag features — under rolling-origin validation with a horizon that matches your real use. Then, deliberately, evaluate the boosting model under random k-fold as well. You are done when you can report both numbers for the same model and state the size of the illusion, and when at least one of the simple baselines is competitive with your best effort.

Where people go wrong

Shuffling before splitting. It is the default in every library and it converts a forecasting problem into an interpolation problem, which is much easier and completely unlike deployment. The resulting metric can be several times better than anything achievable, and nothing in the output signals a problem. The related and subtler version is a rolling feature computed over the full series before splitting — the training folds then contain a summary of future values, and the leak survives even a correct time-based split.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: explain why a seasonal naive baseline must be reported, design a rolling-origin scheme with an embargo for a feature set that includes 30-day rolling means, and say what the embargo length has to be and why.

M16

Prediction is not causation

≈6 h

Almost every model built in the previous fifteen modules is eventually asked a question it cannot answer: not "what will happen" but "what should we do", which is a question about intervention. The two are different in a way that is invisible in any validation metric — a model can be optimally predictive and give exactly the wrong answer about an action. This module is here rather than in a statistics course because the mistake is made constantly by competent people who validated their model correctly and then drew a conclusion the model does not support.

Concepts

  • The distinction, stated formally
    • Observational conditioning p(y | x) versus intervention p(y | do(x)); they coincide only under conditions you have to argue for
    • The fundamental problem of causal inference: for any unit you observe exactly one potential outcome, so a causal effect is never directly measured
    • Confounding, and why a variable that improves prediction can destroy an effect estimate — and vice versa
  • The structures that decide what to adjust for
    • Confounder, mediator and collider, and the fact that they are indistinguishable from the data alone — the DAG is an assumption you bring
    • Collider bias: conditioning on a common effect creates association between independent causes. This is why "control for everything" is wrong, and it is the single most useful thing in the module
    • Simpson's paradox as the arithmetic face of the same issue, with the resolution being that the correct stratification depends on the causal structure, not on the numbers
    • The back-door criterion as the formal rule for a sufficient adjustment set
    • Selection bias from how the sample was collected, which no amount of adjustment inside the sample can repair
  • Estimation, once identification is settled
    • Randomisation as the gold standard, and what it buys — it makes the adjustment set empty by construction
    • Propensity scores: matching, stratification, inverse probability weighting; positivity and overlap as the assumptions that fail quietly
    • Doubly robust estimators, consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is right
    • Quasi-experimental designs when randomisation is impossible: instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and the specific assumption each one trades for
  • Where machine learning re-enters
    • Heterogeneous treatment effects: causal forests and the broader meta-learner family (S-, T-, X-learners) estimating who benefits rather than whether anyone does
    • Uplift modelling in marketing and retention as the same idea in industrial dress, and why targeting predicted-to-churn users is usually the wrong policy
    • Double/debiased machine learning: use flexible models for the nuisance functions, with cross-fitting, and retain valid inference for the parameter you care about
    • The honest closing point: feature importance from M08 and SHAP from M17 are statements about the model, not about the world, and no amount of predictive accuracy upgrades them

Work through

  • Causal Inference: What If — Hernán and Robins, Chapters 1–7 and 12. Free from the authors and the clearest statement of the identification-then-estimation split.
  • Causal Inference in Statistics: A Primer — Pearl, Glymour and Jewell, Chapters 2–4, for the graphical route, colliders and the back-door criterion.
  • Estimation and Inference of Heterogeneous Treatment Effects Using Random Forests — Wager and Athey's causal forests, the bridge from M08 to this module.
  • EconML — Microsoft's library for heterogeneous treatment effects and double machine learning; its user guide is a good practical index of the methods.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapter 36, for a compact machine-learning-oriented summary of the framework.

Practice

Simulate a dataset from a known DAG with a confounder: treatment T, outcome Y, confounder C affecting both, and a noise variable. Fit the best predictive model you can for Y from all variables and read off T's importance. Then estimate the causal effect of T three ways — naive regression without C, adjusted regression with C, and inverse probability weighting — and compare all of them against the true simulated effect. Finally, add a collider that is caused by both T and Y, adjust for it, and watch the estimate get worse. You are done when the best predictor and the correct effect estimate visibly disagree.

Where people go wrong

Adding every available variable to the model on the theory that more control is safer. Adjusting for a collider induces spurious association, and adjusting for a mediator removes exactly the effect you were trying to measure. Both make the estimate worse, and both improve or leave unchanged the predictive fit — so no validation metric will warn you. The adjustment set has to be argued from a causal diagram, and the diagram comes from domain knowledge, not from the data.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: draw a three-variable DAG in which adding a variable to a regression makes the causal estimate worse, name the structure, and explain why cross-validation cannot detect the problem.

M17

Interpretability, and the failure modes of explanation

≈5 h

Post-hoc explanation is now a standard deliverable and is frequently the part of a project stakeholders trust most and understand least. The methods have precise definitions and precise limitations, and both get lost in the tooling. This module comes directly after M16 on purpose: the most common misreading of an explanation is a causal one, and having just done the causal module makes that error visible rather than tempting.

Concepts

  • The axes that organise the field — global versus local, model-specific versus model-agnostic, and intrinsically interpretable versus explained after the fact
  • Global methods and their assumptions
    • Partial dependence plots: average the model's prediction over the marginal distribution of the other features, which assumes independence and therefore extrapolates into regions with no data when features are correlated
    • Individual conditional expectation curves, which reveal the heterogeneity that averaging into a PDP conceals
    • Accumulated local effects as the repair: use conditional rather than marginal distributions, so no impossible feature combinations are evaluated
    • Permutation importance from M08, with the correlated-feature caveat repeated because it matters here too
  • Local methods
    • LIME: fit an interpretable surrogate on perturbed samples around one point. Simple and unstable — the explanation depends on the perturbation distribution, the kernel width and the random seed, and two runs can disagree
    • SHAP: Shapley values from cooperative game theory, uniquely satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy and additivity. TreeSHAP makes it exact and fast for tree ensembles, which is why it became the default
    • What the axioms do and do not promise: a fair attribution of the model's output among the features as the model uses them. With correlated features, adding a redundant copy of a variable splits its attribution in half — the model has not changed, the explanation has
    • The background-distribution choice, which silently determines what a SHAP value is relative to and is almost never reported
    • Counterfactual explanations — the smallest change that flips the decision — which are often the most actionable form for an affected individual
  • The alternative position, argued rather than mentioned
    • Rudin's case: for high-stakes decisions, use a model that is interpretable by construction rather than explaining an opaque one, because an explanation that is not faithful to the model is worse than no explanation
    • The accuracy-interpretability tradeoff is smaller than assumed on structured data: sparse scoring systems, optimal decision trees and generalised additive models are often within noise of a boosted ensemble
    • GA2M / explainable boosting machines as the concrete option — additive shape functions per feature plus selected pairwise interactions, giving a model that is both competitive and directly readable
    • Caruana's pneumonia example, where a model learned that asthma lowered mortality risk because asthmatic patients were treated more aggressively: a genuine pattern in the data, a lethal policy, and only visible because the model was intelligible

Work through

  • Interpretable Machine Learning — Molnar's book, free online. Chapters on PDP, ALE, LIME and Shapley values are the reference for this module.
  • A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions — the SHAP paper, for the axioms and the unification of earlier attribution methods.
  • "Why Should I Trust You?": Explaining the Predictions of Any Classifier — the LIME paper; read it alongside the SHAP paper to see what stability buys.
  • Stop Explaining Black Box Machine Learning Models for High Stakes Decisions and Use Interpretable Models Instead — Rudin's argument, which is the strongest counter-position and should be read in full.
  • Friends Don't Let Friends Deploy Black-Box Models — Caruana's talk, with the pneumonia and asthma case worked through.
  • SHAP — the library documentation; use TreeSHAP for the practice exercise since it is exact rather than sampled.
  • InterpretML — fit an explainable boosting machine on the same data and compare it against your explained black box.
  • Cynthia Rudin's talks and papers — her optimal sparse decision trees and scoring systems, which are the evidence for the accuracy claim in R-55.
  • Friends Don't Let Friends Deploy Black-Box Models (ICML invited talk) — the 2022 update, for the current state of the intelligible-models argument.

Practice

Fit a boosted model on data with two strongly correlated predictors. Compute SHAP values, then add an exact duplicate of one predictor as a third column, refit, and recompute. The attribution splits across the copies while the model's predictions are unchanged. Then produce a partial dependence plot for one of the correlated pair and the corresponding ALE plot, and show where the PDP is evaluating combinations that never occur. You are done when you have both demonstrations in one notebook.

Where people go wrong

Presenting a SHAP summary plot as an explanation of the phenomenon. It is an explanation of the model — a decomposition of this model's output over these features under this background distribution. If a feature is a proxy for something unmeasured, SHAP will attribute to the proxy; if two features are correlated, it will split credit between them in a way that depends on the tree structure. Say "the model relies on X" and never "X causes Y", and if a stakeholder needs the second, the honest answer is M16, not a bigger explanation.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state the four Shapley axioms, explain what adding a duplicated feature does to the attributions and why, and articulate Rudin's argument well enough to defend it against the accuracy objection.

M18

Distribution shift, monitoring and fairness

≈5 h

M06 taught validation on held-out data, which estimates performance on data from the same distribution. Deployment supplies data from a different distribution, always, and the gap grows with time. This module covers what changes, how to detect it, and the fairness constraints that turn out to be mathematically incompatible with each other — a result that forces an explicit choice rather than allowing a technical one.

Concepts

  • The taxonomy of shift, which determines what you can do about it
    • Covariate shift: p(x) changes, p(y | x) stays. Correctable in principle by importance weighting, provided the support still overlaps
    • Label/prior shift: p(y) changes, p(x | y) stays — common in epidemiology and fraud, and correctable by adjusting the prior
    • Concept drift: p(y | x) itself changes, which no reweighting fixes; the only answers are retraining and adaptation
    • Sudden, gradual, incremental and recurring drift as the temporal patterns, each suggesting a different monitoring window
  • Detection and monitoring in practice
    • Input monitoring: per-feature distribution tests, population stability index, and the multiple-comparisons problem that makes a hundred features generate constant false alarms
    • The delayed-label problem: performance is the metric you want and it arrives weeks or months late, which is why proxy signals on inputs and on prediction distributions carry the load
    • Domain-classifier detection — train a model to distinguish training from production data, and read its AUC as a drift measure
    • Feedback loops, where the model's own actions change the data it is next trained on: the predictive-policing dynamic, the recommender narrowing its own candidate pool, the credit model never observing the outcomes of applicants it rejected
    • Hidden technical debt: entanglement, undeclared consumers, correction cascades and configuration sprawl as the failure modes specific to machine-learning systems
  • Fairness, and the impossibility result
    • Demographic parity, equalised odds and equality of opportunity, and calibration within groups — each with a legitimate motivation
    • The impossibility theorem: except in degenerate cases (equal base rates, or a perfect classifier), calibration within groups and equalised false-positive and false-negative rates cannot hold simultaneously. This is arithmetic, not a limitation of current methods
    • The COMPAS dispute as the concrete instance — one side arguing calibration was satisfied, the other that error rates were unequal, and both being correct
    • Proxy variables: removing a protected attribute does not remove its influence, because it is reconstructible from postcode, occupation and purchase history
    • Pre-, in- and post-processing interventions, and the cost each imposes on accuracy
    • Why the choice is normative: the mathematics tells you the options are incompatible and cannot tell you which to pick

Work through

  • Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems — Sculley et al., which named most of the failure modes practitioners now take for granted.
  • Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning — Hardt, Price and Srebro, for the equalised-odds criterion and the post-processing construction.
  • Fairness and Machine Learning: Limitations and Opportunities — Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan, free online. Chapters 2–4 for the criteria and the impossibility result.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction — Chapter 19, for distribution shift in a unified notation.
  • scikit-learn documentation — the model-persistence and inspection sections, which cover the mechanics of the monitoring you will need to build.
  • ACM SIGKDD — the applied-data-science track, which is where deployed-system failures are actually written up.
  • NeurIPS — the Datasets and Benchmarks track, where evaluation methodology and contamination get scrutinised.

Practice

Train a model, then simulate three shifts on the test stream: covariate shift by resampling toward one region of feature space, label shift by changing the positive rate, and concept drift by altering the true decision boundary. Track accuracy, calibration and a domain-classifier drift score through each. Then implement importance weighting and check that it repairs the first and not the third. You are done when your drift detector's false-alarm rate on a no-shift stream is measured rather than assumed.

Where people go wrong

Treating fairness as a constraint to satisfy rather than a choice to make. Teams routinely pick a metric because a library implements it, satisfy it, and report the model as fair — when the incompatibility result means satisfying one criterion has forced a violation of another that someone else considers the important one. The defensible process is to state which criterion you chose, why it fits this decision's harms, and what you are accepting in exchange. There is no configuration that avoids the choice.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: name two fairness criteria that cannot both hold, state the condition under which they could, and explain why removing the protected attribute from the feature set does not make a model fair.

M19

The tabular frontier: double descent, TabPFN and AutoML

≈5 h

Two things happened to this field that the classical picture did not predict. Interpolating models generalise, which contradicts the U-curve M02 taught; and a transformer pretrained on synthetic data now competes with tuned gradient boosting on small tables without being fitted to them at all. This module is the honest account of both, including how much is genuinely settled — and it is the module most likely to need rewriting in two years.

Concepts

  • Double descent and benign overfitting
    • The shape: test error rises to a peak at the interpolation threshold, where the model has just enough capacity to fit the training data exactly, then falls again as capacity increases further
    • It appears in linear regression and random-feature models, not only neural networks, which is what makes it a statistics result rather than a deep-learning curiosity
    • The explanation that has held up: past the threshold there are many interpolating solutions, and the optimiser's implicit bias selects a low-norm one. The relevant capacity measure is not parameter count
    • Benign overfitting in linear regression — the conditions on the covariance spectrum under which interpolating noisy data still generalises
    • What this does to M13: uniform-convergence bounds were bounding the wrong object, since they quantify over the whole class rather than over what the algorithm can reach
  • Tabular foundation models
    • Prior-fitted networks: pretrain a transformer on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a structural prior, then perform Bayesian inference by in-context learning — the forward pass approximates the posterior predictive, with no gradient steps on your data
    • TabPFN's regime and its limits: small n, modest feature counts, and rapid degradation outside them; later versions extend the envelope substantially
    • Why this is conceptually interesting regardless of benchmark position: it moves model selection from a search you run to a prior someone else chose, which relocates rather than removes the inductive-bias problem
    • The evaluation caution: benchmark suites overlap heavily with the synthetic priors these models were trained against, so out-of-suite evidence matters much more than leaderboard position
  • AutoML, assessed for what it does
    • What it genuinely automates: hyperparameter search, model selection, ensembling and stacking — the parts that are search rather than judgement
    • What it does not: problem framing, the validation scheme, leakage detection, feature availability at prediction time, and the cost structure of the decision. Every one of those is a module of this page
    • The systems worth knowing (auto-sklearn, AutoGluon, TPOT) and the honest finding that a well-tuned gradient-boosted model is usually within noise of them on tabular tasks
  • What remains genuinely open — whether a scalable tabular architecture will eventually displace tree ensembles, whether the implicit-bias account of double descent generalises, and whether foundation-model priors can be made auditable enough for regulated use

Work through

  • Reconciling Modern Machine-Learning Practice and the Classical Bias–Variance Trade-Off — Belkin et al., the paper that named double descent and demonstrated it outside neural networks.
  • TabPFN: A Transformer That Solves Small Tabular Classification Problems in a Second — for the prior-fitted-network idea and an explicit statement of the regime it targets.
  • Why Do Tree-Based Models Still Outperform Deep Learning on Typical Tabular Data? — revisit it here as the benchmark against which any new claim should be checked.
  • Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics — for the interpolation and implicit-regularisation material in a textbook treatment rather than a paper.
  • OpenML — the benchmark suites, which are how you evaluate any of these claims yourself rather than accepting a table.
  • ICML: International Conference on Machine Learning — the theory and benchmark tracks, where the tabular-versus-deep question is argued out.

Practice

Reproduce double descent in the simplest possible setting: random-features regression on a fixed dataset, sweeping the number of random features from far below to far above the number of training points, with minimum-norm least squares at each width. Plot test error against the ratio of features to samples. You are done when you see the peak at ratio one and the second descent past it — and when you can show it disappears if you add ridge regularisation, which is the clue to the explanation.

Where people go wrong

Concluding from double descent that regularisation and validation no longer matter. The second descent is real and it is not free: optimally regularised models generally match or beat the interpolating regime, the peak at the threshold is genuinely bad, and where it sits depends on details you do not control. The correct update is narrow — parameter count is a poor capacity measure and the classical U-curve is not universal — and it does not license skipping M06.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: describe the double-descent curve and the interpolation threshold, state the implicit-bias explanation, and give a decision rule for when you would reach for TabPFN, tuned boosting, or a regularised linear model on a specific dataset.

M20

Doing it for real: baselines, error analysis and decisions

≈5 h

Every module so far taught a component. This one is about the sequence in which they are used, and it exists because the most common reason machine-learning projects fail is not modelling — it is that the wrong thing was predicted, no baseline was established, or a score was never converted into a decision. The material is not difficult and is skipped constantly, which is exactly why it is the last module rather than an appendix.

Concepts

  • Framing, which decides everything downstream
    • What is being predicted, for whom, and what action follows — and the test that catches most bad projects: if no decision changes based on the prediction, there is no project
    • Feature availability at prediction time, which is a stricter constraint than availability in the training table and the origin of most target leakage in production
    • Choosing the label: proxies, delay between prediction and outcome, and the censoring that creates
  • Baselines, in strict order
    • The trivial baseline (majority class, mean, seasonal naive), the current process's performance if there is one, a simple heuristic a domain expert would write, and a regularised linear model or a default random forest
    • The reason this matters: without them a respectable-looking score is uninterpretable, and a surprising number of deployed models do not beat the heuristic they replaced
  • Error analysis, which is where the improvement actually comes from
    • Look at the errors individually — a sample of fifty misclassified cases is worth more than another hyperparameter sweep
    • Slice-based evaluation: aggregate metrics hide subgroup failures, and the subgroup is usually the one that matters
    • Distinguishing label noise from model error, and the value of relabelling a sample to estimate the irreducible floor from M02
  • From a score to a decision
    • Expected-cost minimisation: with costs for false positives and false negatives, the optimal threshold is a ratio of costs, and it follows from calibration in M04 rather than from tuning
    • Why an uncalibrated model breaks this completely, closing the loop on that module
    • Capacity constraints — when you can only act on the top k, the ranking matters and the threshold does not
    • Abstention and deferral to a human as a first-class option, and the cost accounting that makes it rational
  • Reproducibility and the handover
    • Seeds, library versions, data snapshots and the environment; the specific pain of an unversioned upstream table
    • Documenting what the model is for and what it is not for — the failure mode being reuse of a model in a context where its training distribution does not apply
    • When not to use machine learning at all: no meaningful volume, no feedback signal, a rule that a domain expert can write, or a decision that must be contestable in a way a learned model cannot support

Work through

  • Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn, Keras and TensorFlow — Chapter 2, the end-to-end project, which is the best written template for this sequence.
  • Applied Predictive Modeling — Chapters 3–4 and 11, for the workflow and for performance measurement done with a practitioner's care.
  • Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems — reread here for the handover and maintenance costs, which are what the last section is about.
  • Computer Age Statistical Inference — Chapters 7–8 and 20, for the perspective of how this whole toolkit relates to the statistical tradition it grew out of.
  • Kaggle Competitions — read two winning solution write-ups end to end; they are the most detailed public accounts of error analysis and validation design that exist.

Practice

Run a complete project on a dataset you have not used before. In order: write the decision the model serves and the cost of each error type; build all four baselines; build one strong model; calibrate it; compute the cost-optimal threshold from your stated costs; slice performance by at least three subgroups; and read fifty errors by hand. You are done when you can state your model's improvement over the best baseline in units of the cost you defined at the start — not in AUC.

Where people go wrong

Optimising a threshold-free ranking metric when the deployment makes a hard, asymmetric decision. AUC treats all thresholds as equally relevant and both error types as equally costly, which is almost never the operating condition. A model with worse AUC can be substantially better at the threshold you will actually use. Decide the operating point and the cost ratio first, then select models on expected cost at that point — and if the costs are genuinely unknown, plot expected cost across a plausible range instead of retreating to AUC.

Checkpoint

You've got this when you can: state your model's decision rule as a threshold derived from a cost ratio, justify that threshold from the calibration curve, and name the baseline it beats along with the margin in units of cost.

Capstone

One integrative project, plus a shorter adversarial one. The first exercises most of the page; the second is the fastest way to find out whether M06 and M16 actually landed.

Project 1 — a tabular model that survives contact with reality. Choose a real dataset with grouped structure and a time dimension: hospital readmissions, customer churn with account history, equipment failures with maintenance logs. Something where the naive split is wrong.

  • Scope: state the decision and its cost structure before modelling; build a validation scheme that survives grouping and time; establish all four baselines; engineer features with every transform inside the fold; fit a regularised linear model, a random forest and a tuned boosting model; calibrate the best one and choose a threshold from your costs; produce SHAP and ALE explanations and write down what each does and does not license; simulate distribution shift and measure degradation; and check one subgroup fairness criterion, naming which you chose and why.
  • Draws on: M03, M04, M06, M08, M09, M14, M15, M17, M18, M20.
  • Done when: you can present a single table showing expected cost for every model including the baselines, and a written paragraph naming the leak you would have committed under the naive validation scheme along with how much it would have inflated the result. That number, measured on your own data, is the deliverable.
  • Stretch: add a causal question — which intervention should be taken, not just who is at risk — and estimate a treatment effect from the same data, stating the identification assumptions.

Project 2 — break four published-looking results. Construct four analyses that would pass casual review and are wrong, then fix each.

  • Scope: (a) a classifier with 0.99 AUC from preprocessing leakage; (b) a t-SNE figure showing clean clusters in pure noise; (c) a feature-importance ranking topped by a random high-cardinality identifier; (d) a model whose most important feature, adjusted for, gives the wrong sign on a simulated causal effect.
  • Draws on: M06, M08, M11, M16.
  • Done when: each has a before figure, an after figure and a one-paragraph explanation of the mechanism.
  • Stretch: find one of these four patterns in a real published paper or public notebook, and write up what a corrected analysis would need.

Mastery check

  • You can state the i.i.d. assumption and name precisely how a given dataset violates it.
  • You can derive the bias–variance decomposition and say why it does not carry over cleanly to 0–1 loss.
  • You can explain ridge through the SVD and predict which coefficients will be shrunk most.
  • You can explain why the lasso produces exact zeros and ridge does not, geometrically.
  • You can construct a model with excellent AUC and terrible calibration, and say which uses it breaks.
  • You can design a validation scheme that survives grouped, temporal and duplicate structure.
  • You can name the four kinds of leakage and give a concrete example of each.
  • You can derive the SVM dual and explain what the kernel trick costs at a million rows.
  • You can explain why more trees never overfit a random forest but do overfit a boosted ensemble.
  • You can say what ordered boosting fixes and why naive target encoding leaks.
  • You can prove EM does not decrease the likelihood, and explain why that says nothing about cluster quality.
  • You can list three things a t-SNE plot does not preserve and demonstrate one.
  • You can distinguish epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty on a Gaussian process plot.
  • You can derive the finite-class PAC bound and explain why such bounds are vacuous in practice.
  • You can draw a DAG where adjusting for an extra variable makes a causal estimate worse.
  • You can state the Shapley axioms and what a duplicated feature does to the attributions.
  • You can name two fairness criteria that cannot both hold and the condition under which they could.
  • You can describe double descent and say what it does and does not change about model selection.
  • You can convert a calibrated probability and a cost ratio into a decision threshold.

Where to go next

  • Deep Learning — the direct continuation. Everything here assumed features were given; that page is about learning them, and M13's theory gap and M19's double descent are both developed further there.
  • Classical Artificial Intelligence — the decision-making half that this page hands off. Probabilistic graphical models, MDPs and reinforcement learning live there, and M12's Bayesian machinery is what its inference algorithms run on.
  • Generative AI: Large Language Models — where the evaluation problem from M06 reappears in a much harder form, since the test set is now the entire internet and contamination cannot be ruled out by construction.
  • Information Theory — cross-entropy loss is a codelength, the ELBO in M12 is a compression bound, and minimum description length is a model-selection principle that arrives at M02's tradeoff from a different direction.
  • Causal inference — M16 is a single module on a discipline. Potential outcomes, structural causal models, do-calculus, sensitivity analysis and the design of experiments are the continuation, and they are what you need before any model is used to choose an action.
  • Bayesian statistics and probabilistic programming — M12 stops at the door. Hierarchical modelling, model criticism, prior predictive checks and the workflow around them are a full subject, and Stan and PyMC are the tools.
  • Convex and stochastic optimisation — M05 used the results without proving them. Duality theory, interior-point methods, stochastic approximation and online convex optimisation are the underlying discipline.
  • Machine learning systems and MLOps — M18 and M20 name the problems and stop. Feature stores, training/serving skew, shadow deployment, data versioning and monitoring infrastructure are the engineering discipline that follows.

Papers & primary sources

Classical machine learning's key papers are mostly readable in an afternoon, and several are more direct about their methods' weaknesses than the textbooks that summarise them. Every DOI and arXiv id below was confirmed through Crossref or the arXiv API rather than recalled — two remembered DOIs in the first pass turned out to be entirely different papers.

  1. R-29 Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures — Leo Breiman, Statistical Science, 2001

    The paper that named the split this page sits across, published with responses from Cox, Efron and Parzen and Breiman's rejoinder — read all of it, because the disagreement is the content. Twenty-five years on, its predictions about which culture would dominate applied work have aged remarkably well. (Project Euclid answers 403 to automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    No free lunch, stated by its author. (MIT Press 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Used in M01
  3. R-31 Ridge Regression: Biased Estimation for Nonorthogonal Problems — Arthur E. Hoerl & Robert W. Kennard, Technometrics, 1970

    Where deliberately accepting bias to reduce variance was first argued in print, three decades before the machine-learning community rediscovered the tradeoff.     Causal forests: M08's ensembles adapted to estimate who benefits from a treatment rather than who has a high outcome, with asymptotic normality and valid confidence intervals. The honesty condition — separate subsamples for splitting and for estimation — is the technical device that makes inference possible and is worth understanding. (Taylor & Francis 403s; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Paywalled Advanced ~15 pp
    Used in M16
  25. R-53 A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions — Scott M. Lundberg & Su-In Lee, 2017

    SHAP. The contribution is showing that several existing attribution methods are approximations to Shapley values, which are the unique attribution satisfying four stated axioms. Read the axioms carefully — they promise fairness of attribution within the model, which is a narrower claim than the way SHAP plots are usually presented.

    Core Free Intermediate ~10 pp
    Used in M17
  26. R-54 "Why Should I Trust You?": Explaining the Predictions of Any Classifier — Marco Tulio Ribeiro, Sameer Singh & Carlos Guestrin, 2016

    LIME, and the paper that made local model-agnostic explanation a standard expectation. Read it alongside R-53 to see what the Shapley axioms buy: LIME's explanation depends on the perturbation distribution, the kernel width and the seed, and the paper is reasonably candid about this.

    Free Intermediate ~10 pp
    Used in M17
  27. R-55 Stop Explaining Black Box Machine Learning Models for High Stakes Decisions and Use Interpretable Models Instead — Cynthia Rudin, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019

    The strongest argument against the entire post-hoc explanation programme, and the one to read in full rather than in summary. Its central claim is empirical as well as ethical: on structured data the accuracy cost of an interpretable model is usually small, so the tradeoff people invoke to justify black boxes frequently does not exist. An arXiv preprint (1811.10154) is available if the journal is paywalled for you. (Nature 403s automated checks; Crossref confirms the record.)

    Core Paywalled Intermediate ~10 pp
    Used in M17
  28. R-56 Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems — D. Sculley et al., NeurIPS, 2015

    Open access from the NeurIPS proceedings. Named most of the failure modes practitioners now take for granted — entanglement (changing anything changes everything), undeclared consumers, correction cascades, configuration debt, feedback loops. Nine pages, no equations, and the highest ratio of usefulness to length on this page.

    Core Free Beginner ~9 pp
    Used in M18 M20
  29. R-57 Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning — Moritz Hardt, Eric Price & Nathan Srebro, 2016

    The equalised-odds and equal-opportunity criteria, with a post-processing construction that achieves them from any existing score. Read section 5 on the incompatibility with calibration; it is the formal core of the argument M18 makes and of the COMPAS dispute.

    Core Free Advanced ~22 pp
    Used in M18
  30. R-58 Reconciling Modern Machine-Learning Practice and the Classical Bias–Variance Trade-Off — Mikhail Belkin, Daniel Hsu, Siyuan Ma & Soumik Mandal, PNAS, 2019

    The paper that named double descent and — crucially — demonstrated it in random-feature models and decision-tree ensembles as well as neural networks, which is what makes it a statistics result rather than a deep-learning artefact. Open access from PNAS. Reproduce Figure 2; it takes twenty lines.

    Core Free Advanced ~6 pp
    Used in M19
  31. R-59 TabPFN: A Transformer That Solves Small Tabular Classification Problems in a Second — Noah Hollmann, Samuel Müller, Katharina Eggensperger & Frank Hutter, 2022

    Prior-fitted networks applied to tables: pretrain on millions of synthetic datasets from a structural prior, then approximate the posterior predictive in a single forward pass with no fitting on your data. Read it for the idea and for its explicit statement of the regime it targets — small n, modest feature counts — because that boundary is where most of the argument about it lives.

    Free Advanced ~30 pp
    Used in M19

Talks & video

The two positions this page presents as a live argument — post-hoc explanation versus intrinsic interpretability — are both best encountered as talks, because their authors argue them more sharply in person than in print. The rest are the authors of the methods explaining their own limitations.

  1. R-60 Friends Don't Let Friends Deploy Black-Box Models — Rich Caruana, BIRS workshop, 2018

    The pneumonia study worked through in full: a model that learned asthmatic patients had lower mortality risk, because they were triaged more aggressively — a real pattern in the data that would have killed people as a policy, and one that was only caught because the model was intelligible. The generalised additive models Caruana proposes are the concrete alternative M17 points at.

Articles, essays & blogs

Short pieces that do something a textbook cannot — usually by being interactive, or by being willing to say a popular method is being misused.

  1. R-66 How to Use t-SNE Effectively — Martin Wattenberg, Fernanda Viégas & Ian Johnson, Distill, 2016

    Interactive, and it demonstrates every t-SNE failure mode in about ten minutes: cluster sizes meaning nothing, distances between clusters meaning nothing, apparent structure appearing in pure noise, and the same data looking entirely different at different perplexities.   2. R-67 Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference, and Social Science — Andrew Gelman and collaborators, ongoing

Tools, datasets & playgrounds

Implement the method yourself first, then use these. The gap between your version and the library's is where the engineering lessons are, and several of these libraries encode a lesson from this page directly in their API.

  1. R-75 Optuna — Preferred Networks and contributors, ongoing

    Hyperparameter search with pruning, a define-by-run search space and good visualisation of the search itself. Its value for M06 is that the trial budget is explicit, which makes the "search budget is a form of capacity" argument concrete — plot the best-so-far curve and you can see the validation set being overfitted.

    Free Intermediate
    Used in M06
  2. R-76 SHAP — Scott Lundberg and contributors, ongoing

    The reference implementation of R-53. Use TreeSHAP for the M17 exercise because it is exact for tree ensembles rather than sampled, which removes one source of confusion. Pay attention to the background-dataset argument — it silently determines what the values are relative to and is almost never reported in write-ups.

    Free Intermediate
    Used in M17
  3. R-77 PyMC — PyMC developers, ongoing

    Probabilistic programming in Python with NUTS and variational inference, and the easiest route into M12's exercises. The example gallery covers hierarchical models and Gaussian processes with runnable notebooks; pair it with ArviZ for the diagnostics, which is where the real learning is.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M12
  4. R-78 CatBoost — Yandex and contributors, ongoing

    The third gradient-boosting implementation, and the one to reach for when categorical features dominate — its ordered target statistics remove the leak M14 demonstrates without you having to build out-of-fold encoding by hand. Slower to train than LightGBM and often better out of the box on categorical-heavy tables.

    Free Intermediate
    Used in M09
  5. R-79 EconML — Microsoft Research / PyWhy, ongoing

    Heterogeneous treatment effects and double machine learning in one library — causal forests, meta-learners, doubly robust estimators and instrumental-variable methods. Its user guide is a good practical index of the M16 methods, and pairing it with DoWhy makes the identification step explicit rather than implicit.

    Free Advanced
    Used in M16
  6. R-80 OpenML — OpenML community, ongoing

    Thousands of datasets with standardised splits, plus curated benchmark suites and the recorded results of everyone else's runs on them. The right way to evaluate a claim from M19 yourself: pick the suite the paper used, then pick one it did not, and compare. Integrates directly with scikit-learn.

    Core Free Intermediate
    Used in M19
  7. R-81 UCI Machine Learning Repository — University of California, Irvine, ongoing

    The oldest dataset collection in the field and the source of most benchmark datasets in the older literature. Useful for reproducing published results, and useful as a caution: several of its most-used datasets have known quality problems and documented leaks, which is itself a lesson about benchmark provenance.

    Free Beginner
    Used in M11
  8. R-82 InterpretML — Microsoft Research, ongoing

    The explainable boosting machine — the GA2M implementation behind Caruana's argument in R-60 — alongside the post-hoc methods, so you can compare an intrinsically interpretable model against an explained black box on the same data.     Free Intermediate
    Used in M17
